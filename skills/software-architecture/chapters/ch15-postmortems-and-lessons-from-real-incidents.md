# Chapter 15: Post-Mortems and Lessons from Real Incidents

## Core Idea
Large outages rarely come from one broken component; they come from a small change that meets a hidden dependency, then from a recovery path that depends on the thing that broke. This chapter extracts the causal mechanism and the transferable lesson from published incidents at large providers, without the narrative. Read each as a pattern to test your own design against: "if this happened to us, what would we see, and could we still fix it?" Details are as published by the companies; internal causes beyond their reports are unverified and are marked as such.

## 15.1 Ordered rule lists: a merge that moved a catch-all above the specific rules
- **Mechanism**: a CDN provider was standardising routing-policy metadata for a subset of its prefixes. In the policy, terms are evaluated in order: advertise specific prefixes, then reject everything else. A reordering produced by the change moved a "reject the rest" term above the terms that advertised some internal prefixes, so those prefixes stopped being announced. About half of the traffic in 19 busy data centers was affected for roughly two hours; edge servers could still answer clients (clients saw server-error pages, so TCP, TLS and HTTP to the edge worked) but could no longer reach origin servers or resolve names beyond the edge.
- **Lesson**: first-match rule lists (firewalls, routing policy, access rules, feature flags) are code where order is semantics; a diff that "only adds informational terms" can still reorder. Review the effective result (what is announced after the change), not the textual diff; roll out one location at a time so the first failure is also the last. Distinguish "the edge answered with an error" from "the edge is down" when diagnosing: they point at different hops.
- **Does not hold when**: the policy engine is declarative with no ordering.

## 15.2 Self-withdrawal: health checks that remove you from the map
- **Mechanism**: a social-network company's maintenance command took down the connections between its data centers. A tool meant to audit and block risky commands had a bug and let it pass (a safety net that was never exercised). The authoritative DNS servers sit in small edge facilities that also announce their own addresses to the internet; by design they withdraw those announcements when they cannot reach the data centers (a health check meaning "do not send me traffic"). With the backbone gone, all of them withdrew at once, so the DNS servers' own addresses vanished from the internet and names stopped resolving everywhere, even though the machines were running.
- **Lesson**: automatic withdrawal is correct for one unhealthy site and catastrophic when the same condition hits all sites at once; give such health checks a floor (never withdraw the last N sites) or a correlated-failure guard. Recovery then failed in layers: out-of-band network access was down too, internal tools depended on the same DNS, physical access to hardened sites was slow, and restarting everything at once risked a surge (power draw dropped by tens of megawatts and a sudden return was itself a hazard). Practised "storm" exercises on a data-center failure made the staged restart possible.
- **Does not hold when**: the control path is physically and logically separate (out-of-band network, independent DNS for internal tools, a break-glass procedure rehearsed).

## 15.3 Cross-boundary packet loss, healthy servers marked unhealthy, then an autoscaling stampede
- **Mechanism**: a messaging platform saw packet loss on the cloud provider's network between boundaries (the provider later raised the capacity of that cross-boundary system). Synchronous requests waited on slow network round trips: a small share of high-latency requests used about 40% of server time. Service discovery marked many backends unhealthy, capacity fell below demand, and automation tried to add about 1,200 servers at once. The provisioning service was never load-tested for that rate: it hit its open-file limit, the cloud provider's API rate limit and a 30-minute operation timeout, producing partially provisioned servers that could not take traffic. The observability stack reached its database over the same unstable network, so engineers lost visibility; paging fired on a downstream symptom (errors) rather than on the root condition (packet loss).
- **Lesson**: (1) health checks measured over a degraded network amplify the outage by shrinking the fleet, so cap the fraction of nodes that can be ejected; (2) autoscaling is a client of other systems and needs rate limits, concurrency caps and a rule that half-provisioned nodes never join the pool; (3) alert on the cause where possible (loss, saturation of shared links); (4) keep observability on an independent path and keep a runbook for querying metrics directly; (5) keep chatty services and their databases in the same network segment and set timeouts so slow requests are killed early.
- **Does not hold when**: capacity truly is lost (then fast provisioning matters, with the above guards).

## 15.4 Internal network congestion and retry amplification
- **Mechanism**: a cloud provider runs a main network and an internal network for foundational services (monitoring, internal DNS, authorization, parts of the compute control plane), joined by routing and address-translation devices. An automated capacity change in a main-network service caused a large number of clients in the internal network to open connections; the connecting devices saturated, packets were dropped, clients retried, and retries kept the devices saturated for hours. Customer workloads already running mostly continued; operations that needed the internal network failed (creating instances, control-plane APIs). The operators' real-time monitoring lived behind the congested link, so diagnosis was slow; DNS was repaired first because everything depends on it. After traffic was moved to another path, callers kept cached "this dependency is down" state and recovered late.
- **Lesson**: the system effectively attacked itself; clients must back off with jitter, circuit-break and cap retries (ch03); the link between a "foundation" network and the data plane is a shared bottleneck needing its own capacity alarms; negative health caches need expiry or probing; separate the monitoring path from the thing monitored.
- **Does not hold when**: dependencies are few and local; the principle still applies to any shared choke point.

## 15.5 Connection-pool and port exhaustion that looks like a slow server
- **Mechanism**: a web tier creating a new backend connection per request (and keeping it alive) used up the ephemeral port range after a few tens of thousands of requests; requests hung, the proxy timed out, the client timed out, and the symptom pointed at the server (details in ch13 13.6).
- **Lesson**: when latency rises without CPU or memory pressure, count connections and file descriptors; a missing pool or an unreleased connection is a common cause.

## 15.6 Platform regressions invisible to application code
- **Mechanism**: an OS default change made kernel code preemptible, stretching short locks in a database that forks a process per connection (ch12 12.7); throughput halved on the new kernel with the same database version.
- **Lesson**: platform upgrades are deployments; benchmark before and after, and know the knob (huge pages, preemption mode) that the fix lives in.

## 15.7 Cluster-store operational debt: compaction, garbage collection and hot partitions
- **Mechanism**: a chat platform storing trillions of messages in a log-structured store (partition key = channel plus a time bucket) found reads dearer than writes: a hot channel's recent messages sit in the memtable and several sorted files, so many concurrent readers cause cascading latency, compaction falls behind, operators rotate nodes out so they can compact and bring them back, and managed-runtime garbage-collection pauses cause latency spikes and sometimes long stalls needing manual reboots. Remedies layered: (a) a thin data-service layer with one query per endpoint and no business logic, written in a language with no garbage collector, coalescing identical concurrent reads into one database query (the first request spawns a worker, later ones subscribe to it); (b) consistent-hash routing from the API tier to the data service by channel so identical requests meet in the same instance; (c) a different engine without a managed-runtime collector and with core-per-shard design; (d) a migration that dual-writes new data and bulk-moves old data through local files shipped in one transfer instead of millions of network writes (estimated three months became nine days); the long tail was partitions full of tombstones (deletes are writes). Result reported: 177 nodes to 72 larger nodes, read tail latency from roughly 40-125 ms to about 15 ms, steady insert latency.
- **Lesson**: protect the database from the shape of the load (coalescing, routing) before blaming the engine; plan for tombstones when mass deletes are possible; migrate with dual writes plus backfill and measure the real bottleneck (network round trips) rather than accept the estimate.
- **Does not hold when**: access is evenly spread and reads are cheap; then a larger cluster of the same engine may be enough.

## 15.8 Cross-incident checklist
1. What is the smallest change that could remove the thing that tells us the system is healthy?
2. Does the recovery path (tooling, DNS, VPN, door badge, monitoring) depend on the failed system?
3. Which automatic action (withdraw, eject, scale, retry, restart) is correct once and catastrophic when correlated? Put a cap on it.
4. Is there a shared choke point (cross-network link, DNS, config store, identity) whose capacity is not alarmed?
5. After the fix, can everything start together without a surge? Stage it.
6. Did the alert fire on the cause or only on a downstream symptom?
7. Is order significant anywhere in configuration, and does review see the effective result?
