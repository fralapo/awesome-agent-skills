# Chapter 08: Logo Ideation, Critique, Tests and Presentation

## Core Idea
Ch06 covers construction and delivery. This chapter adds the parts around it: how to explore symbols before drawing, how to critique a mark in a repeatable order, how to prepare it for real reproduction and how to show it. A logo is a system judged on flexibility across surfaces, sizes and years, not a static image judged at first glance; a clever idea does not rescue weak form. Strategy, positioning and the decision to rebrand belong to the art director (`art-director` ch05 and ch07).

## Procedure 1: Ideation Before Drawing
1. **List metaphors without judging**: write or draw as many symbols for the concept as you can (a long list, not a short one). The first idea is usually a cliché; explore the whole metaphor space before choosing.
2. **Decompose**: break the concept into sub-elements and sort them from small to big (protection, for example, breaks into defence, then its tools, then their parts). Each node is a possible symbol.
3. **Combine**: pair two symbols into a new one. A mark that reads as two things (for example a cup and a hat) must read clearly as both: the dual-image check.
4. **Icon, not illustration**: choose a pattern or shape distinctive enough that non-experts recognise it. Overly literal symbols (a weapon for protection) are hard to read as a brand.
5. **One idea per mark**: delete extra details (maps, tiny elements) that add no meaning. Finish one idea before starting the next; parallel half-explored ideas make a mess.
6. **Group the results by theme** and present the set in groups, not as a flat list.
7. **Sketch on paper first**, then build; do not polish a direction that may be discarded.

## Procedure 2: Fixed Name and Awkward Names
- When the client fixes the name you cannot redesign the wordmark; work with stacking, scale, case and emphasis.
- Long names: stack the words and enlarge the key word; balance symbol and wordmark by **visual weight**, not forced alignment.
- Read the name aloud and check client spellings before drawing.
- A descriptor ("studio", "agency") is not a slogan; keep it small and secondary.
- Choose a heavier cut of the family instead of adding a stroke to a light face; prefer a family designed as a set (with condensed and extended widths) to mixing unrelated faces.
- Do not force words together to look clever, or add arrows and flourishes that hide letters.

## Procedure 3: Critique Sequence
1. **Legibility**: can it be read instantly? Legibility outranks cleverness for a new or functional mark.
2. **Gut reaction**, then justify it with specific observations, without prescribing yet.
3. **Elimination**: remove the weakest option, repeat, rather than choosing a favourite.
4. **Silhouette**: fill the mark solid to judge shape, rhythm and balance without detail.
5. **Distance**: step away and come back; staring destroys objectivity.
6. **Validation**: mock up in use, as a pattern, as an animation.
Colours of similar lightness reduce legibility: separate them by **value**, not only hue. A rule of thumb (a heuristic, not a law): odd-numbered groups of elements feel more rhythmic than even ones.

## Procedure 4: Reproduction Checks
| Check | What to do |
|---|---|
| Compact lockup | Build a square or box version for tight spaces |
| Business-card test | Scale to card size with full contact details; the name must stay readable |
| Print at actual size | Never design only on screen; compare options side by side at the same scale |
| Thin gaps | Thin white gaps close up in print: widen them; make positive and negative widths clearly different |
| Small detail | Separate elements with a knockout or white outline rather than an inversion |
| Tracking | Tighten letter spacing for compact marks; wide tracking makes the mark too big to scale |
| Flat reproduction | No shadow or outline on a mark that must print flat |
| Fine detail | Mesh gradients, tiny shadows, pencil-like nibs, fussy counters and pun-details turn to mush small |
| Colour neighbours | Hues adjacent on the wheel (red and orange) merge in poor reproduction; warm colours advance, so put the key element in the warm one |
| Dead space | Anchor shapes (a circle) give balance but must not leave dead negative space; fill or rescale |
| Corners | Thick transitions at corners look heavy on a smooth curve |

## Procedure 5: Six Tests Before Showing
1. **Aesthetic**: good at a glance (simpler passes more easily).
2. **Application**: uniforms, vehicles, packaging, wrapping, merchandise, patterns.
3. **Scale**: recognisable small and large; if not, build responsive variants with simplified versions.
4. **Fatigue**: still pleasant to see every day?
5. **Taste**: people with strong taste give consistent feedback with reasons.
6. **Moodboard**: place it beside brands that have lasted 30 to 50 years; does it belong?

## Proportion Notes
Set icon and wordmark heights from one shared circle system (a golden-ratio relation is one option); build from ovals and mirrored halves, then merge into one silhouette; derive rectangles from diagonals and subdivide into quarters so modular parts line up. Align type to the angles of the illustration and balance negative space on both sides; a heavy base makes a lockup feel stable.

## Where Opinions Differ
- **Distinctive silhouette vs instant legibility.** People read word shapes, and symbols gain meaning through repeated exposure, so a mark seen daily (product, transit, packaging) may favour a recognisable shape over letter-by-letter clarity. A new brand, small sizes and wayfinding favour instant legibility. Decide by exposure frequency and context.
- **Trend vs fad.** A trend is a lasting shift; a fad is a short-lived shape or style. Ask why you follow either. Referencing older design is a reference, not a trend; do not dismiss ideas by label.

## Restraint, Classic Choices and Lockup Hierarchy
- **Judge the mark alone first, then in applications** (signage, products, packaging). A simple mark is strong when the surrounding photography and art direction carry the emotion; keep the logo and the imagery as separate decisions.
- **A clean typeface with one distinctive trait can carry a logo.** Restraint is not weakness, and "solid" is a valid goal: a logo does not have to be exciting, it has to last and flex. Judge lasting strength and versatility, not novelty or how "interesting" it looks.
- Trend-driven typefaces and decoration date quickly. "It is played out" is not a reason to replace a sound classic face; the reason must come from the brand. When a client pushes back on a classic choice, explain the lasting-value judgment (fatigue, 30-to-50-year test above).
- In a lockup the **name dominates**; a tagline or descriptor sits lower in the hierarchy and can be lighter. The typeface must fit the brand's culture: a delicate or foreign face clashes with a heavy mark.
- Check the mark does not resemble a well-known existing logo.
- Realistic mock-ups with the real finish (metallic, bevel, reflection) raise conviction, but only if the flat mark already works.

## Presenting a Logo
- Show it in context (card, signage, product, screen), not on an empty page; flat marks look different on a wall or a bottle.
- Show the process and the reasoning; presenting a mark cold, without process, invites silence and endless revision.
- Offer one or two deeply developed routes rather than many shallow ones (see `art-director` ch05).
- Explain which tests it passed and which variants exist (full, compact, one colour, reversed).
- Strong reactions to a mark mostly reflect taste; the brief and the business goal decide.

## Common Mistakes
- Designing only on screen; never printing the mark.
- Starting from one symbol (a lock for security) and polishing it.
- A visual pun that is clever but unreadable small.
- Judging by first impression a mark that must live for years.
- Adding political or colour symbolism the mark does not carry.
- Buying or selling a logo as a graphic only, without competitive and positioning research behind it.

## Checklist
- [ ] Metaphor list written, decomposed and combined before sketching
- [ ] One idea per mark, one idea finished at a time
- [ ] Silhouette, one-colour and reversed versions pass
- [ ] Compact lockup at business-card size with contact details readable
- [ ] Printed at real size; thin gaps widened
- [ ] Six tests run; responsive variants made where scale fails
- [ ] Shown in context with process and variants
