# Chapter 12: Packaging, Brochure and Print Production Craft

## Core Idea
Print and packaging add physical constraints to layout: inks, folds, bleed, shelf distance, six sides, mandatory text. Good work turns constraints into hierarchy: rank the information, give it a place on the object, and prove it with a physical mock-up. Decisions about how much of the existing brand to keep belong to the art director (`art-director` ch07); this chapter is the execution craft.

## Print Economy
- **Two-colour design** (black plus one spot) can feel rich; **overprint** two inks to create a third tone; **duotone** photographs raise perceived value at low cost. Do not print a full-colour tone where a duotone carries the same impact.
- Bleed to the edge looks modern but costs more in print; say so when proposing it.
- Thin light colour on white needs thicker strokes to survive print; semi-transparent overlays look dirty on textured grounds, use opaque fills.
- Ask for the deadline reality early; give the designer the right to say no before committing.

## Finishes as a System
- Treat finishes as part of the hierarchy: a **spot varnish** adds partial gloss over a matte surface, so one element catches the light without a new colour.
- Surface sets the tier: glossy plastic or glossy stock reads cheaper than matte or refined finishes with a metal accent; compare the pack beside its price-tier peers (direction-level tier logic in `art-director` ch09).
- Keep finishes consistent across touchpoints (envelope, card, menu, pack); every touchpoint needs the same care as the main mark.
- Do not mix decorative paper textures or twine into a utilitarian identity; the materials should agree with the tone. An "undesigned" aesthetic looks unfinished unless the designer controls it closely.
- Keep leading tight in text blocks (loose leading turns gaps into visible negative space) and limit point sizes to a small, consistent scale.

## Packaging: Procedure
1. **Collect constraints first**: mandatory copy (net weight, legal lines), fixed logo and colours, shape that cannot change (changing a bottle or pack shape implies retooling cost), finishes and materials.
2. **Rank the information**: for a typical product, product form or what it is, then the product name, then the brand; everything else is subordinate. When the original is text-heavy, cut copy to the most important element. Remove duplicated information (the same address or nutrition panel twice).
3. **Sketch on paper first** so weak ideas do not consume computer time. The first idea is rarely the best; make more variations. Narrow to one or two directions and refine in software.
4. **Use a loose grid** for the main elements when space is tight, instead of a rigid master grid. With mandatory copy the design problem is hierarchy and legibility, not content.
5. **Design the object, not the front**: a pack has six sides; the front panel carries most of the message, and a line or border should continue around the edge from front to side.
6. **Mock up physically**: foldable dieline, print, cut, apply to the real shape, photograph and review at real scale. A digital mock-up is not a substitute when the physical one is the proof.
7. **Review at shelf distance** (about six feet, or arm's length for smaller items), among competitors.

## Packaging: Rules
- Show the product's form visually when it matters (a misting effect for a spray), and keep its signature visual cue visible.
- Make mandatory information prominent, not hidden; put required legal information on the front panel when rules demand it (check before layout).
- Use rules that span a section to group dense text; the space between groups exceeds the space inside. Thicken borders until they read as shapes rather than rules.
- Unify elements; avoid separate "islands". Ornaments must relate to the core mark, otherwise they feel disconnected.
- Pick one motif and develop it. Use one recurring motif to unify several logos on a pack; three competing logos on one pack is a defect. Keep a parent-company mark secondary to the product name.
- Use one strong central axis to organise elements when the category calls for formality.
- **Perceived price should match actual price**: premium and pharmaceutical categories call for restraint and simplification; decorative patterns feel cheap on a premium product; dark backgrounds with serif type read premium, busy colour reads cheaper; centred, old-fashioned typography dates a contemporary product.
- Warm reds and yellows are said to stimulate appetite in food; test the claim against the category before relying on it.
- Research the category and culture; respect non-native scripts and languages on the pack.
- Design from the buyer's point of view first, then position where the buyer's view meets the brand's attributes.

## Brochure and Booklet Craft
- Think of the booklet as a three-dimensional object: repeat a line, colour or direction across front, back and inside so they feel related.
- Restraint signals premium: two complementary serifs plus one sans is enough; use a light, ultra-condensed weight for food products rather than a heavy one; a limited palette.
- Contrast of scale: one element clearly big, another clearly small.
- White space is the main luxury cue; when a page feels like a catalogue, remove or shrink images before adding anything.
- Use gold or metallic accents sparingly and repeat them to link diagonals and elements.
- Separate low-contrast elements (blue on blue) with a light backing, a white product or an edge highlight.
- Bleed an image across the gutter substantially and on purpose, not by a small overlap.
- A proportional thirds division gives a dynamic page structure.
- Too many logos and images on one page reads as clutter; a logo placed at a corner without relation to the rest reads as an afterthought.
- Ask for whole spreads or the whole issue when reviewing, so rhythm can be judged (see ch07).

## Common Mistakes
- Presenting only a digital mock-up.
- Tiny copy at the edges; mandatory information hidden.
- Dropping a legacy brand element from the front without a brief to do so.
- Changing shape or colours when the brief forbids it.
- Decorative lines that connect to nothing.
- Printing a full-colour tone where two colours carry the same impact.

## Checklist
- [ ] Constraints and mandatory copy listed before layout
- [ ] Information ranked; duplicates removed
- [ ] All sides and the fold logic designed
- [ ] Physical mock-up at real scale
- [ ] Read at shelf or arm's-length distance
- [ ] Perceived price matches the category and tier
- [ ] Production cost of bleed, finishes and inks stated
