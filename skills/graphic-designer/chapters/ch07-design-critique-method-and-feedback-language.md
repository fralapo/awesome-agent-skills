# Chapter 07: Design Critique: Method and Feedback Language

## Core Idea
Craft critique is a structured reading of the work, not a vote on taste. It separates three things that beginners blend: what is on the page (observation), what it makes the viewer feel or do (interpretation) and what to change (prescription). Quality is judged against shared, concrete criteria and the stated objective; strong personal reactions are data to explain, not verdicts. Reviewing work against a visual direction, and routing notes to other roles, is covered in `art-director` ch04; this chapter is the craft layer: what to check, in which order, and how to phrase it.

## Procedure: Reading a Piece
1. **State the objective** in one sentence. Judge the piece by its job first (stop a passer-by, explain a product, sell a book from a shelf), taste second.
2. **Three-question review**, always in this order: does it hit the objective? Does it communicate in the intended read order? Is it technically sound (alignment, legibility, production)?
3. **Write the read order before editing**: what is read first, second, third. Then make scale, weight, colour and position enforce that order. Hierarchy work starts from this list, not from nudging objects.
4. **Review one dimension per pass**: one pass for spacing, one for colour, one for alignment, one for type size. Attention spread over everything at once misses most defects.
5. **Look at it as it will live**: print at actual size, view at arm's length or shelf distance, view whole spreads or the whole set, not isolated pages. Rhythm, repetition and series logic can only be judged on the whole. Screens mislead for text-heavy and print work.
6. **Zoom in, then out**: at close range check alignments and equal measures (one-pixel differences are invisible at normal view); at distance squint or blur and check the silhouette and the grey value of text blocks.
7. **Step away**: after long staring objectivity fades. Return after a break, as a colourist rests the eyes.

## Procedure: Giving the Note
1. **Gut reaction first** (works, does not work), then **justify with specific observations** (what exactly in the layout causes it), without prescribing a fix yet.
2. **Explain why**: a note without a reason cannot be generalised and gets repeated next round.
3. **Define the adjective**. "Cleaner", "more elegant", "more organic" mean different things to different people. Use a **bad-to-good slider**: pick one parameter (for example contrast), define what low and high look like, place the work on that scale. Judging bad against great is easier than good against great. Build a short list of such parameters per project (a gauntlet of about five checks the work must pass).
4. **Pick the form of the fix by situation.**
   - Reviewing a professional at direction stage: give **criteria** ("calmer, more space, one accent"), let them solve it (as in `art-director` ch04).
   - Coaching a learner, or a note that has come back twice: **sketch the fix on paper** next to the original and compare the two versions side by side.
5. **Elimination beats favourites** when choosing among options: remove the weakest, repeat. A noisy room is not a vote; weigh reasons, not hands.
6. **Name what works** too, so it survives the next revision.

## Phrasing Examples
| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| "Feels cluttered" | "Four sizes and three weights compete; the gap between groups equals the gap inside them" |
| "Make it pop" | "The subject and its background share a value; darken the background or lift the subject locally" |
| "More modern" | "Centred text and a double rule read as dated; try flush left with one scale jump" |
| "Doesn't flow" | "The eye enters at the photo, lands on a caption, never reaches the title" |
| "Elegant" | "Define: two weights, wide margins, one accent, no ornament" |

## Receiving Critique
- Leave ego at the door: the question is whether the piece serves its goal, not whether the earlier decision was yours.
- Disagree without being disagreeable; explain the criterion behind your choice. Explaining your judgment is also how you discover your own criteria.
- Test usability first (can the main action or message be found?), then the business goal; align them where possible.
- Prefer small incremental changes that can be tested quickly over sweeping rewrites.
- Ask for the grading criteria and constraints up front; unwritten criteria produce surprise.
- Strong reactions to a specific example, positive or negative, are the most informative part of any review.

## Common Mistakes
- Judging first-impression aesthetics only, for work that lives a long time in use (see ch08 for logos).
- Prescribing before diagnosing ("make it 12 px lighter" with no reason).
- Reviewing a single page of a set, or a screen mock-up of a printed piece.
- Undefined adjectives passed between designer and client as if shared.
- Defending a past decision instead of testing it.
- Treating a loud critique session as a majority vote.

## Checklist
- [ ] Objective written in one sentence
- [ ] Read order written down and checked against the layout
- [ ] Reviewed at real size and as a whole set, one dimension per pass
- [ ] Each note has an observation, a reason and (if needed) a criterion or sketch
- [ ] Every vague adjective defined or replaced
- [ ] Problems routed to the right role if they are idea, direction or usability issues
