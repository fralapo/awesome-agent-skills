# Chapter 16: Hierarchy, Contrast, Balance and Isolation

## Core Idea
Hierarchy is dominance plus priority: what is seen first, second and last, set before styling. Contrast is the planned difference that creates it; it does not happen by itself the way proximity does. Balance distributes the weight that contrast creates; isolation lets one element win. This chapter is the working model; ch03 and ch10 give the type-level numbers and ch11 applies the model to posters and key art. Movement and rhythm: ch17.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three-stage journey: attract, intrigue, deliver.** The hook stops the viewer, the secondary layer explains, the finisher carries the message and the action.
- **Three levels of importance.** Level 1: the hook (largest element, main image or a headline of a few words). Level 2: secondary detail (subtitles, supporting graphics). Level 3: finisher (body copy, small details, call to action). Apply the three levels per section of a multi-area layout, and to type.
- **Three-flow rule.** Decide where the eye lands, where it goes second, where it ends. Check by stepping back or shrinking to a thumbnail; a calm path means it works, a bouncing eye means adjust contrast, spacing or alignment.
- **Points of interest (POIs).** Map where the eye enters and where it goes next. A POI is not always the biggest thing: a face, a headline, a strong colour or a combination. The first POI grabs, the next explains.
- **Entry, proof, action.** Entry at top left (for left-to-right readers): highest contrast, largest type, under about seven words. Proof next to it: a bold scannable statistic plus a close-cropped concrete visual. Action along the F or Z path in solid readable type, protected by a white-space "moat" and a single accent colour that does not compete with the headline.
- **Layered hierarchy.** Layer 1 hook (colour or visual stimulus), layer 2 anchor word (scale, contrast or overlap), then a question or concept, finally small body text. Clear in about 3 seconds, rewarding at about 15.
- **Scan templates.** F pattern: start top left, move right, then down a left-aligned column. Z pattern: top left, right, diagonal to bottom left, then bottom right. Even a central focal point works with them.

## Hierarchy Rules
- The hook is the largest visual, but **the hook is not always the most important information**: for a product ad the product is the hook; for an event the hook can be the artwork while date and place are the key facts. Decide separately which element attracts and which carries the message, and give the second one the contrast it needs (a bold date in the secondary tier, a stat next to the hook).
- Secondary tier size: ch10 gives 50 to 60 percent of the headline as a start; a gentler step (up to roughly 80 percent) works when the headline is modest. The rule is a clear gap, never near-equal sizes; the smaller the jump, the more weight, colour or position must separate the levels.
- Toolkit: size, weight, colour, orientation, white space, position, typeface style (serif against sans), texture. Size is only one lever: a small white logo on dark can outweigh a big logo on clutter.
- Colour order: warm saturated hues, red first, pull attention; a grey design with one coloured element is a classic emphasis tactic.
- **Odd groups**: five objects with one dominant compose more easily than four. A larger central object flanked by equal sets stands out; unequal flanks dilute it.
- **Serial position**: viewers remember the start and the end of a list, so put the key facts (date, venue) at the very top or the bottom.
- Save the strongest contrast for the most important element. Everything loud at once means the viewer builds the hierarchy themselves.
- **Contrast pacing**: high contrast where the focus lands, softer elsewhere so the eye can rest.
- Hierarchy needs white space; packed elements lose their distinction (ch14).
- Test: thumbnail or step-back; squint; real size and distance; ask someone to name what they remember, in order. A wrong first item is a failed hierarchy, a missed action a failed path. If you must explain the design, it has failed.

**Advanced hierarchy moves (situational, not for every piece)**
- *Selective blur*: blur a copy of the background and erase it (soft, low-opacity eraser) over the key elements.
- *Saturation contrast*: reduce the saturation of competing elements (through a gradient map towards grey) so the headline gains weight.
- *Pattern disruption*: shift a background pattern and stop it with a slightly lighter shade of the ground to open an expanse of space; put the top-level information there. Test flipping the direction.
- *Leading lines*: choose imagery whose lines (architecture, depth of field, a pointing limb, a gaze) ushers the eye to the key area.

## The Nine Forms of Contrast
Colour (opposites, low against high saturation, grey against colour), size, typography (big/small, bold/light, pairing), shape (curved against straight, rounded against sharp), texture (flat against grainy), style (flat minimal beside one photographic element; technical line icons beside cartoon 3D), psychology (a type or colour against its usual meaning: a serif in an urban hip-hop context), emotion (humour on a serious topic, joy next to sadness), concept. Rules:
- Use several at once: a poster can combine colour, shape (angular lines against a smooth product) and texture (grainy ground against a smooth object).
- Contrast is a range of differences, not only opposite hues. Readability depends on **value** difference (ch18).
- Two jobs: draw attention to something specific, and make the design interesting.
- Style, psychology and concept contrast are for specific briefs; overuse of style contrast is a known mistake.
- "Contrast 2.0" for feeds: texture contrast (a clean gradient with one grainy hand-drawn shape), motion contrast (one element pulses while the rest is still), conceptual contrast (a human, imperfect or candid element against polished machine-made visuals). Layered contrasts give stronger pull; motion must reinforce the hierarchy.
- Text over busy imagery: a solid or semi-transparent box, a gradient or a layer mask behind the text.
- Frequent flaw: too small a size gap between heading and subheading. CTAs gain from a contrasting colour and a contrasting shape (a round button in a square, straight-edged layout), and should read as clickable without dominating (a primary button about 1.5 to 2 times the height and width of standard text elements).
- The 3-second feed test: glance like a viewer scrolling; where does the eye land first and next? Habit also trains viewers to expect actions in familiar zones (a call to action in the lower third); arrows, chevrons and ghosted tap zones guide poster and carousel viewers too.

## Visual Weight and Balance
- Darker and denser = heavier. Isolation adds weight: a smaller element with more space around it can be heavier than a larger crowded one. Upper areas feel heavier; a dark dense element near the top weighs more than at the bottom. A small dark shape can outweigh a large light one.
- If you do not control weight, the composition controls where the viewer looks.
- **Symmetrical** balance is comfortable (groups of three beat two), formal and traditional; trust, finance and legal contexts can justify static symmetry. **Asymmetrical** balance: one large element against several small ones, type and fine graphics on the opposite side; lively, modern. Perfect symmetry can feel static and lifeless, extreme asymmetry chaotic: the sweet spot is a slight shift with a counterweight. **Radial**: content centred, space round the edges, weight evenly spread.
- Levers: size, colour, boldness, placement, texture (a textured image balanced by flat bold type that does not get lost).
- Colour balance: stay in one region of the wheel with a neutral accent; avoid jarring intruders; a grey main image keeps a soft mood. For a family of products keep saturation, temperature and tone in balance (one bold variant among subdued ones breaks the set).

## Isolation (single-tactic emphasis)
Make the star element stand out with exactly **one** tactic: a unique hue while the rest is muted, an unexpected scale (oversize or bleed), or subtle motion (blur, trail). Build three artboards side by side and compare; stacking all three on one element ends in noise. The direction-level logic (accent budget, scarcity, where the isolation effect fails) is in `art-director` ch11; the perception tests that verify the result (perception gap, attention thief, thumbnail, logo removal) are in `art-director` ch10.

## Common Mistakes
- Large elements without contrast, which disappear.
- Uniform full contrast everywhere, which flattens the hierarchy.
- Assuming the biggest element must carry the key information.
- Near-equal sizes in headline and subheading.
- A call to action in the colour of its surroundings.
- Competing brand mark, product name and specs in one cluster.
- Treating contrast as opposite hues only.

## Checklist
- [ ] Reading order (first, second, last) written before styling
- [ ] Hook and key information identified separately
- [ ] Three levels clearly different in size, weight and colour
- [ ] Strongest contrast reserved for the most important element
- [ ] Several forms of contrast combined, none overused
- [ ] Weight balanced on purpose (symmetry or counterweight)
- [ ] One isolation tactic, compared against alternatives
- [ ] Thumbnail, squint and 3-second tests passed; hierarchy survives grayscale
