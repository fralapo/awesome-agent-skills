# Chapter 13: Portfolio and Case-Study Review

## Core Idea
A portfolio must state one specific role and prove it with the best work, so that a sophisticated buyer can judge fit in seconds. A finished image proves taste; a case study proves thinking: the problem, the strategy, the system, the context and the result. Reviewing a portfolio, whether your own, a candidate's or a student's, uses the same criteria. Pricing, lead generation and freelance operations belong to `freelance-designer-ops`; this chapter covers the work and how it is presented.

## Review Procedure
1. **Positioning claim**: is there one clear role and one headline benefit for the buyer, not several labels (strategist, designer, ideator, storyteller)? Name the discipline concretely; generic terms ("strategic branding") mean nothing. Write headlines around the buyer's benefit, not the designer's identity.
2. **Buyer's scan order**: (1) the positioning claim; (2) the three best projects above the fold; (3) a case study showing the problem and your specific role; (4) process or behind the scenes; (5) clients, results and validation on one page. Buyers rarely click through several screens; put proof on the page.
3. **Answer the three objections**: experience, results, credibility. Proof is case studies, named clients, testimonials.
4. **Lead with the strongest work**: the first 20 to 30 seconds decide whether the viewer stays; skip long intros and countdowns. Show 3 to 5 professional-level pieces; weaker or student-level work signals the wrong level.
5. **Evaluate each case study** against the structure below.
6. **Check honesty**: on team projects, state exactly which part you did and credit the team; positioning yourself as the orchestrator of collaborative work reads better than a lone-author claim. Show the scale of your practice honestly. Keep the personal brand secondary to the case studies; separate sites or addresses for separate specialties.

## Case-Study Structure
1. **Pain points and strategy breakdown**: state the user's or client's problem first, then show how the design answers it.
2. **The visual system and what each element encodes**: make each colour or zone mean something and explain the code; otherwise it reads as arbitrary.
3. **Mock-ups in context** (wall, screen, product): a poster reads as a poster only when the context is shown.
4. **Role, result and process**: client, your role, outcome, how you got there; show process or behind the scenes.
5. Lead with the strongest piece, then break it down.

## Signs of Mature Practice
- Each decision traces back to the client's goal and pain point; early choices are not arbitrary.
- The sequence is broad strategy, then style exploration, then concept, then execution, and each step refers back to the previous artefact instead of restarting.
- The strategy document stays open at every stage and is used as a decision filter, not read once.
- Technical briefs count: responsive behaviour and developer handover are part of good design for web work.
- The designer can state the underlying meaning of an abstract piece; if a viewer cannot, the abstraction is too abstract.

## Presentation Craft
- Tone is part of the work: an early portfolio that boasts of ability, instead of showing evidence, fails. Messy sketches that carry the idea are acceptable; showing annotated sketches as part of the story demonstrates the reasoning.
- Give each piece of a series room to breathe, so viewers see separate items that also form one whole.
- Replace video-player frames with clean stills or thumbnails that show the outcome.
- Keep the style consistent: a flat-design portfolio (colour fields, simple icons, no gradients) is broken by one full photo.
- Surface the service line, do not bury it under project copy; do not repeat the same claim in every paragraph.
- Lead with purpose, then what, then how.
- A normally avoided effect (moire, for instance) can be justified when it expresses the concept; say why.
- T-shaped positioning: deep expertise in one area with a limited set of supporting skills, listing only skills you can back up with work.

## Common Mistakes
- Single images labelled "campaigns" with no case study.
- Playing a role (strategist) while showing only design deliverables.
- Abstract 3D forms or atmosphere added with no traceable meaning.
- Cluttered detail shots, loose spacing, a divider that splits the composition into two unrelated ideas.
- Too many claims at once; a "too cute" or self-congratulatory tone.
- A one-person site that looks like an agency, which can make agencies see you as a competitor.

## Checklist
- [ ] One role, one benefit headline
- [ ] Best three projects first
- [ ] Each case study: problem, system, context, role, result
- [ ] Decisions traceable to the strategy
- [ ] Credits and own role stated honestly
- [ ] Proof for experience, results and credibility on the page
