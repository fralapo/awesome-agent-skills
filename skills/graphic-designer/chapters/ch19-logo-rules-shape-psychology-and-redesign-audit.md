# Chapter 19: Logo Rules, Logotype Craft, Shape Psychology and the Redesign Audit

## Core Idea
Ch06 covers construction and delivery, ch08 ideation, critique and reproduction tests. This chapter adds the rule set around them: the principles a mark is judged on, what to check before and after drawing (context, voice, distinctiveness), logotype-specific rules, an extended shape map, negative-space marks, an anchor-point audit and a critique list for redesigns. A logo is a memory hook and one cog of an identity system, made for the client's customers and not for the client; it carries one clear idea and does not have to say what the business does. Brand strategy, positioning and the decision to rebrand stay with the art director (`art-director` ch05, ch07, ch12).

## Four Principles and the Current Upgrade
1. **Simplicity**: a busy mark weakens the message; a simple mark is easy to remember and triggers emotion rather than analysis. Real simplicity strips noise but keeps the weight and psychology of the idea; it is not three generic shapes. Simpler marks also read as more trustworthy (technology, wellness, finance): set the level of simplicity to the trust the brief requires, because a mark the brain must decode costs energy and memorability.
2. **Scalability**: from a 16 px favicon and a business card to a stadium screen. Design the scale into every step; a mark that is beautiful enlarged and a smudge small has failed.
3. **Legibility**: the symbol is recognisable at once and not a puzzle; the logotype is readable (kerning, leading, size, weight, pairing). Clever does not outrank clear; make one strong impact with one solid design.
4. **Relatability**: logotype and mark are the voice of the brand (friendly or loud, modest or quiet, proud) and fit the target market.
Extra checks for current use: shape-first memorability (blur, shrink or glimpse the mark; the basic gesture must register before any detail), flat one-colour test (if the idea dies, colour was carrying the concept), about one second to register at thumb-scroll speed, works on dark mode, in a UI and in motion. Design a **system of marks**: full lockup, simplified symbol, micro mark, wordmark, avatar. Assign each version to a placement and test at real size; do not create versions only to fill a guide. Provide stacked and horizontal arrangements where the context needs them (a stacked layout suits phone use).

## Before Drawing
- **Brief questions**: what should the audience understand, what makes it different from the alternatives, where will people meet it? Ask the client for a short questionnaire answer early. Turn vague adjectives ("modern, bold, premium, trustworthy") into judgeable requirements (easy to identify on moving equipment, confident without aggression, distinct from space imagery); two designers read "premium" completely differently.
- **One-sentence brief**: reduce the brief to one or two lines ("a system that quietly protects your data in the background") and check every decision against it.
- **Think in verbs.** Define the brand by verbs (protect, connect, accelerate, simplify, guide) rather than nouns (shield, arrow, mountain, initials), which breed clichés. Then build shape psychology around the action; combine verb and noun for specificity (a generic shield softened with calm horizontal lines for insurance). Three angles for ideas: what the business does, how it does it, what people get.
- **Audience**: know the client's customers: how they dress, what they absorb, what they signal. One quick method: find a successful direct competitor, read the bios of the accounts that interact with it, draft a psychographic (values, lifestyle, goals, pain points). Every concept should reflect at least one feeling of that audience.
- **Voice alignment** (5 words the brand would never say, 5 it would overuse, one 140-character sentence it would post). A bold voice means no timid mark; a calm voice means no aggressive angles.
- **Context first**: place the brand name in plain text inside realistic mock-ups (a round social avatar, a mobile header, a label) before sketching, and scan what the category is crowded with (shields, geometric sans, black-and-white minimal). Seeing the real environment stops over-detailing.
- **One sketch, one idea**: if you cannot say it in one sentence it is too complicated. Do not start from the first industry cliché (coffee bean, mountain, light bulb); use it only as a starting point and simplify. Explore wordmarks too; not every brand needs a symbol.
- Design in black and white first, and keep the sketching on paper (ch06, ch08).

## Distinctiveness Tests (add to ch08 tests)
- **Ownability gap**: put 20 category logos on one artboard at the same size with names hidden. If the marks could swap names, they are interchangeable. Find the visual space nobody uses (proportions, tension, a different typographic voice, bolder simplification). Aim to be ownable, not weird. If your eye does not land on your mark in the grid, push further.
- **Side-by-side under identical conditions**: first plain black on the ground, then on the real item (a clothing label), then at the smallest important placement.
- **Five-second recall**: show the mark for 5 seconds, hide it, ask people to find it in a lineup; if several pick the same wrong one, study the confusion.
- **Feeling test**: show it unexplained and ask for adjectives; compare with the brief.
- **Look-alike check**: reverse image search before presenting.

## Logotype Rules
1. **Letter-for-symbol swaps.** The general rule is not to replace a letter of a wordmark with a symbol: it looks amateur and some readers drop the word. The counter-position is one subtle brand-relevant touch (a rounded play triangle in a media name, rounded corners) for character. Decision rule: the word must still read first, instantly, with the symbol absent from your reading effort; if it needs a moment, undo it.
2. **Type communicates to the audience.** Ask what the brand sells, to whom, and how the buyer wants to feel. Bold condensed italic sans = sport and forward motion; refined light roman or serif = status and elegance; a serif is too formal for martial arts or sport; a friendly playful face is wrong for a funeral director. Letter case changes the message (lowercase humbler, uppercase more assertive in the same face).
3. **Legible at all scales.** Step back 1.2 to 1.8 m (4 to 6 ft) from the monitor and print at several sizes. Offer two versions: tight spacing for large use, wider spacing for small use.
4. **Kern by hand**, never by automatic kerning, and not by raising global tracking. Set the overall value for the brand feel first, then outline the type, ungroup, and nudge pairs. Tricks: blur or squint to see gaps; kern in groups of three letters, then combine; flip the word upside down to remove reading bias. Round letters (O, C) look smaller than straight ones and may be enlarged slightly; diagonal and curved letters (V, A, G) create uneven apparent gaps (see ch03). Tight kerning reads strong or urgent, loose reads luxurious or calm.
5. **Pair type with roles.** The name is the loud, memorable element (big, bold, often uppercase); the tagline is small and quiet (title case). Serif with sans or bold with thin; avoid two dramatic or complicated faces.
6. **Avoid trending faces**; the logotype must not need a trend to work. Check the commercial licence, or build custom letters.
7. **Match logotype to symbol**: no playful symbol with rigid serif, no playful organic font on a technology brand, no odd italic on half a wordmark.
8. Variable fonts speed exploration of weight, width and slant (a slightly condensed cut may suit a tall mark) and client iteration.

## Shape Map (additions to ch06)
| Form | Reading |
|---|---|
| Circle | community, unity, friendship, love, perfection; attention-seeking (about 20 percent of a 50-brand sample used one as the main element) |
| Oval | sturdiness, endurance (common in vehicle marks) |
| Square, rectangle, triangle, straight edges | power, strength, professionalism, efficiency, stability; banks and real estate; with red or blue, dynamic modernism |
| Vertical lines | strength, structure, energy (a secure system) |
| Horizontal lines | calm, balance, protection (stronger with blue) |
| Angled lines | energy, speed, motion |
| Curves | friendliness, happiness, generosity, rhythm; curve plus angle can read as innovation |
| Shield | protection, stability |
| Hexagon | connection, technology |
Rounded forms and a circle round an angular mark suit trust-based sectors; spiky serif forms do not. Straight geometry for man-made and rigid subjects, curves for natural and living ones; asymmetry done well feels organic and alive, symmetry gives order. A circle does not always mean love: context decides. Pair shape emotion with colour and type of the same emotion.

## Negative-Space Marks
Four categories: double entendre (two readings), typographic (letters from empty space), hidden image (an animal in a mountain), enclosed (the empty space as the border of a full image). They are memorable, simple and distinctive, but only when the brand suits them and the idea is planned from the start. Letter-and-object method: pick a letter and a base shape, sketch, build guides from the base shape, draw secondary shapes along its angles, convert strokes to fills, carve with minus-front, use a small element (a star) to imply the missing part of the letter, keep secondary details minimal. Build non-destructively (white shapes or compound shapes) until the end.

## Vector Craft Audit
- **Anchor audit** in outline mode: delete extra points on straight lines, remove fighting handles and uneven bezier tension; each curve does one job. Fewer points = smoother, scales and exports better, easier for the next designer.
- Build with preset shapes (rectangle, ellipse) and mirror tools for precise symmetry; do not start with shape-combining before the concept is decided; use sketches as locked guides, low snap tolerance, outline mode to check precision.
- Fake overlaps with offset paths, then remove the white areas (ch06). Outline all strokes and type before delivery, align to guides, smooth rough corners.
- Record a scale-test action (duplicate repeatedly at 75 percent) to see the mark from large to tiny.
- Grid (late): circular grids for organic marks, straight and 45 degree for rigid; constant unit widths (1x, 2x), repeated angles (for example 72 degrees), controlled symbol-to-type space. Show the grid to the client and the next designer.

## Redesign Audit (existing mark)
1. **Scalability**: too much detail for business-card size? (over-detailed illustrative marks blur small).
2. **Outdated look** and effects (gradients, bevels, drop shadows).
3. **Shape psychology wrong for the sector** (square or spiky for a trust-based sector).
4. **Weak logotype**: odd italics on part of the word, mixed upright and italic halves, counters that close at small size or distance.
5. **Unintended expression**: slanted eyebrows read as angry or threatening.
6. **Heritage and equity**: research the symbol's meaning before keeping, simplifying or dropping it. Keep what carries meaning (the ears of an animal mark, a protective roof) and drop what does not.
7. **Colour equity**: if the public strongly links a colour to the brand (a yellow bank), moving away risks confusion; let brand assets carry the colour if the mark itself can go neutral.
8. **Tests**: full grayscale, reversed white on colour, small size, real-use mock-ups (card, phone).
Logos cannot be redone properly in minutes: a timed no-research attempt yields generic results, although short iterations improve a design. Copy the file before each change to keep earlier states; the second version is usually better than the first.

## Presentation and Handover Additions
- Show at most three fully reasoned systems (one or two for experienced practitioners), each tied to the brief and audience, in an **identical template** so ideas compete on strength, not on presentation. Add one **stretch** concept that is still on-brief and good; if not chosen it makes the others feel safer.
- Open by restating the project goal and what a good logo is (timeless, simple, an extension of the brand) to limit subjective input; start the logo section with a brand-colour split screen (half brand colour with a white logo, half white with the logo in colour); use human, unusual mock-ups; narrate the feeling in context ("calm, confident and spacious on packaging"), not just "logo on packaging". Presentation structure and the persuasion side: `art-director` ch12.
- Motion follows the mark's own direction and the brand character (a slow reveal and hold for refined brands, a bounce for playful ones).
- Handover: clearly named vector files and colour modes, an example of each version in use, a one-page read-me (which file for website, avatar, printer; contact), physical samples flagged. Over-deliver on promises. Pricing, client tiers and scope for a logo job go to `pricing-and-sales-negotiation`.

## Warning Signs
Gimmick effects; raster artefacts; uneven spacing and alignment; inconsistent line weight; busy illustration-like marks; inappropriate or confrontational imagery; type that does not match the symbol; no audience analysis; no iteration; missing vector files; a mark that needs explaining; a stylescape-level vision alignment skipped (stylescapes: `art-director` ch06).

## Checklist
- [ ] One-sentence brief and a verb for the brand written
- [ ] Mock-up context and category scan done before sketching
- [ ] Ownability grid of about 20 competitors checked
- [ ] Mark survives blur, 16 px, one colour, dark mode
- [ ] Logotype kerned by hand, two spacing versions, no trend face
- [ ] Anchor audit done; strokes outlined
- [ ] Redesign: heritage, colour equity and expression checked
- [ ] Systems of marks mapped to placements; read-me included
