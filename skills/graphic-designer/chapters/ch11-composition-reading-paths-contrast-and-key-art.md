# Chapter 11: Composition: Reading Paths, Contrast and Key Art

## Core Idea
Contrast in size, value, weight and colour controls what the eye sees first and how it travels. A composition is a designed path: entry point, route, destination (usually the title or the call to action). Posters, covers and key art are judged by their job first: stopping a passer-by who knows nothing about the subject and telling them genre and promise at a glance. The idea behind the image comes from the creative director and the look from the art director; this chapter is the compositional craft.

## Procedure: Build the Path
1. **Write the three reads**: first read (largest, darkest, highest contrast), second read (close behind it), tertiary (small, light, supporting). The most important element gets the most prominence; giving a supporting element the strongest contrast sends the eye to the wrong place.
2. **Start extreme, then pull back**: exaggerate the differences first, then reduce to a comfortable level.
3. **Choose a path**: a Z-pattern works for many posters (strongest focal point at the start, horizontal move, diagonal cut down, horizontal to the bottom). Faces and eyes are the strongest entry; their eyeline can be traced and made to end on the title. A sequence of three items can be ordered clockwise by varying weight, size and value.
4. **Add leading lines** (beams, paths, directional objects) that converge on the focal point; make light, a gradient flow (light to mid to dark) or a high-contrast element carry the eye on.
5. **Check the context**: imagine the piece on a street or in a foyer, seen by someone with no prior knowledge. Would it make them want to see, buy or read more? Imagery must signal the intended genre; a pose or prop from the wrong genre sends the wrong signal.
6. **Judge at the real scale**: a cover on a shelf or in a display set, a poster on a wall, a thumbnail for a heavy high-contrast image.

## Depth and Contrast Rules
- Foreground carries more contrast (darker darks, brighter brights); the background recedes with less contrast and lighter values. Atmospheric depth: keep foreground blacks deep, lift and soften the middle ground.
- Add contrast **locally on the subject**, not globally; a global curve can blow out the highlights. In a dark image lift shadows with a curve, then mask the adjustment where it muddies the blacks.
- Scale contrast alone can tell the story: a large foreground against a small background, or a tiny secondary element beside a huge one, communicates size without words.
- **Changing the point of view means flipping the contrast**: the viewer's subject becomes large and dark, the hidden subject small and recessive. Poses can stay unchanged; scale and value change the story.
- Treat type like any other shape; the same rules apply.
- Composited stills need unified grain or noise and consistent perspective; badly masked, faded edges look lazy.
- Where a silhouette merges into a background of similar value, add an edge highlight or separate the values.

## Key-art Review Order
1. Eye path and distractions (remove slivers of stray detail that pull the eye, unless they carry context).
2. Crop and headroom (crop excess headroom to keep focal figures tight; no stray clothing edges creating tangents).
3. Edge separation.
4. Value depth.
5. Text associations: credits or names placed beside a figure read as that figure's name; place them under the title or in a separate row.
6. Motion paths and props must not cut across bodies or look like collisions; let elements overlap the central object rather than protrude from it, as protrusions pull attention out of proportion.

## Restraint, Symmetry and Tension
- **Symmetry makes hierarchy unambiguous**: one dominant element, everything else clearly secondary. A perfectly symmetric layout can also create uncertainty about where to look; a slight asymmetry (in the credits, or one element shifted off centre and reduced) adds tension and opens negative space. A strong centre axis reads as rigid.
- **Deliberate asymmetry** (one side left empty, the strongest element on the other) creates unease that suits a dark subject, but only when you know the rule you are breaking. Where the subject calls for tension, symmetry makes the composition flat.
- **Restraint beats spectacle.** When a mark will repeat on merchandise and signage, let it be the hero on one strong colour and an empty dark field; the emptiness sets the tone. Leave out the effects the product is famous for; show the core. Crowding every main face in is the default, generic approach. Extruded 3D effects on a mark that works flat weaken it. An alternative that adds every possible element dilutes the single message.
- **Hierarchy by revelation**: a small tagline the viewer must lean in to read adds tension and keeps the composition clean. This suits a piece seen once at close range; for far-distance pieces keep key information large.
- **Ambiguity** (an unexpected or oblique viewpoint, a path toward a bright opening) raises a question that holds attention; for pieces seen repeatedly (street posters) meaning can arrive gradually. Do not let ambiguity mislead about who is who or what is the subject.
- **Underlying shape**: read the composition's hidden shape (for example an hourglass) and make images support it. Lower the opacity of a background symbol so it frames content without competing with the title. Wide tracking on bold sans type creates unease when the spacing echoes lines in the artwork.
- An unusual framing device (a figure peering through a wall of text, a rotated interface element) can pull the viewer in if it ties to the subject.
- Emphasis must be unambiguous: make the figure clearly larger or let it fill the space.

## Common Mistakes
- All values equal, which flattens hierarchy.
- The eye lands on a vehicle, prop or decoration when the designer wants it elsewhere.
- Header placed in the middle of a crowded area instead of open space.
- Hard, high-contrast linework that cuts through the layout.
- Adding a decorative line "as an anchor" without testing it on the actual layout; opinions differ, so test.
- Retro frame, texture or finish used without the era-specific details (dark image inside a light frame, a consistent aligned block of small credits).

## Checklist
- [ ] First, second, third read written and visible
- [ ] Eye path traced from entry to destination
- [ ] Foreground and background contrast set, local not global
- [ ] Credits and names not associated with the wrong figure
- [ ] Genre and promise clear to a stranger
- [ ] Symmetry or asymmetry chosen on purpose
- [ ] Judged at real display scale
