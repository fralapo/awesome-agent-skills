# Chapter 15: Grid Construction from the Brief and Layout Workflows

## Core Idea
Ch02 explains what a grid is and gives the A4 print setup. This chapter shows how to **build** a grid from a brief with arithmetic, how to choose among grid types, and three repeatable layout workflows for when you face a pile of content and a blank canvas. A grid is chosen after the brief is known, never before: what the message is, where the piece will be seen, and what the viewer should understand or do. It is then tested with changed content, other formats and hidden guides.

## Procedure 1: Brief Before Grid (three questions)
1. **Message or vibe**: calm, shocking, urgent, premium, informative? A chaotic shock message can justify a busy radical layout; a calm, health or luxury message needs simplicity; a tense layout is wrong for a conservative brand.
2. **Where it is seen**: business card, A4 page, banner, phone, watch, billboard. The format sets the structure (a tall narrow page runs top to bottom; a banner runs left to right).
3. **Purpose**: a selling piece leads from an attention hook down a clean path to the action; an informative piece makes information findable.
Also check client style guides first: they override taste (permitted logo versions, minimum size, minimum clear space, banned effects such as drop shadows, prescribed fonts). Ignoring them gets work rejected.

## Procedure 2: Build the Grid in Five Steps
1. **Content and audience.** Who views it, where, and what must they understand or do? List the content in order of attention.
2. **Margins.** Fit them to the format and visual language: open for restrained campaigns, narrower for information-heavy work. Print: roughly 0.5 to 1 in (about 2.5 cm) plus about 0.5 cm bleed, larger for billboards; digital can be smaller.
3. **Columns and gutters.** Formula: column width = (canvas width - outer margins - total gutter space) / number of columns. Worked example: a 1200 px poster, 60 px margins (usable 1080), six columns, 24 px gutters (five gutters = 120 px): (1200 - 120 - 120) / 6 = 160 px per column. Six columns give halves, thirds and sixths; twelve give finer control for a wide banner; ten is a flexible general choice. Choose by task: a timetable needs quick findability, a cultural event can be surprising, a product comparison needs consistent areas.
4. **Spans by priority.** Allocate column spans along the sequence of attention (event: name, date, place, ticket link; product: benefit, details, purchase). The widest area is not automatically seen first: faces, bright colour and contrast pull attention in small areas, and a pale product photo needs a larger span or stronger type. Wider span = higher rank (two columns over one).
5. **Vertical spacing from a base unit** (see ch14): related items close, a larger gap before the next group; check at the intended viewing size (phone, watch). For text-heavy pages add a baseline grid: body leading is the base and headline sizes are multiples (a 14 pt body with a 28 pt headline).

**Test before sign-off**
- **Content change**: lengthen the headline. Widen the span (four columns to five) and adjust breaks and size rather than simply shrinking type; outer margins stay fixed.
- **Format change**: move the same system to a banner, a story, a square. Type, colour relationships and spacing rules stay; only column arrangement changes. That consistency is what makes a campaign.
- **Guides hidden**: view at the intended size without guides. Is the message clear, is the action findable, does it fit the brand? Ask someone unfamiliar what they notice first.
- Banner example: headline, support text and call to action share one start edge on a four-column area; repeated alignment spares the eye a search.

## Grid Types (pick by content)
| Type | Use |
|---|---|
| Column | editorial, web, brochures |
| Modular (columns plus rows) | mixed content, products, galleries; vary module size or span two modules to highlight a bestseller; not every module has to be filled |
| Manuscript | single column with margin notes |
| Hierarchical | big modules for headline and action, less prominent for navigation and footer |
| Asymmetric | hero takes more space, small columns carry the secondary flow |
| Square | gallery feel; colour or pattern in some squares |
| Rule of thirds | place the subject slightly off the lines for tension |
| Compound | column plus modular; keep breathing space between the combined grids |
| Baseline | aligns body, headlines and captions across columns |
| Circular, triangular, isometric | logos, patterns, geometric systems; isometric: lighter tones on top, darker below for volume |
The golden ratio (about 1:1.618) is a starting point to tweak by eye, not a rule. Grids also set hierarchy (span more columns), create white space (decide which rows and columns stay empty; subdivide, for example 3 by 4 into 6 by 6, for finer micro-space control) and control line length.

## Three Layout Workflows
1. **Hierarchy approach.** Sort all content into primary, secondary and tertiary groups (music poster: event name and key image / artists and dates / web address and social handles). Differentiate first by size, then by colour or texture (bold and flat for primary, quieter or faded for tertiary). Keep assets in three folders by level.
2. **Modular layout.** Pick the module shape, decide how many modules (the count controls busyness; a square page might be four large modules each split into four small). Large important elements take one or two large modules, small ones the inner modules. Draw **buffer lines** slightly inside the module lines so elements never touch (micro white space). Keep all grid lines on one layer that can be toggled.
3. **Asymmetrical layout.** Set the goal, place the focal point off centre, and balance it with a cluster of smaller elements on the opposite side (text, a call to action that stands out by colour and contrast). White space and contrast separate the two zones.
For a text-heavy page: reduce type and image size, repair awkward gaps and rivers, repeat dividing lines, group long descriptions in their own proximity zones, then reorder like a puzzle. A small touch (a bolder, differently coloured price) can lift the whole page.

## Breaking the Grid on Purpose
- Keep most elements on the grid and let one break it for contrast. An off-centre headline should still align with an image edge, a block or supporting text; invisible lines run through the whole layout so every element relates to something.
- Use guides for structure, then adjust by eye for polish. Misalignment from unchecked guides is sloppy, not bold.
- **Overlap**: type spilling over an image or a shape over a title adds layers, rhythm and hierarchy; two deliberate overlaps can transform a flat layout. Repeat overlaps so they read as intent (ch10).
- **Frame-breaking and bleed**: let a portrait or an asset leave its rectangle or cross the bleed edge. Repeat it lower on the page but do not mirror it, or it loses dynamism.
- **Colour blocking** organises complex layouts into zones that also signal meaning (a coloured block behind social links, a block under footer text).
- **Diptych and triptych**: two or three panels telling a visual narrative (three characters on a film poster; concept, process, final product for a brand). Keep colour, typography and styling identical across panels.
- Image choice and placement follow the message and reading path, with enough space round them; match the image style to the context (hand-drawn can feel more authentic than a photo for some food menus).
- Every break has a reason tied to the brief. Experimental grids suit recurring pieces (a monthly poster series) and non-conformist messages, not insurance.

## Common Mistakes
- Choosing columns before knowing the content.
- Shrinking type instead of reallocating the span when copy grows.
- Judging spacing only on a desktop screen.
- Different gutters for rows and columns.
- Elements touching with no buffer.
- A grid used as a cage, or ignored without a reason.
- Mirroring a bleed effect on both sides.

## Checklist
- [ ] Message, place and purpose written down; client style guide read
- [ ] Margins, column maths and gutters stated as numbers
- [ ] Spans follow the attention order
- [ ] Vertical spacing from one base unit; baseline grid for text-heavy pieces
- [ ] Longer headline, other format and hidden-guide tests passed
- [ ] Any grid break repeated or clearly intentional, and still aligned to a hidden line
