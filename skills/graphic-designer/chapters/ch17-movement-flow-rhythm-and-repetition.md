# Chapter 17: Movement, Flow, Rhythm and Repetition

## Core Idea
Hierarchy (ch16) says what ranks first; movement says how the eye travels between ranks, and repetition says why the pieces belong together. Directional cues serve the hierarchy: the goal is a path of importance, not motion for its own sake. Repetition is the glue of a piece, a series or a brand, and deliberately breaking it creates the focal point. Poster and key-art specifics are in ch11; direction-level consistency across touchpoints is in `art-director` ch03.

## The Six Levels of Movement
| Level | Technique | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Direct guidance | arrows, gaze, pointing limbs, a bridge or path, a body turned to the logo | the eye follows instinctively; headline top left, cue to the action bottom right |
| 2. Hierarchy-driven flow | size, weight, contrast act like gravity, no arrows | editorial and minimal posters; large bold, then medium, then small |
| 3. Layered flows | one main route plus smaller micro-routes (a few small type blocks that make a short loop and rejoin) | holds the viewer longer; secondary routes must stay weaker than the main one |
| 4. Implied motion | repetition, progressive scaling, blur, directional patterns, tapering gradients with texture, frozen-action photography, repeated geometry getting tighter or looser | suggestion only, no animation needed |
| 5. Flow disruption | an unexpected element on the path (a block rotated 45 degrees across the flow) | the brain wants to resolve an unresolved path; use to slow the viewer at a key message, never at random |
| 6. Temporal flow | control rhythm and pace: impact, linger, release | full-bleed hero = fast hit; fine detail and serif copy = slow scan; lone logo in white space = pause. A product page: hero hit, slow technical blocks, rest in open space |

Too many competing paths make chaos; most designers ignore level 6.

## Direction and Structure
- **Format dictates direction**: a tall narrow format reads diagonally or vertically (key information at the bottom where the eye finishes); a squarer format reads horizontally. Match type slant to the movement of the subject and send type in the direction of the gaze.
- **Composition direction**: horizontal = calm, stable; vertical = balance, boldness, alertness; diagonal = movement, action (energetic warm colours suit it). Layout direction sets the base feeling.
- **Four aspects of visual movement**: movement (shapes working together imply upward or lateral motion), shape of element (a rectangle or slanted shape carries the eye along its axis), structure, subject matter (pointing fingers, arms, gazes).
- **Structural net**: a canvas has inherent structure: the focus sits slightly above the exact centre, the diagonals run corner to corner, there are midpoints between centre and corners, joined by horizontal and vertical axes. Align elements with these paths and they feel natural.
- **Visual tension**: elements pointing in unrelated directions against the net read as disorganised and uncomfortable. Replace them with one solid focal point unless discomfort is the goal.
- Brightness emphasis ushers the eye: a bright graphic top right, then diagonal down to a bright graphic bottom left, then the finishing point (left-to-right readers). Colour flow: a bold hue on the headline, shifting gradually to softer related hues down the layout, then one contrasting colour for the call to action.
- Leading lines from a dominant asset can point to the next asset; use depth of field or architectural lines to funnel the eye into text.

## L-I-F-T: A Four-Step Layout System
1. **Leverage point.** What is the single most important thing? Make it dominant through scale, contrast, position and isolation; push back anything that competes; supporting elements lead towards it. If a glance finds no focal point, the layout is visual noise.
2. **Internal rhythm.** Choreograph the path from the leverage point with consistent spacing, predictable alignment and deliberate contrast. Consistent margins and gutters relax the viewer; one well-placed disruption (a glowing call to action, an off-axis 3D object) re-engages. Fast rhythm for skimmable content, slow deliberate rhythm for dense content. Ask: does the eye jump or glide, are related items grouped, is spacing consistent?
3. **Friction and flow.** Intentional tension (tight leading, jarring objects, unusual type, cropped or blurred elements) balanced by clearly smooth reading zones. Friction is seasoning: just enough adds depth, too much ruins the dish. Audit each friction point: does it add clarity or noise? Remove decoration with no job; too many fonts, colours or focal points is noise. Too much tension = chaos, too much smoothness = boredom. (Conventional practice calls friction a fault; here the line is intent and balance with calm zones.)
4. **Transferability.** The design must survive other sizes, formats and backgrounds: thumbnail (type readable, identity recognisable?), dark and light grounds, mobile, print flyer, motion. Hierarchy holds when small; the layout flexes while the identity stays. A piece that works in only one ideal setting is a problem, not a solution.

## Rhythm
- Repeat spacing, type size and shape proportions to create a beat, then break the beat once (a headline across the grid, a striking graphic) to give that element weight; follow with alignment, contrast or supporting text towards the final detail.
- Repeat a small coloured accent in varied hues for rhythm without repeated shapes; colour grouping works as proximity (regions in a grid), and removing it makes layouts feel lost.
- Repeated spacing values (spatial echo) are in ch20.

## Repetition, Consistency and Unity
- **Carriers of repetition**: colour palette, typeface and type style, line, size and scale, shape, texture, layout and grid, graphics and illustration style, logo placement, and even language and tone (audio and format for apps).
- Repetition produces consistency, memorability, recognition and professionalism. Packaging that repeats background, imagery, font and logo placement is recognisable even without the logo; changing colour, font, layout and imagery at once makes the brand unrecognisable.
- Repeat small details too: icon style and texture, button shapes, back-to-top arrow, rounded chat button echoing circles, equal white-space amounts. Test: close your eyes; if you recall palette, mood and layout, repetition worked.
- Reuse the same grid across related pieces so they feel cohesive even when colour and imagery change.
- Match type style to illustration style: rustic hand-drawn art needs a bold slightly vintage face, not a sleek minimalist one; carry the same illustration style and muted palette to packaging and menus.
- **Break repetition sparingly and only inside a repeating system**: one circular hover area in an angular site is a focal point; a distinct colour in a category of similar colours differentiates. Breaking a pattern that was never established is just disorder.
- A portfolio shows repetition as single authorship: black and white throughout with colour reserved for the highlight, the same type styles, a consistent background texture, a recurring motif.

## Proportion
- One aspect ratio for related images (for example 3:2); mixed ratios look chaotic and distorted images look wrong.
- A grid keeps text, images and captions in uniform modules; a fixed type scale per brand across collateral (brand guidelines forbid resizing logo or text lockups).
- Standardise buttons: same size, colour and placement; the primary button larger (about 1.5 to 2 times standard text elements), secondary smaller but still tappable.
- Break proportion on purpose only for a focal point (an oversize headliner photo) or intentional disorder (text bleeding across a spread).

## Gestalt Quick Reference
Continuation (the eye follows paths, arches, streaks; take the viewer from subject to tagline to logo), figure/ground and closure (the brain completes negative space; a hidden shape rewards the viewer), symmetry and order (a disordered composition makes viewers repair it instead of reading the message), similarity (same colour, shape or size links items far apart), proximity (close items share a relationship; group title, body and button together, separate navigation and other columns).

## Common Mistakes
- Arrows or lines that point at nothing important.
- Secondary routes as loud as the main route.
- Random disruption with no message behind it.
- Several elements pointing off the canvas structure.
- Changing several repetition carriers at once.
- Breaking a pattern before the pattern exists.
- Motion in a feed used as decoration rather than to reinforce hierarchy.

## Checklist
- [ ] Format direction chosen on purpose (tall, wide, square)
- [ ] Main route, then micro-routes weaker than it
- [ ] One disruption per piece, tied to a key message
- [ ] Leverage point, rhythm, friction audit and transfer test run (L-I-F-T)
- [ ] Repetition carriers listed and kept constant across the series
- [ ] Any broken repetition sits inside an established system
- [ ] One aspect ratio and one type scale per family of pieces
