# Chapter 13: Words, Pictures & Multimedia Learning

## Core Idea
Slides, narrated animations and explainer pages teach through two channels (words and pictures) feeding a small working memory. In this model, design has three jobs: cut processing that does not serve the goal, pace the processing that does, and invite the viewer to make sense of the material. Words plus well-placed graphics beat words alone by a wide margin, but a badly integrated graphic can be worse than none.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Select, organize, integrate:** learning from words and pictures means selecting the relevant pieces, organizing each channel into a coherent model, and integrating the two with what the viewer already knows. Understanding is the integration step.
When to use: judging any teaching slide, explainer or narrated video.

**Three kinds of mental work:** *extraneous* (effort that does not serve the goal: decoration, split attention, hunting for labels), *essential* (effort to represent the content), *generative* (effort to make sense of it and link it to prior knowledge). Reduce the first, pace the second, invite the third.
When to use: diagnosing why a slide overwhelms or bores.

**No / rote / meaningful learning:** judge by two tests: retention (can they recall it) and transfer (can they use it in a new situation). Good on both is meaningful learning; good on retention only is rote. Check transfer, not recall.
When to use: choosing how to test whether a lesson or presentation worked.

## Rules & Numbers
Effect sizes are medians of controlled lab studies (above about 0.4 is educationally important, 0.8 large). Most evidence is from short science lessons with beginners, so treat the rules as strong defaults, not laws.

**Reduce extraneous processing**
- *Coherence (large, about 0.9):* leave out material that does not serve the goal: decorative graphics, background music, "interesting" facts that are memorable but lure attention from the mechanism. Viewers remember the fun fact and miss the process.
- *Signalling (about 0.4):* if irrelevant material cannot be removed, highlight what matters: headings, bold, a stronger voice on keywords. Arrows and flashing cues helped less.
- *Redundancy (large):* narration plus identical on-screen text is worse than narration plus graphics, because the eyes read instead of looking. Short labels beside the graphic part they name are fine.
- *Spatial contiguity (above 1.0):* put words inside or next to the part of the graphic they describe. A legend with numbers, or a caption at the bottom, forces eye travel. Same words and graphic, different position, large difference.
- *Temporal contiguity (above 1.0):* present matching words and pictures at the same moment; a few seconds of offset between narration and animation destroys understanding.

**Manage essential processing**
- *Segmenting (large):* break complex material into viewer-paced chunks (a continue button after each step) instead of one continuous run or one dense diagram.
- *Pre-training (about 0.75):* teach names and characteristics of the parts first, then show the whole system.
- *Modality (about 0.75):* with a fast, complex graphic, spoken words beat printed words, because the eyes stay on the picture. Reverse it for slow lessons, simple content, hard-to-hear terms, non-native listeners, or when the viewer needs to look back.

**Foster generative processing**
- *Personalization:* conversational wording (first and second person, polite suggestions) beats formal third person.
- *Voice:* a human voice beats a synthetic one; the obviously artificial breaks the sense of a social exchange.

**Boundary conditions**
- Most rules help beginners; for experts, extra visuals can get in the way of their own mental imagery.
- For physical skills where parts must work together, practise the whole, not the parts.
- Pictures alone usually do not teach: viewers read the words first and use them to steer where they look in the picture.
- Novices choose poorly when given full control of media options and misjudge what they learned. Give them more guidance; give experienced learners more freedom.
- Active learning means cognitive activity, not physical activity. A hands-on task that triggers no selecting, organizing or linking teaches little.
- The medium does not cause learning, the method does. A slide deck pushes everything on screen at once; drawing on a board or revealing step by step paces the content.

## Procedures
**Slide check (teaching slide):** one purpose; delete decoration that does not explain; label inside the graphic; no caption duplicating the narration; heading that tells what the slide is about; step it if the diagram has more than a handful of parts.
**Narrated explainer check:** narration and animation in sync; no duplicate subtitles unless needed for access or language; pause points between steps; names of parts introduced before the full system; conversational script, real voice.
**Accessibility override:** always provide text when a viewer cannot hear, or for dense terminology, in a short form next to the relevant graphic.

## Common Mistakes
- Pretty but unrelated graphic or fun fact → remove, or tie it to the mechanism.
- Legend with numbers → put each label beside the part.
- Caption that reads the narration aloud → keep one channel.
- One dense diagram shown at once → segment and pre-train.
- Narration offset from the animation → synchronize.
- Machine voice and formal prose → human voice, "you".
- Testing recall only → test transfer.

## Mental Models
- **Two lanes, one bottleneck:** pictures and words travel on separate lanes into one narrow gate; do not send the same load down both.
- **Same content, different position:** moving a label can matter more than rewriting a sentence.
- **Memorable is not the same as meaningful:** a vivid fact can crowd out the point it was meant to decorate.
- **Beginners need rails, experts need room.**

## Examples
- **Pump explainer:** text alone left about a quarter recalled and no transfer; the same two sentences placed beside up-stroke and down-stroke drawings raised transfer sharply.
- **Brake-system diagram:** identical words and graphic; labels beside each part beat labels at the bottom by more than a full standard deviation.
- **Cold-virus slide:** a striking "fun fact" appended to the six-step explanation lowered scores on how the virus works while being remembered itself.
