# Chapter 06: Images, Icons, Backgrounds & Effects

## Core Idea
A picture is worth a thousand words only if you know how to search, choose and place it; otherwise it is decoration. Every visual element must answer a function tied to a concept written on the slide or said aloud — images and icons are not fillers for empty space (fear of white space is the root error). Once an image earns its place, give it room: half the slide, the full slide, or bleeding to the edge, in standard proportions, at sufficient resolution, consistent in framing and colour with every other image in the deck. Text placed over images must stay readable, which is a contrast problem solved with overlays, gradients, blur, darkening or a glass panel. Icons come from one set with one stroke, recoloured to the palette, one per concept. The background is the lowest layer but a protagonist: it serves legibility, sets the rhythm of the deck, and breaks that rhythm when a message must hit. Effects — shadows, 3D, glass, textures — are legitimate only when they are subtle, consistent across the deck and functional (separating a layer, directing focus).

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The four functions of a visual**: *evocative images* (large, half slide or more, reinforce a concept written or spoken, immerse and move the audience); *anchor icons* (simple symbols that guide the eye through the points of a list); *descriptive images* (show the reality that was only in your head — the product, one or more views, galleries); *explanatory images* (screenshots of apps, sites, software, diagrams that show specific parts). The function decides position and treatment.
  When to use: reviewing a deck — for each image/icon, name its function; if none, delete it.
- **Five image layouts**: (1) multiple images (sets, product views, mood) in similar proportions, sizes may vary; (2) details — zoom on product parts, or darken the photo except the useful areas; (3) immersion — full-screen, flawless quality, few words, dark transparent layer between photo and text; (4) accompaniment — vertical image on the left or right, never on top or bottom; crop a horizontal photo to the useful part or reduce text; (5) brand identity — masks, text revealing the image, logo or topic silhouettes.
  When to use: picking the layout once the function is known.
- **Three goals of a background**: legibility (contrast with what sits on it); accompany the narrative flow by creating rhythm (a repeated pattern lets the audience focus on content); reinforce a message by breaking the rhythm.
  When to use: designing the background system for the deck.
- **Eight background ideas**: (1) off-white / non-pure black for content-heavy slides; (2) solid colour alternated with white/black slides to mark topic changes or key content (monochrome tints via Shadowlord); (3) gradients as a glow/light on black that moves between slides to guide focus (mesh gradient tools); (4) patterns never directly behind text — as a frame with a solid shape on top; (5) textures (e.g. wall) for a natural, human, warm feel, recolourable; (6) photo, for identification, made readable by choice, black transparent rectangle or reduced brightness; (7) duotone (Spotify-style) in brand colours, less realistic but coherent; (8) intentionally blurred image — creates curiosity (blurred, then sharp on the next slide) or offers nuance.
  When to use: when a section or cover needs a background other than plain light.
- **Shadow rules**: (1) light, never 100% opacity; (2) blur grows with shadow distance and object size; (3) always account for object and background colour. Aesthetic use: a repeated signature (under all images, under list cards). Functional use: the card being discussed "lights up" with a shadow or colour while others stay off.
  When to use: any time a shadow is applied — never accept the preset.

## Rules & Numbers
**Size, crop, resolution**
- Give an image at least half the slide; full slide; or one large image with a white border around. One quality image beats many small ones.
- Never narrow strips at the side "because an image is needed", never images wedged into leftover gaps, never absurd shapes/proportions. If the image matters little, remove it.
- Standard proportions (4:3, 3:2) and the same proportion for every image in a set or grid (e.g. crop all to 2:3).
- Bring the image to the slide edge: same image, completely different impact.
- If an image stays in the same position over consecutive slides, align it exactly (a jump of a few pixels is visible and annoying).
- Resolution: the slide is 1920×1080 px; a 640×426 image cannot fill it (about double the size is needed). Google Images thumbnails are often smaller than declared.
- Never distort: keep "constrain proportions" on and resize with Shift.
- Build the layout around the subject (subject on the right → content on the left), or pick an image that fits the layout, so the subject never ends under the text.
- Rounded corners on images: moderate (too round looks childish); equal margins on all sides around a framed image.
- Physical objects (a flyer) shown smaller so the format is understood.

**Consistency and filters**
- All images coherent in framing (e.g. nobody looking at the camera), subject (young/old, playful/serious) and above all colour.
- Homogenise with one treatment: all black-and-white; B&W plus a colour filter; duotone in palette colours; recolour with theme colours.
- When photos are the focus, sample text/graphic colours from them, or pick complementary colours.
- Text over brightly coloured images: avoid; make the image B&W/lightened/duotone and, only if the narrative needs it, show it in colour on the next slide.
- Team slide: name, role, photo per person; photos coherent in pose and colour (ask for homogeneous photos or unify with filters); photos can sit in shapes built with merge-shape operations.
- Photo portfolio: clean slides, max 2 photos per slide, white or black background only, open each project with the client's logo.
- Mockups: same shadows and style across the deck.

**Text on photos (legibility)**
- Place text in a blurred or uniform zone of the image; white text on dark images.
- Black rectangle in transparency over the full image.
- Gradient overlay: full-slide rectangle, black at 0% transparency where the text is → 100% transparency where there's no content (or brand colour instead of black).
- Coloured veil: full-slide rectangle with a 4-colour gradient sampled from the slide and lightened, diagonal, opacity 85–90%, over a photo with exposure and saturation reduced and contrast slightly raised.
- Darken: brightness −40% for white text on a portrait; −10% under glass panels.
- Blur: sharpness to −100% for cover backgrounds, plus reduced brightness.
- Radial vignette: radial gradient from the centre, two black stops, the first at 100% transparency — focus on a detail.
- Text shadow only on large text over a compromising photo, light and very diffuse. A full white box covers too much; a flat semi-transparent box looks dated — glass is the modern option.

**Watermarks and sources**
- Never images with watermarks: probably illegal, certainly unprofessional, and they pull attention from the image's concept.
- Free: Unsplash, Pexels, Burst; paid: Shutterstock; company archive; a commissioned photographer if there's budget. Avoid Google Images (copyright).
- Search specifically: several keywords, several portals (generic images are used by everyone); avoid stock clichés (the light bulb) — find a different version of the symbol. In free libraries (e.g. Canva) don't take the first result; use suggestions from the same set for coherent variants.
- Abstract moving textures: Pexels video section with abstract terms related to the topic; background videos with almost imperceptible movement immerse without disturbing.

**Screenshots and charts**
- Best: recreate the chart in the presentation software in the deck's style.
- Otherwise: screenshot with a margin around the content (not tight), change the picture shape to a rounded rectangle, place a larger rounded rectangle behind in light grey or the accent colour, optional shadow.
- Explanatory images: highlight details with animated zooms, geometric shapes circling the area, or text callouts linked by a line.

**Icons**
- One library, one style: same stroke weight, same fill logic (never mix outline and filled, monochrome and multicolour). Options: Google Icons (~2,500, adjustable fill/stroke, PNG or SVG), Icons8, outline sets with selectable stroke, size, cap style and colour exported as SVG; or build icons from basic shapes and the pen tool.
- Every icon tied to a textual concept: one icon per bullet point; no "free" icons; never icons alone without text.
- Icons must not be too small relative to their text; place them on circles or squares in palette colours when needed, or in white rounded boxes with a soft shadow.
- If you can't find icons, split the content over more slides or use images.
- Custom icons may modify a standard one (a bar across a communication icon = "lack of communication").
- Logos: not centred on every slide; full logo only on the cover, elsewhere only the small pictogram; footer smaller than the content, no solid band.

**Recolouring**
- SVG: PowerPoint recolours it natively. Keynote cannot: drag the SVG into a free online SVG recolourer, replace the detected colours with the palette HEX, export PNG at original size (or SVG).
- Monochrome PNG in PowerPoint: black → white with brightness +100%; white → black with brightness −100%.
- Google Slides add-on icons: colour them before inserting.

**Backgrounds**
- Most slides: light solid colour. Image backgrounds only where they enhance the text. A different colour for cover, break slides and back cover.
- Never the same background image repeated on every slide: heavy, hurts legibility, flattens the deck.
- Judge backgrounds as a set: Keynote light table, PowerPoint Slide Sorter, Google Slides grid view.
- Patterns only as frames with a solid shape on top; build patterns from basic shapes (e.g. square + triangles rotated 90° evoking tiles).

**Shadows (numbers)**
- White cards on white: opacity 10–20%.
- Blur proportional to distance: blur 20 at a contained distance; double the distance → roughly double+ the blur (e.g. 50), otherwise the shadow looks hard.
- Cards in a mid tone of the main colour: raise opacity slightly; cards in the main colour: higher still. Dark background: black shadow at nearly double, ~40%. Light background in the main colour with a blue shadow: ~44%. "Negative": white cards on very dark background with a main-colour shadow, higher opacity.
- Card shadow in PowerPoint: offset downward, transparency 85–90%, increased blur; one tested setting: transparency 90%, size 100%, blur 48 (8 is too sharp).
- Glass panels: centred shadow, transparency ~60–74% with blur; much more transparent on light backgrounds.
- The lighter the background, the lighter the shadow. One standard shadow per template.

**3D, glass, textures, masks**
- PowerPoint supports 3D models: Insert > 3D Models (free stock library) or a custom-modelled object, animated with Morph for rotations between slides; heavy for the computer.
- Glass (glassmorphism): blur + opacity, sometimes shadow and border; use sparingly and consistently, above complex images. Skip the distortion of "liquid glass" — it hurts legibility.
- Text masks: a word cut out of a slide-sized rectangle (Merge Shapes > Combine/Subtract) with a video or image underneath; pair a wide low font with a tall narrow one for hierarchy.
- Effects (outline, neon) applied to all titles or none; outline works only on very large text; neon with reduced intensity.

## Procedures
**Glass panel (PowerPoint)**
1. Design > Format Background > Picture fill; Corrections > Sharpness −100%; brightness −10% if panels must read darker with white text.
2. Insert the same image as a normal picture, 16:9, edges matching the background exactly; send backward.
3. Add a rounded rectangle, Fill: "Slide background fill", no line — it always shows the right blurred portion, even when moved.
4. Light text on light image: rectangle with a black → transparent black gradient at 0°, placed above the sharp image via the Selection Pane.
5. Border: gradient line white → transparent white (~78%), angle 45°. Shadow: centred, transparency ~60–74%. Slightly darkening the sharp image makes panels stand out.

**Readable cover in 3 steps**: full-slide image → Picture Format > Picture > lower sharpness until blurred → lower brightness → large title.

**Quick functional slides**: extract short phrases from the speech → one coherent, quality image per phrase as full background → black transparent rectangle → phrase in a readable font.

**Colour filter where none exists (Keynote)**: saturation 0 on the photo, a coloured shape on top at reduced opacity. PowerPoint/Google Slides: Recolour.

**Product detail with Morph**: slide 1 product image → slide 2 enlarged image → circle on one detail → text box in free space linked by a connector → Morph; one detail per slide.

**Partner/logo slide**: logos in identical-proportion rectangles or a hairline grid → all logos monochrome (with approval) → align and distribute.

## Common Mistakes
- Images as gap fillers or icons scattered in empty corners → remove, or tie each to a concept.
- Pixelated, stretched, inconsistent images → resolution check, constrained resize, one filter.
- Watermarked stock → licensed or free-library images.
- Default dark, hard shadows → light, soft, proportional shadows or none.
- Dark text on a dark photo, or text on a busy image → overlay, gradient, blur or glass.
- Two clashing styles on one slide (colour gradient background vs colour photo with shadow) → make the photo a secondary background element fused with the gradient.
- Black PNG logo on a dark background → brightness +100%.
- Mixed icon sets → one library, one stroke.
- Pattern directly behind text → pattern as frame.
- Charts pasted from the web → recreate in style, or frame the screenshot.

## Mental Models
- Start from "why": the function and message of the image decide where it goes and how big it is.
- White space is not a mistake to be filled.
- Movement and contrast attract the eye — anything visually loud must be the thing you want looked at.
- A shadow should say "this is above", never become the focus.
- Repeated elements make movement between slides perceptible; consistency makes a deck feel designed.

## Examples
- A travel agency list: slide split into three 640×1080 columns, destination photos as bullet points, box colour sampled from each photo, Morph enlarging one destination at a time.
- A space-suit gallery: all photos B&W with a red (then blue) filter, hung on lines with stars, rotated ±10°, black gradients top and bottom hiding slide changes.
- A pitch problem slide over a desaturated photo veiled by a palette gradient at 85–90% opacity.
- A cover with a blurred, darkened full-bleed photo and a frosted-glass title panel; a three-point list whose white boxes became glass boxes.
- An AI startup deck with a "glow" style derived from the product concept and a consistent illustration set, some animated.
- A destination cover: black slide, place name cut out of a rectangle as a mask with video behind.
