# Chapter 09: Presentations by Context

## Core Idea
The principles stay the same in every context: one message per slide, the voice leads, hierarchy, consistency, and simplified data. What changes is the constraints and the effort that is worth spending. School and university work starts from the teacher's or institution's rules (format, slide cap, mandatory colours, required contents). Exams (thesis, internship report) reward a clear A-to-B narrative, a few strong numbers and visible emotion. Corporate work starts by choosing the right output (a document to send vs slides to present), then applies the brand template, keeps tables and charts minimal, and saves "wow" effort for pitches and events. Pitch decks, product launches and talks need the most design investment, because persuasion and memorability are the point. Portfolios must stay neutral so the work speaks. Training and webinars need a stable template, a visible structure and good technical setup. In every context, first ask: who decides, what will they do afterwards, and where and how will the file be shown?

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Constraint-first briefing**: before designing, ask the commissioner (teacher, university, client, organizer) for format, slide cap, mandatory template/colours, required contents and the projecting PC's software. If a teacher wants "everything written down", the deliverable is a document to send, not a presentation to deliver.
  When to use: any school, academic or client deck.
- **Effort-to-stakes ratio**: spectacular slides (custom illustrations, Morph sequences, loops) only for pitches, launches, events and talks. Internal meetings, weekly updates and briefings get standard layouts, clean alignment and coherent visuals.
  When to use: scoping time for any deck.
- **Problem → alternative → proof → call to action** (persuasive arc): state the problem, present the alternative/solution (optionally with a personal story), show proof (a real case), close with the CTA, and echo the opening at the end (circular close).
  When to use: thesis defences, pitches, service/business presentations.
- **Value before price**: in offers and sponsorship decks, tell what is included category by category and show the price last. The more value is built first, the more a (high) price is justified.
  When to use: offer, pricing, sponsorship and partnership slides.
- **Inspire + educate**: a talk can combine a personal journey (inspire) with a practical part involving the audience (educate). "There is no inspiration without education."
  When to use: talks, keynotes, festival or TEDx-style speeches.

## Rules & Numbers
**School (student presentations)**
- Teacher rules override everything. A slide cap means "be concise" or "be graded evenly".
- Software: Canva or Google Slides (free, online, enough for basic work, needs internet). PowerPoint (free for students, complete but chaotic; what takes 1 h in Canva can take 3 h in PowerPoint). Keynote (Apple only; don't design on an iPhone). Genially for interactive decks with buttons and embedded sites. Check what the school PC has.
- Theme vs blank: a theme saves work, but don't take the first result (classmates will use it too). A ready-made template only works if used almost unchanged: don't lengthen its paragraphs, shrink its titles, move elements, replace the hero image at random or overturn the palette.
- Structure like an essay: introduction, body, conclusion. Be as schematic as possible.
- Cover: title, optional subtitle, every group member's full name, class, subject, school name or logo. Index: first-level chapters only; hyperlinks to sections if the file is sent.
- About 20–30 words per slide to summarize its concept. Bullets can run horizontally. Split long paragraphs into columns with space between them and bold keywords.
- Text over an image only with strong contrast; otherwise split the slide (text left, image right or vice versa). Use quality images (not pixelated) and diagrams consistent in colours and fonts (not lifted from image search).
- Use 16:9, never 4:3. No black on grey. Avoid thick default outlines.
- Close with "Thank you", a reflective question or a quote. Animations: none for decoration; a fade to bring in one point at a time is fine.
- Export: a link/account for online tools, otherwise PDF (opens everywhere, loses animations).
- Take the style from the subject: the colours of a region's flag (desaturated) plus a signature element (sea blue) as the main colour; a book's cover typography and texture; an artwork's colours for the slide; a black background and a heavy sans in white caps for space topics.

**Teachers' lecture slides**
- Slide ≠ handout. If everything is on the slide, students must choose between listening, reading and taking notes. Give the eye entry points: big titles, bold, italics, space.
- 16:9 (converting from 4:3 is a chance to update the content). Name small in the footer, never as a watermark. Logo big only on the cover.
- Avoid low-contrast pairs (yellow/white, yellow/light blue, blue/red, purple/green, light/dark green). Use an off-white background, one accent colour and very dark grey text. No Comic Sans; use Inter or Roboto.
- Minimum projected body text on 1920×1080: 32 pt, so the back row can read it (see ch04 type scale).
- The lecture-vs-home-study dilemma: split the same content over more slides (one idea at a time) and print several slides per sheet. If slides must stay dense, at least write them properly (punctuation, line breaks) and share them.
- Don't paste screenshots of other decks (it breaks the style, distracts, raises copyright issues and signals laziness). Use only humour that fits the current audience.

**Thesis defence**
- Content: only what takes the listener from A to B. Get help from an outside-but-informed reader (supervisor, co-supervisor, a peer).
- Slide count: the common rule is about 15 slides per 10 min, but the number doesn't matter (33 slides for 20 min worked). Reinforcing slides are quick; deep-dive slides are slow. Respect any university cap.
- Backgrounds show structure: colour for the cover and section dividers, white for content, images or graphics for low-content slides.
- Colours: from the institution if imposed; otherwise search the topic and extract colours from images (e.g. Adobe Color). Fonts: a clean sans never fails (Inter, IBM Plex Sans, Roboto).
- Charts: never paste them from the thesis or the web. Simplify to focus on one figure; the full data is in the thesis. Show a percentage as a coloured rectangle filling 81% of the slide before the text appears.
- One animation type throughout (fade). "On click" only for what you cite.
- Cover: mirror the thesis cover (large title, smaller subtitle, supervisors, date small bottom-right, logos bottom-left).
- Close with future developments. Bring a PDF backup, a presenter (or phone) and speaker notes. Rehearse alone, then with people, then in the room. Dress appropriately but comfortably.

**Internship/work-experience report (PCTO-style school exam)**
- Ask teachers for the required contents and the slide count.
- Structure: overview (period, total hours, place, company) → company (vision, mission, employees, activities, org chart with your position) → tasks (3–4 at most) → skills applied and acquired (few, no endless lists) → pros and cons → final reflection and orientation (university or work). With several experiences, add an index ordered oldest to newest (company/logo, period, sector) and repeat the block per experience.
- Overview slide: 4 boxes, each with a small coloured label ("Period") and the value large below it (not bold).
- Sections: break slides for company / experience / conclusions.
- Avoid bullets: each task becomes a short sentence with an icon above it, in a box/card.
- Style from the company's materials (site, social, letterhead, brochure): colours, fonts, logo, shapes, text handling. Use neutral sans fonts (Roboto, Be Vietnam, Poppins, Inter, IBM Plex Sans, DM Sans). Pair the same font in bold and regular, or two very different fonts, never two similar ones. Use a monochrome palette: accent plus one darker and one lighter variant.
- Sizes used in a Canva build: labels about 20 pt, body 26 pt (within the 24–32 body range, 26 suited that layout better than 30). Text beside a vertical photo reduced to about 56 pt with tight leading. See ch04 type scale.
- The title isn't always the biggest element: "Mission" is a label, and the mission sentence goes large.
- A school logo with a white background goes inside a white band at the bottom. Use gradients only on the cover and dividers. Images: few, all the same aspect ratio, in an evenly spaced grid.
- Close with a personal reflection rather than a quote. Share the online deck as a view-only link saved offline.

**University paper / scientific / academic**
- A standard, serious style is fine; you stand out on details: equal margins on all four sides, precise alignment, one geometric font with clean terminals (not rounded), the same font on every slide.
- Three-level hierarchy: title (1); subtitle in the same colour but bolder (2); smaller paragraph (3). Keywords in the main colour.
- Very dark blue text instead of black, white content slides, the main colour on covers and (less of it) on break slides.
- Redraw external diagrams in the deck's style, but only for very important presentations (it takes a long time). Place them on a very light grey rectangle and use the same corner radius for images and diagrams. Captions small and close to the image.
- Split a 3-point list into 4 slides: one preview with small labels, then each point highlighted alone.
- Scientific decks (formulas, equations, diagrams) shouldn't be made "catchy". Make them easier to understand: (1) remove what is superfluous; there is always something; (2) change the font, which alone transforms the style; (3) create order by grouping elements with hairlines, very light background panels, or very light shadows for screenshots on white. Never add decoration.
- Technical/maths slides: one alignment (left), one recognizable text style per role (label, title, formula). Replace an illegible coloured highlight with a coloured underline, and use the same colour to link text and diagram (e.g. radius and circumference).
- Comparison slide: criteria listed once on the left, separated by horizontal lines; group-1 and group-2 values in adjacent columns; coloured circles for changes; a homogeneous dark background; one accent colour.

**Corporate / internal meetings**
- First choose the output: a document to email (full) or slides to support the voice (empty). If colleagues object to the lack of text: "they're slides, not documents".
- Ask for the company template first; it follows the brand and may contain near-ready slides. Without one, find the brand colours and fonts (logo manual).
- Structure with the 5 slide types: cover, break, focus (key number/takeaway), ordinary content (white, the majority), back cover.
- Don't fill slides (anxiety, wanting to look prepared, the wrong medium). One key message per slide; reveal bullets one at a time or split them across slides; put the rest in notes.
- Turn vertical bullets into horizontal rows of rectangles with icons. Turn lists of numbers into big numbers with a description and an icon. Use custom, coherent icon sets for service lists.
- Tables and charts: one per slide. Strip any data the message doesn't need. Minimal style (no vertical lines or outline, thin lines, a highlighted header). Highlight the cited figure, or drop the table and show just the number.
- No SmartArt (it looks like 2000s PowerPoint, gets picked at random and its complex shapes make text hard to manage); rebuild ideas found on Dribbble instead. No "funny" animations: a simple, very fast fade "never fails". Corporate animation bans should become a minimal allowed set (fade on click for focus).
- Internal meetings and recurring reports need care, not fireworks: align properly, keep visuals coherent, use standard layouts, and make frequently updated objects (Gantt charts) easy to edit. Many meetings could be an email; slides are not a prerequisite for looking prepared.
- PowerPoint is the corporate lingua franca (a Canva deck can look out of place at a consulting firm). Link Excel charts so the data updates, and deliver a .potx template.
- "Put everything on one slide, we must be concise" is a contradiction: synthesis means selecting.

**Pitch deck (startup / investors)**
- Slides are necessary; questions come at the end. A short deck (about 13 slides) can skip page numbers. Consider opening with a strong question, figure or image and placing the cover after it.
- *Problem slide:* the word "problem" appears once (as a small label or within the title). A short, large central title with the individual problems arranged around it, each marked with an X. Use a black-and-white photo under a brand-colour gradient overlay so the image matches the palette.
- *Competitor slide:* same style as the rest of the deck (a "pasted-in" table looks unprofessional to investors). A table with horizontal lines only (no fills, verticals or outer border), the line under the header thicker, cell margins at 0, text 24 pt centred vertically, and a slightly bigger accent-coloured header; the slide title stays at 40–48 pt, as on any table slide (see ch04 type scale). Remove uninformative columns (e.g. an "importance" column that says "high" everywhere). Put logos in identical white rounded boxes. Use brand colour + tick for "has it" and grey + X for "doesn't"; never traffic-light green/red. Reveal your differentiators afterwards (click/Morph) inside a highlight box (3 pt outline). Quick variant: a table with white borders, a feature column at 28 pt on very light grey, brand names/logos in the header with yours the most visible, and two cell colours for present/absent.
- *Team slide:* name, role and photo per member. Photos in geometric shapes (combined shapes) that are coherent: same gaze direction, same colour treatment. Ask for uniform photos or apply a common filter.
- *Partner slide:* logos in equal rectangles or a hairline grid, all monochrome (with approval).
- *Offer/pricing/sponsorship slide:* never a pasted Excel table. Slide 1: a small grey "Offer" label, a large bold main sentence, a grey explanatory paragraph, and the packages previewed on the right. Then one slide per category (packages top-right, item titles bold, details smaller between hairlines, accent ticks per plan). Last, the price slide (plans side by side, large prices, explicit discount, e.g. "−50%, €X if confirmed by <date>", instead of an ambiguous yellow highlight). Avoid all caps, alternating heavy row fills and off-brand colours. When presented, use more slides (old price struck through, then the new one); emailed, keep it on one legible slide.
- Tech pitches: derive a graphic motif from the product concept (glows for an AI camera) and build an illustration set from it. Use slow loops behind big-sentence slides only.

**Product launch**
- Steve Jobs–style simplicity: little text, one concept per slide, the voice leads. The memorability comes from content work, not effects.
- Translate the product's website sections into slide sequences (each state = one slide): a feature list with a sliding indicator (equal-width pills, a separate white indicator rectangle, Morph 1 s); a colour-variant selector that updates on each slide around a rotating product composition; a gallery with a larger centre image and side images at −60 brightness (Morph 1 s); three stacked rounded cards for three features with a progress indicator; rounded rectangles entering in sequence (Morph 0.5 s), built from the final composition backwards.
- Take the style from the product's interface, packaging or logo: a near-black background, rounded cards with a small radius ("too rounded looks childish"), and the product as a PNG breaking out of the card. For destination or event launches, a video seen through a knocked-out title.

**Portfolio**
- A portfolio is a presentation for a company. Keep the container neutral; the projects must speak.
- Photography: (1) clean slides, no decoration; (2) open each project with the client's logo and choose shots suited to it; (3) vary layouts, but at most 2 photos per slide; (4) explain your method (how you create, what you offer, your style); (5) white or black background, never coloured (colour alters the photos). Link out to more images instead of bloating the file.
- Designer portfolio: recurring layouts (cover; about the designer; per project: title, intro, materials) and a clickable index so a busy recruiter jumps to relevant projects. The emailed PDF must explain itself: slightly more text with small headings. Project intro slide: brand name and info (goal, strategy) on the left, an evocative image on the right. Use consistent mockups (same shadows and style) everywhere. Nothing touches the slide edge. Highlight with bold, not CAPS. Avoid overly narrow columns. Cover: name, role sought, small photo, contacts and a "Portfolio" label.

**Training / workshops**
- Use your own stable template (little text, abstract illustrations, images). Rebuilding slides each time wastes energy and weakens your brand. If the organizer imposes an unsuitable template, ask to use only the brand colours.
- Keep slides and documents separate: don't project a doc and then email it. Deliver valuable training while people take notes.
- Use the fixed 5-slide pattern. The cover can stay visible before the start, or introduce yourself before sharing the screen. The content frame (deck title, name, year, page number) helps with photos and Q&A. Break slides use at most 2 levels. Keep the background scheme rigid for 2–3 h sessions.
- 3–4 h sessions are improvised from an outline. An iPad + Keynote + stylus lets you write on slides during the session.
- Workshop activity: a messy real slide, paper and pen, "what shape best expresses this?", and after a few minutes non-designers produce several valid redesigns.

**Talks / TEDx-style / keynotes**
- For 10–20 min, leave nothing to chance: set the goal (inspire/educate), analyse the audience, write the script word for word, validate it, mark the slide moments, then design. Expect about 4–5 h.
- Open with show-of-hands questions to build rapport (especially as the last speaker of the morning). Make people smile; they laugh with you, not at you. Avoid special effects, keep slides simple and on-brand.
- While telling your own story, hold one slide so attention stays on you, then raise the tempo with slide changes synced to the voice. Use a sentence slide to break the flow. Image arrangement can carry meaning (old slides stacked like a staircase = growth). Use bridge sentences between parts.
- Present standing, with a remote; use the stage and your body.

**Webinars / online**
- The setting is part of the experience; dark video and poor audio make a bad impression. Use a key light on the face (an LED panel on one side), an accent/background light (can be in the brand colour), a decent microphone (about €100 is enough) and a mirrorless camera (or the webcam, if the lighting is good).
- Control slides from a second device (an iPad running Keynote). Share the audience window, not the whole screen, and keep presenter view with notes for yourself.

## Procedures
**A. Context triage (any deck)**
1. Who commissions it and what rules apply (template, cap, format, contents)?
2. Will it be presented in person, sent, or both? If both, plan two versions.
3. What are the stakes? Choose a standard layout or a custom design.
4. What runs it on the day (software, version, own device)? Plan the PDF/link backup.

**B. Internship report deck in Canva**
1. Write all the contents in a document first.
2. Build a style test slide: fonts (Text > Font > Sans Serif), monochrome palette (duplicate the accent rectangle, then move the picker darker and lighter), recurring shapes.
3. Turn on File > Show margins and align to them (Canva aligns the text box, not the text).
4. Build components (grouped objects) and reuse them; copy a finished experience's slides for the next one.
5. Icons: download Google Icons as SVG/PNG (or use emoji). Org chart: boxes joined by lines, with your own box highlighted.
6. Share > Anyone with the link > Can view; keep the link offline.

**C. Rebuild a pitch competitor table (PowerPoint)**
1. Download the original as .pptx and keep the background and brand graphics.
2. Insert the table: no fill, no borders, then re-enable horizontal lines only; set cell margins to None; distribute the columns.
3. Put logos in equal rounded boxes (Align > Distribute horizontally). Build the ticks/X marks as circle + icon and convert them to shapes.
4. Duplicate the slide: the differentiator boxes are at 100% transparency on the first copy and visible on the second; apply Morph plus a 0.5 s fade, with no bounce.

## Common Mistakes
- Ignoring the commissioner's rules → ask first; they define the deliverable.
- Using the same effort for a weekly update and an investor pitch → scale effort to stakes.
- A thesis deck that copies charts from the thesis → simplify to the one figure that proves the point.
- A pitch table that looks pasted in → rebuild it in the deck's style, with brand vs grey for yes/no.
- Price shown before value → value by category first, price last.
- A coloured background in a photo portfolio → white or black only.
- Lecture slides used as handouts → split into one idea per slide and print several per sheet, or provide a separate document.
- A dark, badly lit webinar → front light, accent light, a dedicated mic, presenter window plus a shared audience window.
- Endless lists of skills and tasks in exam decks → 3–4 tasks, a few skills, icons plus short sentences.
- Rainbow partner-logo walls → equal cells, monochrome logos.

## Mental Models
- **Same principles, different constraints:** context changes the rules of the game (who decides, how it's read), not the grammar of slides.
- **The jury, investor or client decides with emotion and justifies with data:** give them a feeling plus one decisive number.
- **School is a gym:** every assigned presentation is practice for design and public speaking.
- **Care is already a differentiator:** in corporate settings, alignment and coherence beat effects.

## Examples
- **School progression over 5 years:** a 4:3 all-bullets deck (weak) evolved into a final project for an artisans' e-commerce, where images zoom while the speaker talks, a product backstage is shown, and the deck closes on future developments (strong).
- **Startup problem slide:** a duplicated "problem" word and four buried issues became one central title with four X-marked problems around it, over a desaturated photo with a brand gradient.
- **Event sponsorship offer:** one slide with a paragraph and an Excel table became an intro slide, then one slide per category, then a price slide with an explicit early-confirmation discount.
- **Software-house annual showcase (30 min, international clients):** a visible grid derived from the logo with coloured rectangles moving across it, a "zen" logo wall, and feature text on the left with screenshots on the right, using zoom and blur to guide focus.
- **AI company event:** company growth from 4 to 50+ people animated with the logo's symbols; illustrations for spoken hypotheses; key numbers built from simple shapes.
- **Art lesson at school:** an artist slide with a portrait seen through a mask cut into a full-slide rectangle filled with a colour sampled from the painting, the image darkened −40%, and Inter + Lora typography.
