# Chapter 11: Templates, Brand, AI and Resources

## Core Idea
Templates, brand kits, AI generators and asset libraries are accelerators, never substitutes for thinking. A ready-made template is balanced for one specific amount of text, one image and one palette: it pays off only when used almost as designed (little time, limited design skills, low-stakes presentation), and it collapses as soon as paragraphs get longer, titles smaller or elements move. A custom corporate template is the opposite tool: a master with guides, theme fonts, theme colors, a light footer, enough layouts for real content and written usage rules, delivered as a .potx so every user produces coherent slides without design effort. Brand comes first — ask for the template and guidelines before starting; if none exist, sample colors and fonts from the logo, website, packaging. AI is useful at the edges of the process (a first draft when you have no idea where to start, a visual metaphor for a concept, balancing list lengths, validating a script, extracting data from an image) and harmful at the core: generated decks are bullet lists in different arrangements and editing them costs more time than designing. Resources (photos, icons, fonts, colors, textures, 3D) must be licensed, high-resolution, chosen for coherence with the deck — not the first result, which everybody else picks too.

## Frameworks Introduced
**When to use a ready-made template (three cases)**: little time (just drop content into finished graphics); limited design skills (choose among many variants of the same layout); presentation not very important. Otherwise design from scratch or on a custom brand template.
When to use: at project start, deciding between template, brand template and blank slide.

**Five rules for using a template well**: (1) choose by category/goal, not by beauty (a data deck needs a data template, not a portfolio one); (2) you cannot write as much as you want — it is harmonious for that number of lines and that size; (3) don't touch graphic elements without asking why the designer put them there; (4) mind the images — templates look good because their photos are good; (5) don't keep the look 100 other decks have — customize colors and fonts.
When to use: whenever a client, student or colleague starts from a Canva/Slidesgo/PowerPoint template.

**Three corporate-style situations**: full template with layouts (rigid, minimal creative effort, little expressiveness); basic template with only colors and fonts (freedom within brand); nothing (find brand colors and fonts, often in the logo manual, to stay coherent and save hours).
When to use: first conversation with any company deck request.

**Template anatomy**: template = set of layouts (cover, break slide, text+image, text only, image only, data, quote, back cover…) inside a theme (colors + fonts). Master commands layouts, layout commands the slide.
When to use: planning and building any brand template.

**Five slide types for corporate decks**: cover (big title, background image separated from text or solid color); break slide (different background from cover, numbered or titled chapters); special content (key number or takeaway, different background/graphics); ordinary content (white, the majority, easy to read); back cover (contacts, call to action or the main idea to remember).
When to use: minimum layout set of a corporate template.

**Two template goals**: easy for anyone who touches the file, and stylistically harmonious output.
When to use: testing every layout before delivery.

**AI by process stage**: draft generator (outline/first deck) → only for unblocking; image model → mood/style research, illustrations, abstract concepts; chatbot → visual metaphors, colors and animations with reasons, script validation, text tightening; in-app assistants (Copilot, Designer, AI rewrite) → text edits, never final layouts.
When to use: deciding where an AI tool can save time without flattening the deck.

## Rules & Numbers
Ready-made templates
- Don't lengthen paragraphs, don't shrink titles, don't move elements randomly, don't swap the hero image for a random one, don't overturn the palette (legibility breaks). Fixing an "exploded" template often takes longer than starting blank.
- Need more text? In order: reduce size AND narrow the box (long lines are unreadable) → move the image to gain space → delete the image. First ask whether this is a document to send (then it may be denser) or a talk (then cut).
- Three temptations to resist: keeping off-topic elements, moving elements at random, filling space with free stock graphics.
- Graphic elements exist only to support text (icon next to text, never alone), separate content (lines, shapes) or guide the eye (arrows, icons). White space is not an error.
- Images: high quality (template library, Unsplash, Pexels); same type as the original (don't drop a search-engine diagram into a frame designed for a photo — give it its own slide); place the subject inside the frame.
- Recolor by transposing the template's three roles (background white, text black, accent): e.g. white → very light beige, black → brown or very dark green, accent → a green of similar luminance; light/dark may be inverted.
- Fonts: linear and legible; if you change them, change all consistently (never swap title/body fonts between slides).
- Don't pick the first theme in the gallery (classmates/competitors will have it). For school or important work, building a simple own style from a blank slide is often faster than bending a template.
- In Canva: don't export to .pptx (text shifts, transitions change, fonts substitute). If PowerPoint is the deliverable, design in PowerPoint; if Canva, share a view-only link (save the link elsewhere so you don't depend on login on the day).

Custom corporate template
- Start from the simplest blank theme.
- Guides in the master (not in layouts): margins plus center vertical/horizontal; they appear on all layouts.
- Theme fonts in the master: e.g. Aptos Semibold titles / Aptos Regular body (Aptos replaced Calibri as Microsoft default) — or the brand fonts.
- Theme colors: name the palette, replace Text/Background, Accent 1–6 and hyperlink colors. Max ~4 meaningful colors (the slots total 12). Users then pick from the theme with all tints, never from "recent colors"; a theme color changed once updates hundreds of objects, charts and image recolors.
- Monochrome tint system from the brand color: 0% = accent; 90% darker = text, break slides, cover, back cover; 90% lighter = main background or text on dark; add 20/40/60/80% steps for charts and illustrations. Dark slide background = brand color with lowered HSB brightness.
- Footer: remove the date; footer text centered or bottom-left, page number right (bold, accent color); text-box internal margins 0, top-aligned; set through Insert > Header & Footer > Apply to all (changing it there changes every slide).
- Logo in the master only if it works on both light and dark; otherwise per layout, or two masters (light/dark). Full logo on cover and back cover only; small symbol in the footer.
- Layouts: duplicate a layout, move text, Insert Placeholder > Picture/Text. When users switch layouts, content re-flows automatically.
- Indent levels of the body placeholder = paragraph styles, sized from ch04 type scale: format level 1 (e.g. label-like heading: no bullet, Semibold 20 pt, accent color, line spacing 1.15, space after 12 pt), level 2 (body: smaller, Light, grey, no space before), and more. Users apply with Tab/Increase indent; editing the master restyles 100+ slides at once. Remove bullets and drag both ruler indent markers left for non-bullet styles. Rename levels ("style 1 title", "style 2 body").
- Placeholders: shape fits text, top-aligned, internal margins 0 (or ≈11 pt with a light fill when used as a card).
- Save as .potx: opening it creates a new .pptx, so the template is never overwritten.
- Include extras ready to copy (tables, charts, shapes, connectors) and a short usage-guidelines slide/page.
- Test every layout with real content; template building is the slowest part of the work for this reason.
- PowerPoint limits to plan around: one placeholder can't hold two text styles (use two placeholders or indent levels); tables/charts placed in the master become non-editable background; chart templates are saved on the computer, not in the file (deliver separately with install instructions); on Mac table styles, shape styles and theme fonts can't be fully saved; no Word-style paragraph styles.
- A template is useless if: the footer is heavy (e.g. a wide photo strip across the bottom); it offers only cover + one specific slide + title-and-text (people will then only write text); titles are white on white with black shadow.
- Google Slides template in five steps: theme colors from the brand identity → footer (logo, recurring refs) → layouts with text/image placeholders (the hardest part) → ready extra elements → brief usage guidelines. Theme colors entered as hex without "#".

Brand
- Always ask first whether a company template exists; ask for brand guidelines.
- No guidelines: sample from the brand's site (browser extension such as CSS Peeper shows heading/body fonts and page colors; "Inspect element" reveals the font), logo, packaging, cover, or from the photos if the topic is generic (eyedropper, online image color picker; Google Slides has no eyedropper — screenshot and sample).
- Keep brand colors in the theme, not in custom/recent swatches.
- Don't overturn brand elements in a redesign (backgrounds, glows, colors stay); improve structure.
- Red brand: accent on neutrals, white text on red, red reserved in charts for negative data.
- Partner/sponsor logos: identical cells, monochrome (all white/all black) only with brands' approval.
- Clarify at kickoff the delivery software and format (pptx, link, PDF) and the PC that will project: design in the destination software. Keynote files must be exported and tested in PowerPoint; animations get converted or dropped across apps.
- Software fit: PowerPoint = corporate standard (Microsoft 365), Excel-linked charts, the most rigid/complete templates, powerful animation (morph, motion paths, triggers, loops); Keynote = Mac environments; Canva = very small companies and schools (showing up with Canva at a consulting firm may be frowned upon); Google Slides = free, online, limited (only fades are reliable); Figma Slides = for design teams already in Figma (auto layout, numeric corner radius, named theme colors, only Smart Animate). For schools and non-expert users PowerPoint is overkill: a one-hour Canva deck can take three hours in PowerPoint.

AI tools
- Generated decks (Gamma, Beautiful.ai, Tome): 10 slides in seconds, but mostly bullet lists rearranged, repetitive, "documents more than slides"; good only when you have no idea how to start and no ambitions. Once you see them you start re-imagining them and lose time. Refining a single slide with AI for a few minutes can approach the intended result.
- PowerPoint Designer proposes layouts that often overturn your work: ignore it. Copilot can generate slides or edit text from a prompt; review everything.
- Chatbot prompt for metaphors: "How would you visually build a slide expressing the concept of [sentence]?" — it gives ideas (e.g. excuses as the bars of a tightening cage, also usable as an animation) and can suggest colors and animations with reasons; the visual interpretation remains yours, which keeps decks from looking alike.
- Image generators: replace style research on Dribbble/Behance/Pinterest for illustrations and abstract concepts; add 16:9 and terms like "PowerPoint" or "slide deck" to the prompt (generic "presentation design" was misunderstood). The more precise the input, the better. Check copyright before reusing generated images.
- Useful chatbot chores: equalize bullet lengths (then edit), extract the data table from a chart image into Excel, validate a talk script before designing slides.
- AI works better on generic topics (much data) than on a specific startup; it still lacks visual synthesis tuned to the audience.

Resources
- Inspiration (search for content, not "slides"): Awwwards (sites of the day, Collections: transitions, cursors, slideshows), CSS Design Awards, Dribbble (Popular view; also "presentation design"), Behance, Pinterest (search e.g. "data viz", "timeline"; one board per project), app screens, dashboards, posters, packaging, product UIs; a daily-inspiration browser extension (fonts, palettes, brands); consulting-firm slide libraries for analytic content structure (restyle them). Archive finds by slide type (cover, charts, gallery) in a notes tool.
- Photos: Unsplash, Pexels (also its stock footage: search abstract terms for subtle moving textures), Burst, Shutterstock (paid), company archive, a commissioned photographer if budget allows, Canva's library. Never search-engine images (copyright, thumbnails smaller than declared) and never watermarks (likely illegal, unprofessional, distracting). Full-bleed on 1920×1080 needs ≥1920 px wide: a 640×426 image is far too small (about double is the minimum). Avoid clichés (light bulb).
- Icons: Google Fonts Icons / Material Symbols (adjustable weight and fill, SVG/PNG, recolorable), Icons8, Iconfinder and other add-ons in Google Slides (add the required credit), Noun Project for reference shapes, PowerPoint Insert > Icons (choose carefully; native ones are plain). One set, one style, one stroke weight. Recolor SVGs with an online "SVG recolor" tool when the app can't (Keynote).
- Fonts: Google Fonts and Fontshare (check licenses for commercial use). Pick families with many weights. Frequently used: Satoshi, General Sans, Inter, IBM Plex Sans, Roboto, DM Sans, Poppins, Be Vietnam, Epilogue, Archivo (incl. Semi Expanded), Funnel Sans, Aptos; display: Clash Display, Antonio (condensed), Bebas; serif accent: Playfair Display, Lora; mono: IBM Plex Mono. Avoid system defaults that date a deck (Calibri, Arial, Times New Roman) and Comic Sans.
- Color tools: Coolors (generator with lock/spacebar, generate methods, explore by theme, image picker, contrast checker, gradient palette, color picker with conversions), Adobe Color (extract from images), Picular (keyword → colors), Shades/Shadowlord (tints and shades of a hex), any contrast checker.
- Backgrounds: mesh-gradient generators (tech/AI covers, glows on black), Visiwig (patterns, used as a frame behind a solid shape, never directly behind text), Transparent Textures (warm, human textures, recolorable), Duotone by Shape Factory (Spotify-style brand duotones), PineTools (intentional blur).
- 3D: PowerPoint's free stock 3D model library (Insert > 3D Models; searchable in local language), animatable with Morph; custom-modeled objects for premium product launches. Insert text first, 3D last; files get heavy — test on the presenting PC for lag.
- Canva elements: crown = Pro, no crown = free; search in two languages; use "same set" suggestions for coherent variants (two different tape pieces from one set, not one duplicated); mockups via Edit image > Mockups; frames for masks.

## Procedures
**Build a corporate PowerPoint template**
1. View > Slide Master; start from the blank theme; delete unneeded layouts.
2. Right-click > Grid and Guides > add margin guides and center guides on the parent master.
3. Set title/body fonts on the master (Home).
4. Master > Colors > Customize Colors: name the palette; fill Text/Background, Accent 1–6, hyperlinks with the brand tints (generate with a shades tool).
5. Format footer and page-number placeholders (margins 0, top-aligned); remove date. Then Insert > Header & Footer > tick slide number and footer > Apply to All.
6. Format indent levels 1–5 as named text styles.
7. Build layouts for the five slide types plus text+image, data, quote, image-only; add picture/text placeholders; make a dark variant (Background Styles; title/footer colors).
8. Place logo (master if it works on both backgrounds, else per layout).
9. Add a guidelines slide (text limits, icon rules, image rules, palette use) and an extras slide (table, chart, shapes).
10. Test every layout with real content; File > Save as Template (.potx); deliver chart templates separately if used.

**Adapt a ready-made template**
1. Choose by goal/category.
2. Map your content to its layouts; if a slide needs much more text, split it or choose another layout.
3. Transpose its three color roles into your palette; swap fonts globally.
4. Replace images with equally good, same-type photos; keep subjects inside frames.
5. Delete off-topic elements; never add free graphics just to fill.

**Use AI without flattening the deck**
1. Write and validate the script yourself (AI can critique it).
2. Highlight where slides are needed.
3. For hard concepts, ask a chatbot for 3–5 visual metaphors; pick and sketch one.
4. Use an image model for mood/illustration (16:9, "slide deck"); verify rights.
5. Design layouts by hand; use AI only for text tightening and data extraction.

## Common Mistakes
- Picking the prettiest template instead of the right category → choose by goal.
- Pouring long text into a template → cut, split, or change layout.
- Default theme colors/fonts ("seen a million times") → brand or custom palette and fonts.
- Brand colors stored as recent colors → theme colors.
- Template with one content layout and a photo footer → several content layouts, light footer.
- Exporting Canva to pptx → design in the delivery tool.
- Trusting AI-generated decks as final → use as a draft at most.
- Watermarked or low-res stock → licensed, ≥1920 px for full bleed.
- Mixed icon sets/emoji styles → one set, one stroke.
- Logo large on every slide → cover/back cover only, symbol in footer.

## Mental Models
- A template is a balance: touch one weight and the scale tips.
- The software doesn't design; the head does — standard templates give dignified but interchangeable decks.
- A brand template is a product for non-designers: make the right thing the easy thing.
- The first search result is everyone's result.
- AI gives speed where thinking isn't needed and sameness where it is.

## Examples
- Convention template received for a client: wide photo footer, only three layouts, white titles on white with black shadow — rebuilt with a light footer and multiple content layouts.
- Company file without template after a training: master guides, Aptos Semibold/Regular, petrol dark layout with light-blue title, theme palette with tints, .potx delivered.
- Student-style Canva deck rebuilt from a blank slide: style slide first (fonts, monochrome palette, rounded white boxes on color), reusable components, gradient only on cover and dividers, view-only link.
- Template slide using emoji bullets, speaker name top-left and title repeated bottom-right: rebuilt with a clean footer and balanced icon boxes.
- Tech/AI cover: Pinterest reference → mesh gradient image → huge title in a tech font (Epilogue).
- Product-launch concept: custom 3D camera model rotated across slides with Morph in PowerPoint.
- AI test: an old talk script fed to a deck generator returned 10 repetitive bullet slides; only per-slide refinement approached the intended design.
