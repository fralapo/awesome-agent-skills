# Chapter 04: Text & Typography on Slides

## Core Idea
Text on a slide is a visual anchor for what the speaker says, not a transcript of it: if everything you will say is on the slide, your presence adds nothing, and if a block of text is badly formatted nobody even tries to read it. Before choosing a font, decide what the one message of the slide is and cut the rest into speaker notes. Then make the remaining words "beautiful to look at" first and readable second: one sans-serif family exploited through its weights, a fixed size scale with strong contrast between levels (everything big means nothing is important), short left-aligned lines, controlled line spacing, a few emphasised words, no justified text, no ALL CAPS sentences, no lonely words at line ends. The audience scans before it reads (titles, bold words), so the biggest text must carry the takeaway, and secondary text must be visibly secondary. Text-heavy slides are acceptable only for decks sent by email, which must be self-explanatory.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Message title + small label**: the generic topic word ("Problem", "Context", "Mission", "Revenue 2025", "Idea") moves to a small label top-left; directly under it a large sentence states the actual takeaway ("what the real problem is", "why the idea is remarkable"). Charts, tables, images below only support that sentence. Reading only the titles of the deck must tell the story.
  When to use: every content slide; especially when a deck will also be read without the speaker.
- **Reduce-then-amplify recipe**: (1) take the text you will say, (2) read it carefully, (3) pick the key messages that must emerge, summarising each slide to one sentence or 1–3 words, (4) only then open the software; put the sentence big, reinforce it with one icon or image, say everything else aloud. Prioritising content is not done inside PowerPoint.
  When to use: any slide that started as a paragraph or a page of notes.
- **The 3 problems of bullet points**: anonymous (look like everyone else's), endless (10 points nobody reads; they only serve as the speaker's prompt), text-only (nothing visual clarifies them).
  When to use: as the diagnosis before redesigning any list slide.
- **Size scale by slide type** (see Rules & Numbers): a fixed set of sizes, mostly multiples of 8, chosen by what the slide contains rather than by eye each time.
  When to use: building a template or reviewing a deck where text sizes drift slide to slide.
- **One family, many weights**: each weight/style (Light, Regular, Medium, Semibold, Bold, Italic, Condensed) is effectively a font; use them to (a) format parts of a paragraph, (b) set a mood (a big title in Light instead of Bold), (c) swap to a more readable weight when a size looks too heavy or too thin. You don't have to use them all.
  When to use: always as the default typographic system; add a second family only deliberately.
- **The seven formatting levers**: typeface, line spacing, alignment, line length (measure), white space, weight variations, colour.
  When to use: as a checklist for any text block, on slides or in Word reports.
- **Contrast, emphasis, breathing room**: text must separate from the background; hierarchy must show what to read first (via size and colour); white space must surround elements.
  When to use: a three-word final check on each slide.

## Rules & Numbers
All sizes assume a 1920×1080 canvas; they do not transfer to other slide sizes unchanged (scale proportionally). Sizes also depend on the font's x-height (same pt, some faces look bigger) and on colour/position, which also shift hierarchy.

**Amount of text**
- One idea per slide; split long content across several slides rather than shrinking it.
- Bullet lists: 3 points is ideal (rule of three); 5 is the hard maximum per slide. 6 points → two columns of 3 separated by a thin grey line (1–1.5 pt), each point max 3 lines.
- A single bullet point is not a list: remove the symbol and set the sentence large.
- Text-heavy slides only for documents sent by email/PDF; there you may write more and introduce paragraphs with small headings.

**Type scale (pt on 1920×1080): the canonical reference for every chapter**
| Role | Size | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cover / statement / menu headline | 80–160 | 120–160 when one word or phrase fills the width; cover display text up to 200–300; quotes at the low end, ~80 (96–128 is too much for a quote) |
| Message title on a content slide | 64–88 | key sentence ~80, up to 3 lines, 2–3 words in accent colour |
| Title on list / data / chart slides | 40–48 | the slide must also hold a list, a table or a chart |
| Subtitle under a large title | 48–64 | balanced against the title |
| Body text | 24–32 | minimum 32 when slides are read across a classroom or large room |
| Data descriptions / secondary text | 24 | grey paragraph under a message title 24–32 (28 typical) |
| Key numbers | ≥56 | 56 in dense data slides |
| Hero numbers | 120–200 | caption 24–32 |
| Label / eyebrow above a title | 20–28 | ~24 typical; increased letter spacing, optionally in an outlined rounded rectangle |
| Sources, footnotes, footer | 18–20 | chart axis labels 18–20 too (bar-chart category labels up to 28 when there is no Y axis or grid; see ch07) |

Common slide recipes built from the scale:
| Slide type | Main text | Secondary text |
|---|---|---|
| Statement slide (one short phrase, quote, key word) | 80–160 | — |
| Title + text (message slide) | key sentence 80, up to 3 lines | paragraph 28, grey, in the same text box; 48 pt space before it |
| Phrase + small text | phrase 64 | paragraph 28–32 |
| List / content slide with title | title 40–48 | description 24–32 (list details 24–28) |
| Three-point slide | point title 48 | 2-line description 28, grey; icon in accent colour above |
| Sparse big-number slide | label 24 + framing sentence 80, numbers 120 | description 24–32 |
| Dense data slide | title 40–48, numbers 56 | data descriptions 24; axis labels 18–20 |
| Text beside a vertical image | ~56 with reduced line spacing (below the 64 floor because the column is narrow) | — |
- Reconciliation: headline sizes (80–160) are for sparse statement-style slides; message titles (64–88) for content slides that carry one sentence; the tighter title range (40–48) for slides that must also hold lists, data or a chart. Choose the row by content, then never mix rows inside one slide.
- Prefer multiples of 8 where possible (24, 32, 40, 48, 56, 64, 80, 120, 160, 200) to keep proportions harmonious; exceptions (28, 18–20) are accepted where readability demands. The scale is a guide, not a law.
- Keep a large ratio between adjacent levels: text close together with similar sizes flattens the slide. "A lot of difference, but not too much."
- When resizing a client deck, shrink secondary texts instead of inflating everything.
- When a deck title appears on every slide, make it small (e.g. 20 pt Medium, low contrast: footer/label size) so it never competes with the slide title.

**Font choice**
- Default to a sans serif: hard to get wrong, readable at every size. Serif for very long reading texts (documents), history/literature themes, or only in titles and large text for elegance.
- Avoid Calibri (old PowerPoint default) and Arial (overused); system fonts look 1990s. Aptos (new PowerPoint default) is acceptable.
- Sans serifs that work: Inter, Poppins, Space Grotesk, Manrope, Satoshi, General Sans, Roboto, IBM Plex Sans, Open Sans, Epilogue (tech covers); condensed: Antonio, Roboto Condensed. Sources: Google Fonts and Fontshare (check licence for commercial use).
- Pick a family with many weights. Pick for feeling: forms convey mood (massive space suits → heavy condensed; packaging with tall bottles → tall condensed with almost no line spacing).
- Script/calligraphic fonts: almost never; never in corporate decks; at most one word.
- Test: not "is it beautiful?" but "can it be read?" — have someone else read it.
- Small text: use Regular, not Light.

**Font pairing**
- Max two families; beginners use one. Two fonts must be clearly different (similar ones look like a mistake).
- Start from a sans (Manrope, Poppins, Roboto, Inter) and add a serif, a monospace or a script in small doses. Proven pairs: Inter + Lora; Manrope + a contemporary serif; Satoshi with a tall condensed display face.
- Second font only in large text (titles, big phrases), never in small text — ideally on only 1–2 words for emphasis.
- Paste with "keep text only"/destination formatting to avoid intruder fonts from other decks.

**Line spacing**
- PowerPoint "single" = ~120% of size (Keynote ~127%).
- Paragraphs: multiple 1.1–1.2 (≈130–140% of size). Big compact titles: 0.8–0.95 (0.9 typical). Never 1.5 — lines separate and read like a list.
- Prefer "exact" line spacing for control on titles (e.g. 80 pt title at exact 88; a 57 → 51 reduction on a serif title).
- Space between paragraphs via "space before/after" (e.g. 8 pt after a title line, 48 pt between key sentence and paragraph), never with empty returns.
- Proximity: spacing inside a point/block must be smaller than spacing between points/blocks.

**Line length**
- Projected slides: 40–50 characters per line (or fewer). Documents, reports and emailed decks: 50–80 characters.
- Never run lines edge to edge; keep text columns at half or two-thirds of the slide; but avoid 2–3-word lines that force constant returns.
- Smaller text needs shorter lines; a measure that suits 40 pt is wrong at 12 pt.

**Alignment**
- Left for anything longer than 2–3 lines; centred only for very short phrases (2–3 lines max); right only for footer info/captions/page number. If the text is left-aligned, align the title left too.
- Never justified on slides: PowerPoint, Keynote and Google Slides don't hyphenate, creating rivers of white. Justified is acceptable only in long Word documents with hyphenation and correct language set.
- Bullets always left-aligned; a centred bullet list never exists (centre a keyword list only after removing the symbols).
- Anchor text boxes at the top when the title sits at the top, so added lines don't shift the first one.

**Emphasis**
- Highlight 1–3 key words per paragraph/short text, chosen by what you say aloud; never a whole sentence.
- Methods: bold; bold + slide colour; a thick drawn underline in a slide colour (e.g. 7 pt white line ≈ letter stroke weight); the same line thicker as a highlighter behind the word; a simple shape evoking the concept.
- One emphasis format for large texts and one for small texts across the deck; never bold + italic + gradient together. Gradient text: max 2 colours.
- Weight contrast must be visible: Bold vs Regular (Medium vs Bold is too close). If you emphasise, emphasise for real.
- Avoid default italic/underline for emphasis on slides; use bold, not UPPERCASE.
- Coloured text that becomes unreadable (e.g. light green) → switch to an underline in that colour.
- Legal/normative text that must appear: full text in grey, cited part in a contrasting colour, or reveal parts one at a time — and read it aloud.

**Case, punctuation, widows**
- No ALL CAPS on many words: word shapes become rectangles, losing ascenders/descenders. Uppercase is acceptable for 1–3 big display words or short labels.
- Only necessary punctuation: no multiple "!!!", no full stops or colons after titles.
- No lonely words or short words at line ends, at least in titles: rephrase, change size (consistently across slides), slightly adjust tracking, or resize the box. Avoid a very long line followed by a very short one (fix the rag with manual returns).
- Edit client text for consistency (lengths, capitals, punctuation) before laying it out.
- Web addresses: drop the protocol prefix and the trailing slash; show only the bare domain.

**Letter spacing**
- Never exaggerated spacing on multi-line text. Increased tracking only on 2–3 words: labels/eyebrows or a central title.
- Large display titles can take slightly reduced tracking (e.g. 1.5 → 1 at 64 pt; tight on 200 pt condensed).

**Bullet points**
- Same length for every point (if the first has 5–6 words, so do the others) and same syntactic structure.
- Max one sub-level (keyword + short description); symbol only on level 1.
- Symbol-to-text gap set with ruler indents, close but not glued; custom symbols coloured or themed.
- Reveal one point at a time on click (a simple fade), as you mention it; or keep all visible and dim inactive points to 30% opacity (active 100%).

**Text in shapes & autofit**
- Write text inside the shape (double-click), not in a box laid over it; colour the text box fill instead of adding a separate rectangle.
- Autofit: never "Shrink text on overflow" (cause of text shrinking by itself); use "Resize shape to fit text" for normal boxes and placeholders, "Do not autofit" for fixed-size cards.
- Card internal margins example: 0.7 cm left and bottom, 0 top; too large a margin pushes text out. Placeholders: internal margins 0.
- Text shadows: off by default (they look like double vision); only on large text over a photo, light and very diffuse.

## Procedures
**Rebuild a "title + text" slide**
1. Copy the label from the other slides (top-left) for consistency.
2. Write the key sentence (≤3 lines) at 80 pt; colour 2–3 words in the accent.
3. In the same text box add the elaboration at 28 pt grey; set "space before" 48 pt.
4. Put one image covering the right part of the slide.

**Turn a bullet list into something visual**
1. Cut to 3 (max 5) points of equal length.
2. Choose a layout: title left (two lines) + points with icons right; horizontal boxes/cards (the box replaces the bullet symbol); one point per slide with a small progress indicator (1 2 3 4) or Morph linking them; image columns for evocative content (e.g. destinations), accent colour sampled from each photo.
3. Give each point one icon expressing its meaning; put points in rounded rectangles for order.
4. Build the three-point slide from the numbers slide: keep label + phrase, replace numbers with 48 pt titles + 28 pt grey descriptions.
5. Animate: fade per paragraph, on click.

**Set text styles once in the Slide Master (PowerPoint)**
1. View > Slide Master; select the top master thumbnail (not a single layout).
2. Set fonts for title and body placeholders.
3. Treat each indent level as a paragraph style: remove the bullet, drag both ruler indent markers left, set weight, size, colour, line spacing and space before/after (e.g. level 1 "heading", label-sized: Semibold 20 pt, accent, multiple 1.15, after 12; level 2 "text": smaller, Light, grey). Rename levels.
4. Placeholders: Resize shape to fit text, top-anchored, internal margins 0.
5. Apply styles on slides with Tab / Increase indent; change the master to update every slide at once.

**Fix lonely words**: rephrase → adjust box width → adjust size consistently → small tracking tweak, in that order.

## Common Mistakes
- Generic title ("Problem") with the message hidden in the subtitle → small label + message title.
- Everything too small → use the scale; everything too big → no white space, no hierarchy; similar sizes side by side → increase ratio.
- Title and points with identical formatting → distinct styles per role (label, title, body, formula).
- Text column spanning the slide → narrow to half/two-thirds, use the free space for a visual.
- Justified text → left-align.
- Three or four unrelated fonts from copy-pasting → one family, paste as text only.
- Calibri/Arial/system or calligraphic fonts → a characterful sans.
- Mixed bullet lengths (1-line and 2-line points) → equalise; an AI rewriter can help, then fix by hand.
- All points shown at once → one at a time.
- Bold on whole sentences, or bold + italic + gradient → 1–3 words, one method.
- Exaggerated letter spacing set by a slider → standard tracking.
- Text shrinking by itself → change autofit option.
- Title centred and nudged left by hand → real left alignment.
- Rainbow document: many text colours → brand colours only for title levels and accents.

## Mental Models
- If the slide says everything, the speaker is redundant; voice and slide must complete, not duplicate, each other.
- Readers scan first (titles, bold), then decide whether to read: design for the scan.
- White space rests the eye, makes text look shorter and shows what belongs together.
- "If everything is big, nothing is important": legibility is not a shouting contest; size enough for the room, then air for focus.
- Chaos on the slide becomes chaos in the audience's head; consistency of length and language reads as clarity.
- Content is the foundation: messy wording collapses any design, like a house without foundations.
- The label is small, the message is big — the title is not necessarily the largest text (on a "Mission" slide the mission sentence is the information).

## Examples
- A pitch's problem slide: "Problem" became a small label; a 2-line title states the real problem; four points sit in white rounded boxes, each with an icon in a coloured circle, text 32 pt, title ~80 pt in dark blue instead of black, key word underlined with a 7 pt line.
- A template-based slide with emoji bullets, speaker name top-left and the deck title bottom-right: rebuilt with the title top-left, a 6-point list split into two columns of 3 with a thin divider, emoji in white rounded boxes with a soft shadow, a clean footer.
- A school deck where the presentation title was the largest element on every slide: shrunk in place; the slide title moved left and enlarged; a "10 rules" list became one rule per slide with a slider-like indicator.
- A three-numbers KPI slide: label 24, framing sentence 80, numbers 120 in light blue, descriptions 32, blocks laid out horizontally with thin grey separators.
- A brand deck inspired by packaging: tall condensed uppercase titles at 200 pt with tight tracking, emphasis state 300 pt vs 100 pt, secondary text in a geometric sans.
- A legal slide: full article in grey, the cited clause coloured, read aloud by the speaker.
