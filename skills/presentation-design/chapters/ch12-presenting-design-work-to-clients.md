# Chapter 12: Presenting Design Work to Clients

## Core Idea
The design is the product and the presentation is its packaging. A client approves faster when the work is introduced with context first, walked through in sequence, explained in references the client already knows, and followed by targeted questions. Early directions are conversation starters, not finished designs.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Walk-Through Sequence (any visual piece)**: the listener needs a frame of reference, then the sequence, then the meaning.
1. Set context: what it is, for which event or audience, and the overall arc.
2. Walk the frames in order and say how the story develops.
3. Call out key details; pass repetitive frames quickly ("more of the same").
4. Name the peak moment and why it matters.
When to use: any design critique, pitch or review of a multi-frame piece (campaign, storyboard, identity system).

**Direction Round ("style-scape" step)**: a first presentation of early directions before any real design.
1. Recap the discovery words the client chose.
2. Show a small set of clearly distinct directions (about four), each with an evocative name and a short descriptor.
3. Filter each direction through a named target-user persona.
4. Ask which parts the client likes and combine them into one chosen direction.
5. Hand that direction to the design team as the sandbox.
Rules: contrast the options (near-duplicates look like padding); never present around twenty; names set the narrative, so choose them with care; state that nothing is designed yet so people judge direction, not execution; do not defend hard, involve the client so they co-own it; use persona questions ("how would this customer react?") to move the client from personal taste to audience fit; check colour balance (a bold palette can read as too primary and need softening).

**Identity (Logo) Presentation Method**:
- *Prerequisites*: solid discovery, a direction round done, only best work, a slide app (not loose files).
- *Order*: (1) recap the original goal or pain point; (2) recap the process and the discovery words, looking for patterns, about 5 minutes at most; (3) present three closely related options; (4) keep a hidden comparison slide of all options for when the client asks; (5) ask targeted questions; (6) give time for feedback and set a deadline.
- *Five slides per option*: isolated logo on white with generous space; one-colour version on light and dark backgrounds; standard mock-ups drawn from discovery; small-format uses; large-format uses; then the isolated logo again.
- *Why three*: the client can reject one without being forced to pick a favourite in the room.
- Explain the thinking behind each option, ideally with a visual link to the inspiration.
- Small- and large-format applications often trigger upsell requests.
- Use mock-up templates with swappable artwork (smart objects) so options can be updated quickly; keep backgrounds and photos on-brand.

**Targeted Questions (after presenting)**: "Did we miss the mark completely?", "How would [persona] react?", "Did we step in the right direction to solve [pain point]?", "Is there one direction we can cross off?" Avoid "What did you think?", which invites personal taste. Do not expect creative direction in the meeting; end with next steps and a feedback date.

**Reference-Based Pitch Language**: translate what is seen into references the client already knows.
1. Name the concept with a short theme label.
2. Name the inspiration source and why it is relevant.
3. Tie the direction to the target client's character and taste.
4. Explain each visual device with a concrete reference (a map contour as a graphic motif, reversed lettering recalling sail lettering).
5. Close by describing how it feels to hold or experience, using other senses.
Rules: describe the concept in plain words, then tie shapes and colour to that idea; link choices to meanings the audience already knows; avoid jargon (font names, kerning) unless the client uses it, use analogies; choose descriptors carefully (a word such as "salty" may carry a negative meaning); if the audience cannot read the text, still explain the feeling; the pitch must stand on its own so the client does not need to decode the visuals; show the reference boards and explain why they were chosen so feedback lands on the right things.

## Common Mistakes
- Flipping through slides without setting context.
- Emailing many files one by one instead of presenting a sequence.
- Opening with logos and no recap of the goal.
- Insider vocabulary the listener has not learned; a term that connects to nothing.
- A pitch that sounds like a sales push; skipping the sensory description.
- Many near-identical options; open "what did you think?" questions.

## Mental Models
- Context, then sequence, then meaning.
- Options are a question to the client, not an answer to defend.
- The client's feedback is only as good as the question that produced it.
