# Chapter 01: The Process: Message Before Software

## Core Idea
A presentation is the act of presenting: a complete form of communication in which the speaker (voice, presence, charisma) and the content (storytelling) matter more than the slides. Slides come last, and their job is to amplify what is said out loud. They should never repeat it or stand in for it. Opening PowerPoint first makes you answer every question at once on a blank slide: content, visualization, font, colour and slide count. You lose hours moving objects around and end up with decoration rather than communication. The working order is fixed. First set the foundations (audience, goal, technical context). Then structure the content (an outline, or a word-for-word script) and pick the key message of each slide. Next, sketch the visual narrative on paper (storyboard) and collect inspiration from outside the slide world (moodboard). Only then design in the software, and finally rehearse and prepare delivery (remote, presenter view, PDF backup, a test on the target machine). Working this way is not always faster, but the result is more effective, and each phase spends energy on one kind of question at a time. Before any of this, ask whether slides are the right medium at all: a product demo, a whiteboard, a document or an email can serve better.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The three tools of a presentation**: (1) content/storytelling takes the audience from point A to point B; (2) voice/presence/public speaking carries it; (3) slides amplify it and "close the circle". Great storytelling without presence doesn't land. Without storytelling the audience takes home nothing. Stunning slides without the other two are almost useless. Hiding behind spectacular slides usually covers gaps in the first two.
  When to use: when a client or speaker wants to "fix the slides" before the message and the delivery are settled.
- **The 5-step method (+ bonus)**: 1. Foundations: audience, goal, technical context. 2. Structure content: an outline or a script, with a key message per slide and the text hierarchy written out in full. 3. Visual narrative: a hand-drawn storyboard with no colours or fonts. 4. Inspiration: a moodboard pulled from websites, apps, dashboards and posters. 5. Design in the software, pulling the earlier steps together. Bonus: rehearse and set up delivery.
  When to use: every deck, from a school project to a client keynote.
- **Three foundation questions**: *Who is it for?* (technical language or not; topics to include or avoid; headcount, which sets how much interaction is possible). *What is the goal?* Inform, educate, convince or inspire; each needs a different narrative structure and call to action, and the goal is the action you expect from the audience. *What file is needed on the day?* (Keynote or only PowerPoint, fonts to embed, cables, which machine).
  When to use: in the brief, before any content work.
- **The presentation-context matrix (Nancy Duarte)**: X axis is audience size (small to large); Y axis runs from stage/structure to spontaneity/discussion. Stage + small audience: a casual occasion, so speak first without support, then discuss at a whiteboard. Stage + large audience: a formal occasion rehearsed many times, so slides are necessary. Spontaneous + small audience: an interactive occasion, so hand out documents and discuss them. Spontaneous + large audience: pre-packaged content consumed passively.
  When to use: to decide whether slides are needed at all.
- **Three pre-deck questions on the medium**: (1) How does the audience want to be informed? Is there a standard, and do other speakers use slides? (2) How many people will be there: 5, 50 or 1,000? (3) What is the venue: screen, monitor, or nothing?
  When to use: before committing to building a deck.
- **Three questions per slide (message)**: What do I want to say with this slide (one key message, not two or three)? What must stick? (Example: you show three alternatives but want one to come across as best. Only then can you set visual priority.) How can I make it visual? The aim is clear and immediate first; beautiful comes later.
  When to use: on every slide of the outline, before sketching.
- **Three questions per slide (layout)**: Which elements, and how many? What is the key message? Where should the audience's eye land?
  When to use: during storyboarding.
- **Slide vs document**: a slide supports the voice, so it is "empty"; a document has to say everything because you are absent, so it is "full". A projected document pulls attention away from the voice. Slides sent by email are missing information.
  When to use: at the very start, to choose the deliverable. If both are needed, make two versions.
- **Technical vs emotional slides**: technical slides (charts, tables, data) build authority; the audience's focus is on the slide and the voice supports it. Emotional slides act as a stage set that gives weight to what is being said. Too many technical slides bore people; too many emotional ones cost credibility. Mix them to set the rhythm.
  When to use: when sequencing the storyboard.

## Rules & Numbers
- **Order is non-negotiable:** speech first, slides second. Pick the slide-worthy messages while reading the speech, outside PowerPoint. Never make that selection inside the software.
- **Script vs outline:** short talks (1–20 min) get the full speech written word for word. That checks the logical flow, finds the right words, and lets you internalize the text (not parrot it) so you can say it as if for the first time. Long sessions (1–2 h trainings, 3–4 h workshops) get an outline and are largely improvised.
- **Where a slide goes:** mark the script at the points where a concept or keyword needs emphasis, or where you want to show exactly what you are saying. Everything else stays in the voice.
- **Each sentence needs a before and an after** (cause and effect). Write a bridge sentence between sections so the speech flows.
- **Validate the script** with someone outside the project who still knows it (supervisor, colleague), or with an AI assistant. That shows what is essential, because to the author everything feels important. "If you understand it, it doesn't follow that others will": ask several people.
- **Slide count is an output, not an input.** "N slides per N minutes" (the common rule of thumb is about 15 slides per 10 min) and "one topic per slide" are lazy methods that produce a monotonous rhythm. Process: write the script (it sets the time), select the concepts that deserve a slide, design, rehearse, cut or add. Only then count. Reference point: 33 slides for a 20-min thesis defence worked fine. A visual slide can last about 10 s; a diagram lasts much longer. A content-first deck usually has "quite a few" slides, and nobody notices if they are well designed. It is the speaker who sets the duration, not the number of slides.
- **If a maximum is imposed** (university, teacher, organizer), respect it and don't overload slides to compensate. The constraint exists to force synthesis or make grading even.
- **Not every topic gets equal time:** slides that *reinforce* get little time; slides that *go deeper* get more. Add accents against monotony: a video, a pause that builds anticipation, several slides on one topic.
- **Voice/slide split:** summarize the slide's content in one sentence or 1–3 words, put it on the slide in large type, add an icon or image to support it visually, and say everything else. "If everything is on the slide, your presence adds no value." Putting everything you say on a slide is presenter laziness (Nancy Duarte).
- **Fear of forgetting is not a reason to fill slides.** Use speaker notes (visible only to you), a sheet of paper or (nicely made) cue cards.
- **Dense slides are allowed only with a strategy:** read the content together with the audience, highlight parts and analyse them one at a time, or stop talking and give people time to read. Never show a wall of text while talking about something else.
- **Storyboard rules:** a cross between a wireframe and a film/comic storyboard. No colours, no fonts, no pixel distances. Paper, an iPad or a whiteboard app all work. If a slide has to be added later, sketch it on paper first. To find layout structures, search images for standard forms (e.g. timeline variants, depending on whether dates or phases matter).
- **Layout defaults decided on paper:** a key number goes large and centred, with supporting elements. A timeline shows all milestones on the left and the selected date's description on the right. A process gives each step a title, a subtitle and an icon, with arrows for direction, plenty of space, the first step on the left and the last on the right; if there are too many steps, use more slides. A complex concept map is not one slide: one box per slide, with the arrows becoming transitions in the speech.
- **Moodboard:** do not take inspiration from other slides; slide style has barely evolved. Use websites (Awwwards, the Collections section), Dribbble (Popular view, or search "presentation design"), Behance, Pinterest (search e.g. "data viz"), apps, dashboards and posters. Collect them on one blank slide and extract fonts, colours, shapes and silhouettes. The style must be current and fit the topic. Skip the moodboard if a mandatory brand already fixes the style.
- **Questions to ask of every reference:** How are colours applied? How is hierarchy created? How are objects positioned? What creates consistency? How is it animated? What would translate to a slide? Take the idea, transform it, and restyle it in the deck's style. Never copy and paste.
- **Brand style, three cases:** (1) a full template with layouts: rigid, minimal creative effort; (2) a basic template with colours and fonts: freedom inside the brand; (3) nothing: look up the brand colours and fonts, often in the logo manual. With no brand book, derive the system from the logo and existing channels (site, blog, packaging).
- **Use a ready-made template only** when time is short, design skills are limited, or the presentation is not important. Design important decks and pitches from scratch or on a bespoke brand template.
- **First actions in the software:** guides (margins), theme colours and theme fonts. Build custom layouts only for long decks, not for a 10-slide deck. Format: 1920×1080 px, 16:9.
- **80/20 reuse:** about 20% of the designed elements make up 80% of the deck. Copy-paste them (e.g. the same title style, such as an 80 pt bold message title in the accent colour, on every slide; see ch04 type scale). Check consistency in thumbnail/slide-sorter view.
- **Match effort to stakes:** pitches and events justify spectacular work. For internal meetings, weekly updates and briefings, care plus standard layouts is enough; care alone already sets you apart. Content that changes often (e.g. a Gantt chart) must be built to be easy to edit.
- **Time budget:** a 10–20-min talk takes about 4–5 h for speech plus slides. A full client deck takes about one month of part-time work: immersion in the topic (if it isn't clear to the designer it won't be clear to the audience), style research when there is no brand identity, and review rounds ("never right the first time").
- **When NOT to use slides:** a team chat, or a consultation with 4–5 people where the flow changes with questions; slides become a barrier between consultant and client. Use the product itself in a demo, a video (e.g. customer reviews), a whiteboard or documents. Many slide-driven meetings could be an email. Slides *are* needed at conventions, thesis defences, school reports and startup pitches, where they show the audience where you are and where you are going, and where questions usually come at the end.
- **Slides vs emailed document:** the presented version spreads content across many slides and paces it with slide changes and animation. The emailed version (handout) compresses more per slide, on a fixed but flexible grid, with few clear element groups, because every extra group fragments scanning. Same design, denser, built to be analysed alone. Message-style titles make a deck readable without a speaker.
- **Delivery checklist:**
  - Always present in full-screen presentation mode, never from the editing view and never by scrolling a PDF.
  - Use a presenter remote (from about €10). Standing and using the space and your body beats being stuck at the keyboard. A smartphone works too.
  - An iPad can act as the remote (Keynote): draw or circle on slides, see the previous and next slide, read the notes.
  - Use presenter view with notes. Online, open the presenter window and share only the *audience window* (Canva: Present → presenter view), not the whole screen.
  - Always carry a PDF backup: it opens anywhere but loses animations and transitions. Check the exported PDF, because animations that cover overlapping images break in PDF.
  - Know in advance which software and which version the deck will run on, and test it there. Keynote → PowerPoint export keeps speaker notes but misreads animations (Magic Move does not become Morph, fades change), loses transparencies and shifts fonts and arrows. HTML export keeps animations but drops notes, and you must carry the whole folder. iCloud/online versions lack non-system fonts and stutter. If possible, connect your own device.
  - Embed fonts or use system/Google fonts when presenting from someone else's machine.
  - For online/browser decks (Canva, Google Slides), save a view-only link so you don't depend on a login on the day.
- **Rehearse in three passes:** (1) alone with the slides, to make transitions natural; (2) in front of people, for emotional state and the logic of the speech; (3) in the venue, for space, microphones and monitors. Rehearsal also tells you what to cut or add.

## Procedures
**A. Whole-deck workflow**
1. Brief: audience, goal (inform/educate/convince/inspire, plus the action expected), technical context (software, machine, fonts, cables, venue, headcount). Ask for any constraints: template, slide cap, format.
2. Decide the medium (Duarte matrix): slides, document, demo, whiteboard, email, or a mix aligned to the goal.
3. Write the content in a text tool (docs or notes app): the full script for talks under about 20 min, an outline for long sessions. For each point that needs a slide, write the key message and the text hierarchy in full.
4. Validate the script (an outside reader or an AI assistant) and smooth the bridges between sections.
5. Highlight in the script where a slide should emphasize or show something. These are the "slide moments".
6. Storyboard every slide by hand (no colour, no fonts). Answer the per-slide questions: elements, key message, where the eye lands.
7. Moodboard from non-slide sources and extract fonts, colours and shapes, or apply the mandatory brand template.
8. Set up the file: 1920×1080, guides, theme colours, theme fonts, footer. Then design following the storyboard, reusing the core 20% of elements.
9. Review the sequence in slide-sorter view for consistency and background rhythm. Design the cover last.
10. Rehearse in three passes, export the PDF backup, test on the target software, and pack the remote.

**B. Single-slide recipe (5 steps)**
1. Define the key message: why this slide exists.
2. List precisely what has to go on it.
3. Sketch on paper how the content fits: what is visual, what is text, whether there is a title and where.
4. Look for inspiration (Dribbble, Behance, websites).
5. Build it in the software and think only about graphics.

**C. Turning a dense draft into slides**
1. Extract the draft's text into a doc or notes app.
2. Identify the distinct concepts; each one becomes its own slide (e.g. one oversized diagram plus bottom bullets became three slides).
3. Storyboard each one, then rebuild.

**D. Paper-and-pen workshop exercise** (for teams that "aren't designers")
1. Show a messy, overloaded slide and analyse it together (too dark, text too small, an image that adds nothing, a message that only emerges after reading everything).
2. Hand out paper: "What shape best expresses this message?"
3. After a few minutes, compare the sketches; several valid versions always appear. Then turn the sketches into slides.

## Common Mistakes
- Starting from the blank slide or the background → start from what you want to say; the background is a quick late decision.
- Writing on the slides everything you will say → one sentence or 1–3 words on the slide, the rest in your voice and in the notes.
- Asking "how many slides?" first → weight each piece of content, design, rehearse, then count.
- Monotone rhythm (same time on every slide, all technical or all emotional) → alternate data slides with visual/emotional slides; add pauses, a video, or multi-slide sequences.
- One whole topic crammed into one slide → one key message per slide; split.
- Projecting a document, or emailing speaker slides → choose the output first; produce two versions if both are needed.
- Copying the style of other decks or the first image search result → take inspiration from websites, apps and posters, and adapt.
- Destroying a ready-made template by forcing content into it → a template only works if used almost as designed; otherwise start from a clean file.
- Spending weeks on internal-meeting slides → standard layouts plus care.
- Presenting from edit mode, glued to the keyboard → full screen, presenter view, remote.
- Discovering on stage that animations or fonts broke → ask about the target software in advance, test there, carry a PDF.
- Clicking and waiting for everything to appear (awkward silences) → "on click" only for elements you mention at that moment; one animation type (fade) throughout.
- Typos on the cover → proofread; a typo there is the most visible error in the deck.

## Mental Models
- **Slides are the stage set, the speaker is the actor.** The set amplifies but never replaces the performance.
- **Design is the finish line, not the starting line.** Functional first, beautiful second.
- **Every content has its favourite outfit:** the right visual form for each piece of content is found on paper, not by trial and error in the software.
- **Whoever is prepared knows how to simplify.** Synthesis means selecting, not compressing everything into one slide.
- **The audience wants you to succeed.** Use that to reassure nervous speakers. A blank moment that feels endless to you lasts a second for them: breathe and regroup.
- **"No inspiration without education":** a talk can inspire (personal story) and educate (practical part) in one arc.
- **A good process beats a good tool:** "the software doesn't matter"; one tool may only make an animation smoother.

## Examples
- **Client draft in PowerPoint:** one oversized diagram filled the page and the key messages sat as bullets at the bottom. Extracted to a notes app, the content turned out to hold three concepts and became three slides. The moodboard was skipped because a mandatory brand red fixed the style.
- **Talk at a school festival (10–20 min):** goals set as inspire + educate. The audience (students, designers, followers) led to a two-part narrative: a personal journey, then a slide redesign done on the spot with the audience. The script was written word for word, validated with an AI assistant, and marked where slides were needed. It opened with two show-of-hands questions, used one slide held long while talking about oneself, then faster slide changes synced to the voice, plus sentence slides to break the flow.
- **Thesis defence (20 min):** 33 slides. Data from a 16-page market study reduced to the one percentage that justified the claim. Speaker notes saved a memory blank.
- **Consultation with 4–5 prospects:** slides dropped in favour of a product demo and review videos, so the conversation could follow the client's questions.
- **One project, two outputs:** presented slides that support the speech, plus a 10-slide denser version to send by email.
- **A 5-min university public-speaking exam:** the speech was written word for word and built on a carrying metaphor (dancing = speaking: choreography = speech, steps = words, movement = voice), with the opening statement echoed in the close.
