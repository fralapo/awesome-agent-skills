## Facts vs Positions: how evidence is labelled

Every concept in the chapters has the fields **Thesis**, **Mechanism**, **Evidence**, **Trade-off**, **Does not hold when**. The **Evidence** line uses these labels:

- **Experimental**: controlled comparison or laboratory task (for example, retrospective memory versus moment-by-moment ratings; leading-question memory studies; choking under attention to technique).
- **Observational**: surveys, cohort data, case series. Correlation, not causation (for example, stress beliefs and mortality; language and savings).
- **Practitioner**: a method taught from experience in a field, no controlled test cited (habit-diagnosis logs, looping, the task-saturation rule).
- **Position**: an interpretation or a philosophy of life, written "(position)" or "In this model...".
- **Contested**: results with active disagreement, replication difficulty or later critique; the chapter says why. Examples: the constructed-emotion account of emotion, the income plateau in lived happiness, the stress-mindset mortality estimate, language and saving, the flow multipliers, willpower as a depletable resource, the exact rate of planted false memories, the precise positivity ratio in couples.

Read **Does not hold when** before recommending anything: if the limit applies, drop the rule.

## The Core Principles (recurrence order)

1. **Memory is a story.** Endings, peaks and changes dominate; duration is neglected; the remembering self decides.
2. **Attention distorts importance.** Whatever you think about looks larger; ask what a normal week looks like.
3. **Motive before method.** Wanting to see clearly (scout) beats more logic training; defending is the default.
4. **Your future self is another person.** Expect to change; add humility, curiosity and courage.
5. **Under overload, shrink the problem.** Accept you will not finish everything, then do the next fastest useful step.
6. **Emotions are constructed from body signals plus context.** (contested) Reinterpretable inputs give you leverage, not total control.
7. **Interpretation of arousal changes performance.** Preparation, not calm, is often the useful reading.
8. **Meaning and depth beat repetition for memory;** but memory is reconstructive: confidence is not accuracy.
9. **Do not think about the steps of a well-learned skill under pressure.** Practise under realistic pressure.
10. **Change the loop, not the person.** Keep the cue and reward, replace the routine; use people, not only willpower.
11. **Boredom has a function.** Idle attention is where inner questions arise; protect some.
12. **Frames, mess and solitude** generate creative work; groups come after individuals.
13. **Prove you understood, then respond.** Listening is an active, checkable skill; find the understory.
14. **Maturity is values you can act on and tolerance of disapproval;** love is learned skill, not only a feeling.
15. **Fail small, in new territory, with a hypothesis.** Code the stakes honestly.
