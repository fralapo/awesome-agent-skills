# Chapter 14: Forming Opinions and Trusting Sources

## Core Idea
Opinions are formed faster than they are checked, and the information environment rewards speed, certainty and indignation. This chapter covers the cognitive side of that problem: how deep your study should be before you hold a view, why some content rewards you without informing you, how trust itself becomes scarce when signals are cheap to fake, and how to keep access to sources you will need later. Argument tactics (dodging, twisting, flooding) are in ch09; side-switching and steelmanning are in ch09 (9.3); news overload and stress are in ch04 (4.8); attention economics and feeds are in ch12. Evidence methods are in `scientific-thinking`.

## 14.1 Match confidence to depth of study
- **Thesis (position)**: the confidence of an opinion should match how deeply you have studied the question. Saying "do your own research" is vague; saying how deeply you read, and which sources, is checkable.
- **Mechanism**: a rough scale of study depth helps. One version, adapted from rating systems used in other fields: (1) heard it; (2) read a summary; (3) read a primary source or two; (4) read several sources that disagree; (5) reconstructed the argument and tested its weakest step; (6) made and checked predictions over time. Confidence should rise with the level, and the person should be able to name the level reached.
- **Practice**: before you state a strong view, say which level you have reached and what would change your mind.
- **Trade-off**: deep study takes time that most questions do not deserve; the scale is for claims you intend to act on or repeat.
- **Does not hold when**: the question is one you can settle with a direct test in minutes. Run the test.

## 14.2 Content that rewards without informing
- **Thesis (position)**: some content that is true but useless still activates the same reward response as useful content, and indignation spreads faster than nuance. Platforms that pay for attention amplify both.
- **Mechanism**: the feeling of having learned something is cheap to produce, and social sharing rewards content that provokes emotion. Metrics such as watch time or shares then select for these qualities. Goodhart's law applies: when the metric becomes the target, the content drifts toward what raises it, not toward what informs.
- **Evidence**: partly experimental for reward responses to trivia; the link to platform design and the sharing effects are observational and contested in size.
- **Practice**: notice the difference between "that was interesting" and "I can now do or decide something differently". Count the useful takeaways per item consumed.
- **Trade-off**: pure information without entertainment can be hard to sustain; some entertainment is a healthy part of learning.
- **Does not hold when**: the item is explicitly entertainment and you know it. Then no correction is needed.

## 14.3 Trust is scarce when signals are cheap to fake
- **Thesis**: when images, voices and verification badges can be produced or bought cheaply, the signal "this looks authentic" loses its value, and trust must come from relationships and independent corroboration.
- **Mechanism**: a verified badge that no longer confirms identity, a realistic synthetic voice or video, and an authoritative-looking document can each mislead. Panic-driven withdrawals from a bank within days show how quickly confidence in an institution can collapse once its signals are doubted. Corroboration by independent sources and by repeated, costly interactions (a track record, a person who will be embarrassed if wrong) are harder to fake than a single artefact.
- **Practice**: for an important claim, find two independent sources and check the original; for a request involving money or data, verify through a channel you already trust, not the one that asked.
- **Trade-off**: verification takes time and can slow decisions that need speed.
- **Does not hold when**: the claim is low-stakes and easily reversible.

## 14.4 Sources decay: keep what you will need
- **Thesis**: online content disappears, moves or changes. Important sources should be recorded when you read them.
- **Mechanism**: links break, pages are edited and paywalls appear. A cited claim without a stored copy or a date becomes impossible to re-check. Rates of link rot cited in commentary vary by study and should not be taken as a fixed figure.
- **Practice**: record the date, the URL, the author and the relevant quote in your notes; keep a local copy of important documents where the licence allows; prefer primary sources, which are more likely to remain stable than commentary on them.
- **Trade-off**: archives take storage and can accumulate clutter; curate them.
- **Does not hold when**: the material is widely mirrored and stable (official laws, standard references). Cite the stable identifier instead.

## 14.5 Probabilities instead of certainty
- **Thesis (position)**: a belief held with a probability (not 0 and not 1) can be updated; a belief held as certain resists evidence and invites defence.
- **Mechanism**: confidence expressed as a number or a range makes it clear what would change the estimate. A person who says "I am quite sure, about 80%" can later be shown to be well or badly calibrated. A person who says "I am certain" has nothing to revise.
- **Practice**: state a credence for important forecasts and keep a short log to check them later; wait before judging a breaking story, because early reports are often revised.
- **Trade-off**: probabilistic language can sound evasive in conversation and can be used to avoid commitment.
- **Does not hold when**: the question is a fact that can be checked. Check it.

## 14.6 Fact-checking the first version of a story
- **Thesis**: early versions of a story are often incomplete or wrong; the first claim is the least checked.
- **Mechanism**: quick reports are written before facts are confirmed, and corrections travel more slowly than the original. Numbers are the most error-prone part: missing denominators, mixed time periods and cherry-picked extremes can make a fair statistic look dramatic.
- **Practice**: find the primary source; check the denominator and the period; look for the figure in a second independent source before repeating it.
- **Trade-off**: waiting costs the satisfaction of being first, and sometimes it costs the chance to act on early information.
- **Does not hold when**: the decision must be taken now; then act on the best current estimate and say it is provisional.
