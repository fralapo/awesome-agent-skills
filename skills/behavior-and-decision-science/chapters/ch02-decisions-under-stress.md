# Chapter 02: Deciding Fast Under Stress

## Core Idea
When demands exceed capacity, people do not decide worse because they are weak but because they are **saturated**: attention narrows, rational capacity is spent, and spare capacity is taken by self-criticism. In this model the remedy is not heroic analysis but a small, trained rule: do the **next fastest useful thing**, then the next. This chapter combines an operational-practitioner rule set with a model of how people behave in real emergencies. Almost all of it is practitioner experience, not controlled experiment; the labels say so.

## 2.1 Task saturation
- **Thesis**: there are three resources (energy, time, money); only time cannot be created. The average person faces a very large number of small decisions a day (a figure over a thousand is stated, source unclear). Past a personal threshold, performance, mood and confidence fall together: *task saturation*.
- **Mechanism**: saturation lowers cognitive ability, raises stress and anxiety, and produces a sense of "unproductive success": busy but not finishing anything. A feedback loop follows: feeling unproductive creates a negative mindset that creates more problems.
- **Evidence**: practitioner (an intelligence-service training heuristic). The general idea that overload degrades performance is well supported (general knowledge); the exact figure for daily decisions is not verified.
- **Trade-off**: keeping a margin means doing fewer things in parallel and saying no.
- **Does not hold when**: tasks are trivially parallel or automated; there is no real saturation to manage.

## 2.2 Subtract two
- **Thesis (position)**: estimate how many tasks you can carry out simultaneously with confidence, then subtract two.
- **Mechanism**: fewer tasks leave more resources for each one, productivity per task rises, momentum carries to the others, and mindset stays positive. The opposite spiral appears when you sit at your estimated maximum.
- **Evidence**: practitioner heuristic; not tested. Reasonable, but the number "two" is a convention.
- **Trade-off**: a conservative limit can feel like under-using yourself.
- **Does not hold when**: your estimate is already pessimistic (chronic under-commitment); then the rule worsens avoidance.

## 2.3 Operational prioritisation: the next fastest task
- **Thesis (position)**: once saturated, first accept that you will *not* finish everything, then ask only: "what is the next task I can complete in the shortest time?"
- **Mechanism**: time is a neutral arbiter ("you cannot argue with time"), so the question is hard to corrupt by emotion. Each completed task removes one item, builds momentum and returns confidence. In an emergency the same rule yields: take cover (fast), assess the room (fast), decide the next step, one decision at a time, "not the most complicated one, not a hero play".
- **Evidence**: practitioner, plus general knowledge that small completions reduce overload (momentum effects in work design). No controlled test is cited.
- **Trade-off**: shortest-first can postpone the most important task indefinitely. In this model it is a **recovery** rule for saturation, not a planning method for a normal day.
- **Does not hold when**: a single task is critical and cannot wait (safety, a deadline with penalties). Then triage by consequence first, speed second.

## 2.4 Head trash
- **Thesis**: when rational thought drops out under saturation, spare capacity is filled with emotional thoughts: self-criticism, regret, "I will never do this again".
- **Mechanism**: these thoughts convince you to stop trying and to escape instead of face the problem, and make you feel unable to do things you can do.
- **Evidence**: practitioner; consistent with research on rumination and worry as attentional load (general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: you cannot silence it by force; the useful move is to give the mind a concrete next action.
- **Does not hold when**: the thoughts reflect a real, persistent mood problem; this skill gives no clinical advice (see Hand-offs).

## 2.5 Practice the routine before you need it
- **Thesis**: one successful use proves nothing; the rule becomes reliable only when drilled until automatic.
- **Mechanism**: under stress you do what you can do on autopilot (see ch06: performance under pressure). Practising at low stakes (home, ordinary work) makes the routine available when saturated.
- **Evidence**: practitioner; consistent with training research (general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: time spent practising.
- **Does not hold when**: never; the cost is small. The limit is that practice does not substitute for real exposure.

## 2.6 How people actually behave in a crisis
- **Thesis (position)**: most people go through a sequence in disasters: **denial and disbelief**, **deliberation (milling: discussing with others what is happening)**, then a **decisive moment** where they act, cooperate or, most often, do nothing. The most common response is to continue what they were doing.
- **Mechanism**: the brain compares incoming signals to prior experience; with no experience or training it labels the situation "normal" (normalcy bias). In some crashes people died seated with smoke around them while others who had noted exits beforehand and had some prior experience got out. Fear is useful in small doses; high stress-hormone load impairs thinking. Training, knowing the environment (exits, stairs, risks) and rehearsal shorten denial and delay.
- **Evidence**: observational, from survivor accounts and disaster research, with anecdotal illustration. Behaviour varies widely between people and situations; the single anecdotes are not statistics.
- **Trade-off**: preparing has a small ongoing cost (looking at exits). The model also says some survival instincts (shutting down) fit modern buildings and evacuation poorly.
- **Does not hold when**: the context gives clear signals and trained roles (professional responders); then the sequence compresses.

## 2.7 Emotion as information, not command
- **Thesis**: under stress you decide better when you treat strong emotion as a signal about arousal or uncertainty, not about the facts (links to ch03 and ch04).
- **Mechanism**: a racing heart, a churn in the stomach or dread on waking does not prove the world is wrong. Ask what physical cause could explain the sensation (tiredness, hunger) before building a story.
- **Evidence**: experimental support for reappraisal (see ch04); the specific wake-up example is illustrative.
- **Trade-off**: reinterpreting can mask a real problem if used as a reflex.
- **Does not hold when**: the sensations are severe, persistent or alarming; seek professional help.

## Quick Rules
- Name the saturation: "I am past my limit; I will not finish all of this."
- Pick the shortest useful next step; complete it; repeat.
- Write down nothing about yourself except the next action.
- Drill the rule in ordinary days.
