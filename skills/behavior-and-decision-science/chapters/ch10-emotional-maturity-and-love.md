# Chapter 10: Relationships, Emotional Maturity and Love as a Skill

## Core Idea
In this model, a good life and a good relationship come from **practice and values you can act on**, not from a feeling you wait for. Maturity means moving from performing for approval to standing for something and tolerating disapproval; love is a **learned set of skills** used with a flawed partner, not a recognition of a perfect match. These are popular-psychology and philosophical positions, supported in places by research (attachment, relationship studies), but they are not clinical guidance, and this skill gives none.

## 10.1 Two kinds of well-being
- **Thesis (position)**: pleasure and comfort (hedonia) differ from a deeper "my life was worth living" feeling (eudaimonia). The modern environment is optimised for the first.
- **Mechanism**: quick dopamine hits and promises of easy happiness do not produce lasting life satisfaction; the second kind comes from struggles that were worth it.
- **Evidence**: a long philosophical distinction and psychological research on well-being (general knowledge). Treat the strong causal claims as position.
- **Trade-off**: seeking meaning can be overrated; ordinary pleasure has value.
- **Does not hold when**: someone is in deprivation or suffering; comfort is then a real need.

## 10.2 The backwards law
- **Thesis (position)**: the more you chase a positive experience, the more the chase is negative; accepting a negative experience becomes positive.
- **Mechanism**: the more you want to impress, to be beautiful, to be rich, to be loved, the more inadequate you feel; the more you want to be happy all the time, the more easily you get upset. Instead of "what will make me happy?" ask "**what am I willing to struggle for?**" and "what pain do I secretly enjoy?"; meaning is found in the struggles you relish.
- **Evidence**: philosophical and practitioner; related to research on acceptance (general knowledge). Not clinical.
- **Trade-off**: can romanticise suffering.
- **Does not hold when**: the struggle is imposed by abuse or injustice.

## 10.3 Adolescent versus adult mindset
- **Thesis (position)**: the adolescent mindset is bartering with the world, performing for approval; the adult mindset stands for something and tolerates being disliked.
- **Mechanism**: the modern world is more transactional, so performing seems always necessary; if the performance never fails enough to push you toward living by principle, you do not cross over. Principle-based living makes you **antifragile**: setbacks teach rather than collapse you. The principles are basic virtues available in any situation and always socially constructive.
- **Evidence**: philosophical position.
- **Trade-off**: may produce rigidity.
- **Does not hold when**: you confuse stubbornness with principle; test it against ch01.5 (scout mindset).

## 10.4 Choose values you can control
- **Thesis (position)**: you must care about something; the question is what. Good values are **immediate and controllable**, and constructive.
- **Mechanism**: pleasing others is a bad value because you cannot control what others feel; honesty, vulnerability and your own behaviour are controllable. The self-help blind spot: not "how do I become happier or richer?" but "why do I want that, and how am I measuring it?" A badly defined goal achieved can still harm you. A commitment is a controllable act: after a decision to commit to a partner, the many "alternative lives" that preoccupied the mind went quiet.
- **Evidence**: practitioner and personal narrative.
- **Trade-off**: committing closes options.
- **Does not hold when**: commitment is made under pressure or to a harmful situation.

## 10.5 Certainty, identity and avoidance
- **Thesis (position)**: much suffering comes from certainties about myself, how the world should be and how people should treat me. The more something threatens your identity, the more you avoid it, good things included.
- **Mechanism**: avoiding a hard conversation and sabotaging a good relationship may have the same root: the good thing also threatens your self-image. Holding identity more loosely makes you more open and more objective. A journaling exercise: take a struggle, name its core belief, then ask "what if I am wrong?" and related questions.
- **Evidence**: practitioner; related to research on self-verification and identity threat (general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: loosening identity can feel destabilising.
- **Does not hold when**: you need a stable base after trauma; then seek support rather than loosen.

## 10.6 Process, not result
- **Thesis (position)**: people want results but not the process; "if you do not want the cost you do not actually want the thing".
- **Mechanism**: advice "publish fifty pieces and come back" was given to hundreds of aspiring writers and very few did it; doing it would answer most of their questions. Extraordinary success also complicates relationships, since you no longer know why people approach you. Large success often comes from a small number of correct contrarian bets, which most people will not make (their own thinking depends on others, they may be wrong, and they may be too scared to commit).
- **Evidence**: personal anecdote and a famous investor's remark; survivorship bias is a risk (general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: reading outcomes as "just a few bets" ignores luck.
- **Does not hold when**: no feedback arrives; a process without feedback is wasted effort.

## 10.7 Why romantic culture fails couples
- **Thesis (position)**: choosing a partner by instinct and feeling has been the norm for about two centuries; in this model it has not made people happier, because it ignores the **difficulties** such couples will have.
- **Mechanism**: romantic prejudices: love should follow instinct; each person has one soulmate recognised by a special feeling; one should not need to explain it; true partners understand without language; you should not criticise or try to change a partner; reason is the enemy. Counter-view: treat "unromantic" as a prompt to question: talking about money, childhood patterns, families and compatibility belongs to a serious search for a workable relationship. An older view: love is an **education**, and lovers help each other grow.
- **Evidence**: philosophical and cultural argument. The historical story is simplified (general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: more deliberate partnering can lose spontaneity.
- **Does not hold when**: relationship harm exists; then the question is safety, not compatibility.

## 10.8 Childhood scripts and attachment
- **Thesis (position)**: childhood leaves an "emotional language" that we replay on innocent partners. Attachment research describes patterns such as avoidant behaviour (withdrawing when intimacy grows).
- **Mechanism**: historical observations of children separated from caregivers led to attachment research. A figure that about half of children are let down by caregivers and so many adults carry attachment wounds is **loosely stated and not verified**. Useful point: we cannot understand ourselves only alone, as we cannot see the back of our head without a mirror; other people, and sometimes professional help, give a view.
- **Evidence**: attachment theory has substantial research behind it (general knowledge), but popular typologies are simplified and individuals do not fit them neatly.
- **Trade-off**: labelling a partner or oneself can become an excuse.
- **Does not hold when**: you use the labels to diagnose. This skill does not give clinical or therapeutic advice; refer to `depth-psychology` for interpretive frameworks and to qualified professionals for treatment.

## 10.9 Create the right person; the good-enough partner
- **Thesis (position)**: instead of searching for the right person, work on being one; the compatible person is the one who works at compatibility.
- **Mechanism**: a playful early question: "how are you crazy?" (what do you struggle with); a person who can answer is safer than one who cannot. A good-enough partner does not assume you are wrong because there are problems, can repair crises, is curious, listens and is patient. The wrong partner stonewalls, blames and refuses responsibility. Speak in **therapeutically informed language** ("I hear your point of view, it may not be mine") instead of insults. Change takes long: a few weeks of effort is as short as six lessons of a new language.
- **Evidence**: practitioner and cultural commentary, consistent with research on couple communication (general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: patience can slide into tolerating a bad situation.
- **Does not hold when**: abuse, coercion or fear.

## 10.10 Judgment by career and the loss of buffers
- **Thesis (position)**: modern societies judge people by their achievements ("status love"); older societies had buffers: religions that value people for who they are, nature that decentres human judgment, and isolation.
- **Mechanism**: being surrounded by other people all the time and online strengthens the unitary sense that you are what you do. Secular culture can borrow the buffers.
- **Evidence**: cultural-historical argument.
- **Trade-off**: romanticises the past.
- **Does not hold when**: not applicable to everyone.

## 10.11 Bonding, urgency and blame in couples (position)
- **Thesis (position)**: partners attach and detach according to how the other makes them feel, so a pair is held together less by chemistry than by emotional presence, compromise and shared values (interests matter less than values), and it is maintained like a field, not found.
- **Mechanism**: urgency and over-intensity early ("love bombing") tend to push the other away; binding quickly after a break may signal difficulty being alone rather than readiness; assigning the whole fault to the other after a split protects the self but blocks learning; early chemistry fades, so the relevant question is what is built after it. Apps and feeds add continuous comparison and an always-open "option B", which lowers commitment to repair work. A partner cannot supply unconditional, parental love; expecting it is a script (10.8).
- **Evidence**: couple-communication research supports presence, repair and shared values (general knowledge); the claims about apps, the pandemic and weaker in-person skills are Observational and contested; the rest is Position.
- **Trade-off**: "work on it" can become tolerance of a bad situation.
- **Does not hold when**: abuse, coercion or fear; then the question is safety, not effort.

## 10.12 Adult friendship and being alone
- **Thesis (position)**: adult friendships form where life is actually shared (regular contexts, repeated contact), and a good friend is someone you can "be" with, not only "do" things with, who can say no to you.
- **Mechanism**: chat and notifications lower the push to meet in person; chosen solitude and feeling alone in company are different states, and the second tends to worsen when sufferers hide it, because showing pain is judged as weakness; opening up to trusted people or a professional discharges it. Isolation is not a form of health.
- **Evidence**: friendship formation by proximity and repeated contact is Experimental (general knowledge); the rest is Position/Practitioner. For kinds of loneliness and the withdrawal spiral see `depth-psychology` ch09.
- **Trade-off**: more contexts take time and tolerate awkwardness.
- **Does not hold when**: persistent low mood or withdrawal; refer to a professional.
