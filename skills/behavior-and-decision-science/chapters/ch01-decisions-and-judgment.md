# Chapter 01: Decisions and Judgment

## Core Idea
In this model, human judgment is shaped by three systematic distortions: **what we keep from an experience is a story, not a record**; **we weigh whatever we are thinking about too heavily**; and **we assume the person we will become wants what we want now**. A fourth factor, the motive behind reasoning (defending versus mapping), decides whether we can correct the first three. Evidence labels: see SKILL.md (experimental, observational, practitioner, position).

## 1.1 Experiencing self and remembering self
- **Thesis**: there are two different "selves" with different interests. The experiencing self lives moments and cannot be asked about them afterwards; the remembering self keeps score, tells the story of a life and is the one that decides.
- **Mechanism**: memory does not store the sum of moments. It stores a story built from changes, significant moments and endings. Most moments of life leave no trace at all (a psychological present of a few seconds, hundreds of millions of them in a life). Decisions are made between *memories of experiences*, and the future is imagined as *anticipated memories*, so the remembering self "has the voice" even when the experiencing self pays the cost.
- **Evidence**: experimental (retrospective pain ratings versus moment-by-moment ratings; a controlled extension of a painful procedure at a lower intensity produced a better memory and a worse total experience). Well replicated in its core form; strength of the effect varies with the kind of episode (general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: optimising for the story (best ending, a vivid peak) can raise what people feel afterwards while lowering the total lived experience. Optimising for lived moments ignores why people want a meaningful life story.
- **Does not hold when**: the experience is short and has no clear ending or peak, or the person rates it while it is still happening. A thought experiment in this model: would you pick the same holiday if every picture and memory of it were erased? If the answer changes, the two selves are in conflict and you must decide whose interest wins; there is no neutral rule.

## 1.2 Peak-end and duration neglect
- **Thesis**: remembered quality of an episode is driven by its most intense moment and its ending, and almost not at all by its duration.
- **Mechanism**: a longer episode that ends mildly can be remembered as better than a shorter one that ends at its worst. A second week of a holiday adds few new memories, so a two-week holiday is "barely better" in memory than one week while it is twice as long to live.
- **Evidence**: experimental. Replicated across painful medical episodes and other settings (general knowledge for the breadth).
- **Trade-off**: design the end deliberately (service, meeting, onboarding, farewell). But engineering the ending to fix memory can be used to mask a bad middle; this is an ethical choice, not a free trick.
- **Does not hold when**: people evaluate in the moment, or the ending is irrelevant to the decision (for example, an ongoing relationship).

## 1.3 Happiness is not one thing
- **Thesis (position)**: the word "happiness" lumps together at least two measurable things: how people feel while living (moment-to-moment emotion) and how satisfied they are when they evaluate their life. The two correlate only moderately (a correlation around 0.5 is stated), so knowing one tells you little about the other.
- **Mechanism**: life evaluation depends on goals, income, comparison and contrast; lived emotion depends mostly on who you spend time with and what you do. Time with people we like is the dominant source of positive lived emotion in this model.
- **Evidence**: observational survey data on very large samples. A reported finding that lived emotion stops improving above an income threshold while life evaluation keeps rising is **contested**: later large studies and adversarial re-analyses found a more complicated picture (general knowledge). Treat any specific threshold as dated.
- **Trade-off**: which measure a policy or a design should target is a value choice. Choosing only satisfaction can ignore daily suffering; choosing only moments can ignore purpose.
- **Does not hold when**: you use a single score to decide for a person; ask which kind of well-being is at stake.

## 1.4 The focusing illusion
- **Thesis**: you cannot think about one circumstance affecting well-being without exaggerating its importance. In the model this is "a cognitive trap with no way of getting it right", only ways of reducing it.
- **Mechanism**: when you picture moving to a sunny place, attention goes to the contrast (the climate) and ignores that daily life is made of the same activities everywhere. Later, people who moved will *think* they are happier whenever they compare, while their lived emotion did not change.
- **Evidence**: experimental and survey (judgments about other people's lives are more biased than self-reports). Well supported as a general effect; sizes vary (general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: noticing the illusion does not remove it. Practical counter: ask "what will I be doing, and with whom, in a normal week there?" instead of "how good is the feature?"
- **Does not hold when**: the circumstance really does occupy most of daily attention (chronic pain, a long commute, constant noise). Then it is not an illusion.

## 1.5 Scout mindset versus soldier mindset
- **Thesis (position)**: good judgment depends less on intelligence or on knowing rules of logic than on which emotional stance you are in: soldier mindset (defend a belief, win) or scout mindset (map what is really there, even if unpleasant).
- **Mechanism**: motivated reasoning is unconscious. Ideas feel like allies or enemies; evidence for allies is accepted loosely, evidence for enemies is examined hard (a call by an officiating referee against your team versus for it). The historical case of a nineteenth-century espionage conviction shows a whole institution finding thin evidence compelling and then explaining away contrary evidence. The stance that resists is built from feelings: curiosity, pleasure at learning, intrigue when expectations fail, and a self-worth that is not tied to being right on any single topic. Scouts see changing your mind as a virtue, not weakness.
- **Evidence**: motivated reasoning is experimentally well established. The claim that scout traits "do not correlate much with IQ" and that they predict good judgment is an interpretive claim from a researcher's own work and anecdote; treat it as a hypothesis (position).
- **Trade-off**: constant scouting is expensive and socially costly in groups that reward loyalty. A soldier stance is sometimes useful: persistence, morale, advocacy.
- **Does not hold when**: you are performing an advocacy role knowingly; then the problem is only mistaking advocacy for truth-seeking.

## 1.6 The illusion of continuity (future self)
- **Thesis**: people look back and see how much they changed, but look forward and expect to stay the same. This "illusion of continuity" leads to decisions, promises and rules that a future self may not want.
- **Mechanism**: the past contrast is vivid; the future change is amorphous. A planned preference (written in advance) can bind a later self whose situation, values and sense of what makes life worth living have changed, for example a pre-written instruction for a serious illness. The legal solution (follow the document) does not settle the ethical problem: the earlier self may not understand what the later self would want. The same logic applies to long promises, long sentences and old laws that once seemed enlightened.
- **Evidence**: practitioner narrative plus forecasting research (affective forecasting errors; general knowledge). The case history is an anecdote, not evidence of frequency.
- **Trade-off**: not binding yourself leaves you without commitment; binding yourself too tightly wrongs your future self. Three practices in this model: stay curious (shape who you become); add humility to certainty (your future self may disagree); be brave (your future self will have capacities you lack now).
- **Does not hold when**: the decision is genuinely irreversible and values are stable (a deliberate vow is exactly such a commitment). Then the point is to choose it knowingly.

## 1.7 Narrative, emotion and logic as two routes to a decision
- **Thesis (position)**: people are persuaded either by evidence and logic or by stories and what "people like me" do; someone who answers a data argument with a story is telling you which route they are on.
- **Mechanism**: switching to the other route (a story to someone who reasons by anecdote; data to someone who asks for facts) is a way of listening, not a trick. This links to ch09.
- **Evidence**: practitioner. Dual-route accounts exist in persuasion research (general knowledge), but the specific brain-location claims in popular sources are oversimplified.
- **Trade-off**: matching style can drift into manipulation; the test is whether you would be willing to say what you are doing.
- **Does not hold when**: the decision needs a calculation (dose, budget). Then the logic route is the only valid one.

## 1.8 Typical investor errors: mechanism and antidote
> Educational concepts, not investment, tax or legal advice.

The errors of this chapter have a well-known home in money decisions, where feedback is slow and noisy and the price moves every day. The table gives each error a mechanism and a pre-commitment antidote; none is a prediction or a recommendation.

| Error | Mechanism | Antidote |
|---|---|---|
| **FOMO (fear of missing out)** | Rising prices and stories of gains make the cost of staying out vivid; urgency is manufactured ("last chance"); social proof replaces analysis | A cooling-off rule decided in advance; "if a stranger offered this, would I pay?"; another opportunity will come |
| **Market timing** | Waiting for a dip or exiting on a scare requires two correct calls (out and back in); the best days cluster near the worst ones, so missing a few of them costs much of the total return (position: illustrative historical computations, regime-dependent) | Decide the entry and exit rule in advance, based on need for the cash, not on news |
| **Anchoring on prices** | The purchase price or the previous peak becomes the reference; "I will sell when I am back to even" confuses the market's reference with the holder's | Ask "would I buy this today at this price?"; compare against alternatives, not against cost |
| **Disposition effect** | Selling winners quickly (to lock in a pleasant gain) and holding losers (to avoid realising a loss); realising a loss feels like admitting error | Rules by purpose of the money and by review date, not by gain or loss; separate the decision from the memory of cost |
| **Overconfidence** | A few good outcomes in a rising market are read as skill; confidence grows with information, not accuracy; more trading follows and lowers net results through costs | Record predictions with dates and probabilities; compare with a plain benchmark after costs |
| **Product overload and over-diversification by count** | Many products give a feeling of diversification and activity; overlapping holdings, layered costs and complexity hide what is actually owned (choice overload, ch01 hand-offs) | List what each item does that the others do not; cut duplicates; prefer few, understandable instruments |
| **Recency and extrapolation** | The last year feels like the next ten (the illusion of continuity of 1.6 applied to markets) | Look at long, multi-regime histories; accept that regimes change |
| **Loss aversion and panic selling** | A fall feels worse than an equal rise feels good; selling ends the discomfort but crystallises the loss, especially if the horizon was long | Match money to horizon beforehand: money needed soon in safe assets, long-horizon money not forced to sell |

- **Thesis**: most losses come from a mismatch between the horizon of the money and the horizon of the product, plus emotion at the wrong moment, not from instrument choice.
- **Mechanism**: selling a ten-year product after three years crystallises a loss; forecasting does not work systematically because followers would remove the edge, and good news is already in prices; an investment pitched with urgency is a sales technique, not information.
- **Evidence**: behavioural-finance literature on disposition, overconfidence and trading volume is sizeable but effect sizes vary with sample and period; the market-timing numbers depend on the historical window (general knowledge, treat as illustrative).
- **Trade-off**: staying still can miss a rally and can also hold a failing asset; pre-commitment rules reduce impulsive errors at the price of flexibility.
- **Does not hold when**: a company or issuer has actually failed, or the money is needed sooner than planned; then selling is a decision about the facts, not an emotional error.
- **Hand-offs**: market and return arithmetic -> `investing-and-financial-history`; reading backtests -> `scientific-thinking` ch02 (2.9).

## Decision Checklist
1. Which self is deciding, and which self will pay?
2. What is the ending and what is the peak?
3. Which detail am I overweighting because I am thinking about it?
4. If this conclusion were false, what would I expect to see?
5. Would my future self, with different circumstances, resent this rule?
6. Is this money-decision driven by urgency, a past price or a recent run, rather than by the horizon of the money (1.8)?
