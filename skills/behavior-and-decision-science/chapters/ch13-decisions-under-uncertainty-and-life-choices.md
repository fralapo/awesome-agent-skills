# Chapter 13: Decisions Under Uncertainty and Life Choices

## Core Idea
Most important decisions are made before the outcome is known, so their quality has to be judged by the reasoning, the alternatives visible at the time and the size of the possible losses, not by how things turned out. This chapter adds mechanisms for that: separating luck from skill, sizing bets so that a bad run cannot end the game, treating life choices as design problems, and examining the motives behind a choice. It complements ch01 (judgment traps and the two selves) and ch11 (failure as learning). Nothing here is personal financial, legal or medical advice.

## 13.1 Judge the decision, not only the result
- **Thesis**: a good decision can produce a bad outcome and a bad decision can produce a good one. Learning requires separating the two.
- **Mechanism**: outcomes include chance. If a process is right 70% of the time, three bad outcomes in a row are still compatible with a good process. Judging by outcome rewards lucky bets and punishes careful ones, and teaches the wrong lesson.
- **Practice (decision record)**: before a consequential decision, write the question, the options considered, the main reasons, a rough probability for the key uncertainties, what you would expect to see if you were right, and a date to review. Compare with what happened, and separate what you could control from what you could not.
- **Evidence**: practitioner method; the principle is consistent with decision-analysis literature (general knowledge), but records themselves are not tested here.
- **Trade-off**: writing takes time and can make small decisions feel heavy; reserve it for decisions that are costly or hard to reverse.
- **Does not hold when**: the decision is trivial or reversible within minutes. Then just decide.

## 13.2 Ruin comes from sequences, not averages
- **Thesis**: in a repeated game, a positive average can still end in ruin if the bankroll is too small to survive the bad stretch that will eventually come.
- **Mechanism**: in a fair game against an opponent with unlimited resources, a player with finite capital is ruined with probability one if they keep playing forever. Even when the expected result is positive, the path can cross zero first. Over many rounds the frequency of wins converges to the probability, but the absolute deviation from the average grows (the typical deviation of a count of heads grows roughly with the square root of the number of flips). The lesson: with a fixed edge, the size of each bet and the reserve decide whether you survive long enough for the average to matter.
- **Evidence**: general knowledge (a standard probability result; simulations agree with it).
- **Trade-off**: small bets and reserves slow the upside; accepting a risk of ruin can be justified only if the ruin is survivable and the stake is affordable.
- **Does not hold when**: the outcome is not repeated and the stake can be absorbed by others. Single events cannot be averaged, so judge the downside directly.
- **Non-financial reading**: the same logic applies to committing all of one's time, savings or health to one bet. Ask what a bad sequence would do before the first step.

## 13.3 Small, fragile edges
- **Thesis**: an advantage that depends on a condition that can disappear is fragile, and its value must be discounted by the chance that the condition changes.
- **Mechanism**: a card-counting edge in a blackjack game is small and high-variance, and it depends on cards staying out of play until reshuffling. Automatic shuffling removes that condition, and venues can refuse players who win consistently. A strategy that works only while others do not notice has a limited shelf life.
- **Evidence**: general knowledge of the game; the size of the edge is approximate and depends on rules.
- **Trade-off**: fragile edges are not worthless; their value is smaller than the naive estimate.
- **Does not hold when**: the condition is structural and durable.

## 13.4 Expected value is not the typical path
- **Thesis**: the expected value of a choice describes an average over many similar choices. An individual faces one path, and the shape of the distribution (how likely a bad outcome is, how bad it is) can matter more than the average.
- **Mechanism**: with a small sample, the observed spread of results can differ from the theoretical variance, so a few observations can understate or overstate risk. A choice with a high expected value and a small chance of a catastrophic result may be worse for a person than a choice with a slightly lower average and no catastrophe.
- **Practice**: ask for the probability of a bad path and its size, not only the average. Estimate variance from theory or from a broader sample, not from a few data points.
- **Trade-off**: estimating tails is hard and easy to get wrong; state the uncertainty.
- **Does not hold when**: the stakes are small and repeated many times, where the average is the right guide.

## 13.5 Update from a neutral starting point
- **Thesis**: when a choice has no clear winner, starting from an explicit neutral prior and updating on specific evidence is more disciplined than starting from a hunch.
- **Mechanism**: Bayesian reasoning starts from a prior and multiplies it by how likely the evidence is under each hypothesis. A change of job, for example, can start at a neutral estimate of "better or worse", and each specific factor (pay, growth, stability, commute, the people) moves that estimate by an amount the person can justify. The final number is a judgment, not a measurement, so the reasoning matters more than the decimal.
- **Practice**: write the neutral start, the two or three factors that matter most, and the evidence for each. State which fact would change your estimate.
- **Trade-off**: explicit weights make hidden preferences visible, which can be uncomfortable; they also make the estimate easy to manipulate if the weights are chosen to fit the desired answer.
- **Does not hold when**: the factors are not comparable (for example, a value decision that has no shared scale). Then decide on the values directly.

## 13.6 Life choices are design problems
- **Thesis (position)**: decisions about where to live, with whom, and what work to do have no single optimum. They are better approached as design: prototype, test, talk to people who have already chosen, and run small experiments.
- **Mechanism**: a large decision bundles many variables that interact. A calculation cannot capture the lived experience of an ordinary week in a place or in a job. Prototypes (a month of living in the place, a trial project, a conversation with someone in the role) expose the everyday texture that a summary hides.
- **Negative criteria (position)**: it can help to identify what you want to avoid and rule out options that have it, because some options are easier to reject than to rank.
- **Status quo**: staying is a choice too. Anxiety about the new often weighs more than the destination itself, and everyday costs such as schools, housing and insurance are real frictions that deserve explicit estimates.
- **Trade-off**: prototypes take time and can be misleading if they are too short or too pleasant.
- **Does not hold when**: a decision is forced by a deadline or a legal requirement. Then the design method still helps with the question of how to make the best of the given options.

## 13.7 Minimising future regret is a guide, not the whole story
- **Thesis**: asking which choice you will regret least is useful, but it is incomplete and can be distorted.
- **Mechanism**: people predict regret poorly and often overweight the regret of inaction. Stated motives, even noble ones, can hide approval-seeking or vanity. A check: would the motive still be there if nobody knew about the choice?
- **Evidence**: regret research is mixed; regret-by-age surveys have strong survivorship bias (people who took big risks may not reach old age to answer, and many regrets are linked and cannot be isolated).
- **Trade-off**: examining motives can feel self-critical; it is also what stops a decision from being driven by a wish to be seen.
- **Does not hold when**: the motive is clearly good and the regret analysis confirms it; then go ahead.

## 13.8 Choice overload, option design and "good enough" (contested)
- **Thesis**: a very large number of options can make choosing harder and more likely to be deferred, and a single option removes agency. A small set of options and explicit personal rules help.
- **Mechanism**: comparing many alternatives raises the cognitive cost and the chance of regret. Setting criteria before browsing narrows the set. A standard of "good enough" stops the search when a threshold is met, while a maximiser keeps searching and often feels worse.
- **Evidence**: contested. The original effect of choice overload on purchase rates has replicated inconsistently, and the size depends on the domain and on whether people have a clear preference. The mechanism of criteria-first decisions is more robust than the headline effect.
- **Trade-off**: fewer options can hide a better one; a personal rule can become rigidity.
- **Does not hold when**: the choice is simple, or the person already knows what they want.

## 13.9 Selection in what we hear about decisions
- **Thesis**: advice from people who made a risky choice and succeeded is biased by who is around to speak. Survivorship bias applies to life advice as much as to investment.
- **Mechanism**: the people who failed are less visible, and their stories are often absent from the record. Rules such as "always follow your dream" or "always keep your options open" are heard from winners and rarely tested against losers.
- **Practice**: ask how many people tried a similar path and what the distribution of outcomes looked like, not only the best examples.
- **Trade-off**: base rates are hard to find for personal decisions.
- **Does not hold when**: the path is well documented with complete records (for example, a licensed professional training route with published pass rates).
