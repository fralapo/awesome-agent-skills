# Chapter 11: Failure and Learning

## Core Idea
Not all failure is equal. In this model, failure comes in three kinds, and only one is worth seeking: the **intelligent failure**, the small result of a good experiment in new territory. Learning from failure depends on coding the situation's stakes accurately, practising in safe environments and giving yourself permission to experiment. The classification is a practitioner framework, not an experimental result.

## 11.1 Three kinds of failure
- **Thesis**: avoid basic failures, catch complex failures early, and have more intelligent failures.
- **Mechanism**: (1) **Basic failure**: a simple error in known territory (spilled juice; a very large mis-sent payment). (2) **Complex failure**: several causes, a perfect storm (supply-chain breakdown from illness, weather and decisions). (3) **Intelligent failure**: the "right kind of wrong", where new knowledge comes from.
- **Evidence**: framework from organisational research and case studies (general knowledge); not an experimental taxonomy.
- **Trade-off**: classifying afterwards can be self-serving: "it was an intelligent failure".
- **Does not hold when**: the territory was known and the failure was due to carelessness.

## 11.2 Four criteria of an intelligent failure
- **Thesis**: a failure is intelligent when (1) it occurs in **new territory** where the knowledge for success does not exist, (2) it is in pursuit of a **goal**, (3) it is **hypothesis-driven** (you did your homework; it is not random trying) and (4) it is **as small as possible**, just large enough to learn from.
- **Mechanism**: think as a scientist: what do I hope to do, what progress do I want, what do I know, what do I not know, what might I try next to see what happens? Scientists on the leading edge expect to be wrong sometimes and treat each failure as a step toward discovery.
- **Evidence**: practitioner framework.
- **Trade-off**: small experiments teach less than big ones, but cost less.
- **Does not hold when**: the environment is high-risk (see 11.3).

## 11.3 Experiment where stakes are low or medium
- **Thesis**: experiment in simulators and labs, not in real flights or at the bedside.
- **Mechanism**: aviation uses simulators; healthcare uses labs. Intelligent failure is possible only where stakes are medium to low.
- **Evidence**: practice in safety-critical fields (general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: simulation never fully reproduces reality.
- **Does not hold when**: no safe environment exists for the experiment.

## 11.4 Code the stakes correctly
- **Thesis**: what holds us back from experiments is that we wrongly think the stakes are too high.
- **Mechanism**: "if we get it wrong, it is just wrong": inconvenient and disappointing, but usually not awful. A televised cook dropping an omelette and laughing codes the situation as low stakes, while most people would code it as catastrophic. Remind yourself of the true rational stakes: someone might laugh or disagree; so what?
- **Evidence**: practitioner; related to research on fear of negative evaluation and the spotlight effect (general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: underestimating stakes can be reckless.
- **Does not hold when**: social stakes are real (a hostile workplace where mistakes are punished). Psychological safety is a precondition (general knowledge).

## 11.5 Failure in the process of making things
- **Thesis (position)**: the same logic appears in creative work: a rough, embarrassing path (see ch08) leads to a result; sharing doubts and giving two options are themselves acts of experimentation.
- **Mechanism**: a fear of embarrassment drives work as much as ambition; a rejected shortcut idea left unsubmitted shows an integrity limit. Another example: after a bad first exam, changing method to competitive group retrieval practice (ch06) turned a worst grade into one of the best.
- **Evidence**: anecdotes.
- **Trade-off**: personal stories do not generalise.
- **Does not hold when**: the failure reflects a mismatch you cannot fix.

## 11.6 Process over outcome in learning
- **Thesis (position)**: to learn something, do the process, because feedback from doing answers most questions; wanting only the result is a sign you do not want the thing (ch10.6).
- **Mechanism**: shipping many small attempts, getting feedback from them, and then asking is more informative than asking first.
- **Evidence**: practitioner.
- **Trade-off**: attempts cost time and visibility.
- **Does not hold when**: a single failure is irreversible.

## 11.7 Failure review checklist
1. Which of the three kinds was it?
2. Was there a hypothesis? What did we expect?
3. What did we learn, and what do we now know that we did not?
4. Was it as small as it could be?
5. Were the stakes coded correctly, before and after?
6. What is the next, smaller, experiment?

## 11.8 Persistence advice and survivorship
- **Thesis (position)**: "never give up" is too generic: it ignores that stories of persistence are told by those who made it, and it makes the person who stops feel weak.
- **Mechanism**: survivorship bias hides everyone who worked just as hard and did not arrive; luck and starting conditions are invisible in the stories that get told. Stopping a harmful relationship, a dead-end plan or a sunk cost can be the healthy choice; paths are not linear, and what matters is consistency in what is worth continuing and love for the activity, not endurance for its own sake. A slogan like "improve by one per cent a day" fails because difficult days break the compounding arithmetic; motivation follows action, and bad days can be lived without forcing positivity.
- **Evidence**: survivorship bias is a well-described general effect; the one-per-cent compounding claim is a metaphor, not a measured law; the rest is Position.
- **Trade-off**: permission to quit can become a way out of every discomfort; use the failure checklist (11.7) to tell a dead end from a hard stretch.
- **Does not hold when**: the goal is genuinely valuable and the obstacle is temporary and learnable; then persistence is correct.
