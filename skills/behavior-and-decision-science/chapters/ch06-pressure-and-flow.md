# Chapter 06: Performance Under Pressure and Flow

## Core Idea
Performance fails under pressure mostly because **attention goes to the wrong place** (over-monitoring skills that should run on autopilot) and because **worry consumes working memory**. The remedy is to practise under the conditions of performance, to offload worries and to shape the environment. Flow is the opposite condition: attention fully in the present. The evidence for the mechanisms of choking is stronger than the evidence for the size of flow effects.

## 6.1 Choking is over-attention
- **Thesis**: under evaluation, people try to control steps that are best left outside conscious awareness, and this "paralysis by analysis" lowers performance.
- **Mechanism**: attention is limited. Asking skilled athletes to attend to a detail of their movement (which side of the foot touches the ball) made them slower and more error-prone. Skilled shooters and performers describe the best performance as not thinking. A pressure episode begins with an evaluation signal (an important observer), then self-focus, then loss of fluency.
- **Evidence**: experimental, replicated in motor skills; the paralysis-by-analysis mechanism is a standard finding (general knowledge for related results). Some replications of related "stereotype threat" work are weaker; not needed here.
- **Trade-off**: beginners must think about steps; the rule applies to well-learned skills.
- **Does not hold when**: the task is novel or requires deliberate reasoning (a new proof, unfamiliar tool); then attention helps.

## 6.2 Unhook the prefrontal cortex
- **Thesis (position)**: you can take attention off over-control with simple distractors.
- **Mechanism**: singing a song, or focusing on an unrelated body part (a toe), is said to keep the mind off step-by-step details. Practising a skill under conditions closer to performance narrows the gap between training and competition.
- **Evidence**: practitioner tips supported by experiments on attention and choking (general knowledge); the golfing anecdote is only rumoured.
- **Trade-off**: distraction techniques can interfere if misused.
- **Does not hold when**: the skill needs continuous monitoring (for example during surgery or flight; use checklists, not distraction).

## 6.3 Practise under the conditions you will perform in
- **Thesis**: rarely do we rehearse in the conditions of performance, which is why we flub when all eyes are on us.
- **Mechanism**: for exams, close the book, retrieve answers from memory, with time limits, and practise with peers who compete for the right answer. For talks, practise before an audience, a camera or a mirror. A student who changed from solitary study to competitive group retrieval went from the lowest score in a large class to one of the highest.
- **Evidence**: experimental research supports retrieval practice and pressure training (general knowledge); the personal story is anecdote.
- **Trade-off**: realistic practice is uncomfortable.
- **Does not hold when**: stakes are lethal; use simulators (see ch11).

## 6.4 Write down worries before the event
- **Thesis**: expressive writing about an upcoming stressful event can free working memory.
- **Mechanism**: putting thoughts and worries on paper before the event "downloads" them so they pop up less in the moment, like writing a to-do list at night to be able to sleep.
- **Evidence**: experimental in test-taking research (general knowledge); the instruction is practical advice; the underlying data are not reproduced here.
- **Trade-off**: ten minutes of preparation.
- **Does not hold when**: writing turns into rumination (repeating without resolution).

## 6.5 Environment: contagious anxiety
- **Thesis**: anxiety about a subject spreads from adults to children.
- **Mechanism**: brain imaging found that people who are anxious about maths show activity in regions linked to physical pain when merely anticipating a maths test. Children of anxious teachers or parents who help a lot with homework learn less in a school year. Social talk ("I am not a maths person") makes it acceptable to fail. Doing playful maths with parents instead of worksheets improved children's attitudes and results across the school year.
- **Evidence**: observational, imaging and an intervention study; causal interpretation is partial. Gender differences described for one country are specific to that culture and period.
- **Trade-off**: parents are not to blame; it is an environment to shape.
- **Does not hold when**: a learning difficulty is the root; then anxiety is secondary and needs different support.

## 6.6 What flow is
- **Thesis**: flow is an altered state in which a person performs at their best and feels it: sense of self diminishes, time dilates, action feels effortless and autotelic (done for itself), control is high.
- **Mechanism**: it is reportedly consistent across people. It requires attention in the present. Researchers have identified a list of triggers (22 are reported); they all drive attention into the now, by raising dopamine, raising norepinephrine or lowering cognitive load. Novelty is named as a dopamine trigger; other triggers in the list are not detailed here. Clear goals and immediate feedback are commonly cited focus triggers (general knowledge).
- **Evidence**: the phenomenology is well established by interview and questionnaire (general knowledge); the neurochemical and the "22 triggers" accounts are less certain and come from one research lab; **treat the list as a working hypothesis**.
- **Trade-off**: flow is a state you can prepare for, not command.
- **Does not hold when**: tasks are routine or too easy or too hard; flow follows a balance between challenge and skill (general knowledge).

## 6.7 Preparing for flow: motivation stack and focus block
- **Thesis (position)**: motivation works as a sequence: extrinsic rewards, then curiosity, passion, purpose, autonomy and mastery; each helps hold attention.
- **Mechanism**: passion supplies "free focus"; purpose links it to something outside you and brings pro-social neurochemistry; purpose demands autonomy and then mastery. The brain is said to have a 90 to 120 minute focus cycle similar to sleep cycles; align a block of uninterrupted work with your own rhythm (lark or owl), after distraction management in advance (phone, email and social media off).
- **Evidence**: practitioner; the ultradian-cycle claim is plausible but weakly supported (general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: a long block requires protected time and may not be possible in interrupt-driven jobs.
- **Does not hold when**: you do collaborative support work; there, availability is the job.

## 6.8 Flow claims: handle with caution (contested)
- **Thesis (position)**: flow is said to multiply productivity, learning and creativity by several times (figures like 400 to 700 percent for creativity, up to 500 percent for productivity, 240 to 500 percent for learning are quoted).
- **Mechanism**: evolution is said to have tied flow to cooperation and innovation: when resources are scarce, cooperate and create. Group flow is said to raise collaboration and empathy.
- **Evidence**: **weak and contested**. The large percentages are attributed to a consultancy study and to other studies whose details are unverified; they have not been independently verified here. Use the mechanism qualitatively, not the numbers.
- **Trade-off**: promising, but a risk of overselling a state that cannot be forced.
- **Does not hold when**: you use "flow" as a sales promise.
- **No clinical use**: claims about treating addiction, heartbreak or trauma with high-intensity experiences are not supported in this skill and are not advice.
