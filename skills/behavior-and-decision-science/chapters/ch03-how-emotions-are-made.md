# Chapter 03: How Emotions Are Made

## Core Idea
In this model, an emotion is not a reflex triggered by an ancient circuit; it is a **prediction the brain constructs in the moment** from past experience, the body's state and the context. This view, held by one research programme, is **contested** by other approaches (general knowledge). Even its proponents note that scientists do not agree on how to define emotion. Treat it as a useful working model with practical implications, not settled fact.

## 3.1 Two myths and the alternative
- **Thesis (position)**: two popular beliefs are wrong in this model: that emotions are hardwired, universal circuits that "happen to you", and that a rational part of the brain must defeat an animal part.
- **Mechanism**: the brain constantly regulates the body (glucose, oxygen, salt and other conditions) and receives sensory summaries. Those summaries reach awareness only as simple feelings: pleasant or unpleasant, calm or worked up. They are features of an emotion episode, not emotions themselves. An emotion is a story the brain builds that links those sensations to what is happening around you, using concepts learned earlier.
- **Evidence**: the claim rests on a body of experimental and imaging reviews by one research group; the strongest counter-view treats some basic emotions as universal and biologically grounded (general knowledge). Open dispute.
- **Trade-off**: gains a lever (you can change inputs) at the cost of a strong claim that other researchers reject.
- **Does not hold when**: you take it as proof that feelings are "not real". The sensations are real; the interpretation is built.

## 3.2 Prediction and experiential blindness
- **Thesis**: perception works by prediction. An ambiguous image is meaningless until a missing piece of knowledge arrives; after that, you cannot "unsee" it.
- **Mechanism**: the brain sifts a lifetime of experience for what the input is *most like*, weighs probabilities and constructs the experience before you are aware of it. Prediction also explains how you understand words as they are spoken. New knowledge changes what the same input feels like.
- **Evidence**: experimental (perceptual priming is well established; general knowledge for the breadth). The extension to emotion is the contested step.
- **Trade-off**: speed and efficiency against error: you perceive what you expect.
- **Does not hold when**: input is strong and unambiguous; prediction then matters less.

## 3.3 The same body signal, different emotions
- **Thesis**: a churning stomach is hunger in a bakery and dread in a hospital waiting room.
- **Mechanism**: the sensation is identical; the context supplies the category. Intense distress on waking, followed by a mind that races through reasons, is the brain searching for a cause for a sensation that may be purely physical.
- **Evidence**: illustrative example, with experimental support from misattribution studies (general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: reattributing can help or mislead.
- **Does not hold when**: a medical condition is present; sensations need care, not reinterpretation.

## 3.4 Faces and bodies do not carry emotion by themselves
- **Thesis (position)**: movements of the face and body have no intrinsic emotional meaning; observers (people or machines) supply meaning from context and from their own head.
- **Mechanism**: a stone-faced stare can be remorselessness or stoic acceptance depending on a culture's prescribed behaviour. A smile can mean sadness, a cry joy. A detection system that reads only faces and bodies asks, in this model, the wrong question: emotion meaning lives in the link between movement and context.
- **Evidence**: this contradicts a long tradition of universal facial expressions; evidence from both camps exists, and the dispute is active (general knowledge). A courtroom example is illustrative only.
- **Trade-off**: be wary of confident emotion-reading in legal, hiring or security settings; but do not conclude that expressions carry no information.
- **Does not hold when**: the context is well known and shared; expressions then predict a lot.

## 3.5 You are the architect of your experience (limited form)
- **Thesis (position)**: since emotion is built from past experience, new experiences become tomorrow's predictions. You cannot reach back and change past meanings (that is the work of therapy), but you can change your present inputs.
- **Mechanism**: learn new concepts, read, watch, act in unfamiliar situations, practise reinterpreting arousal (ch04). Repetition makes the new patterns more automatic, like learning to drive.
- **Evidence**: experimental support for reappraisal training (see ch04); the broader claim is a model. Individual control varies: the model says everyone has "a little more control than they think", not total control.
- **Trade-off**: more control means more responsibility: not blame for what you feel, but ownership of what can be changed.
- **Does not hold when**: you are using this to dismiss serious conditions. In this model, depression is described as the brain's attempt to save metabolic cost (fatigue, difficulty concentrating, not moving), which does not mean the cause is "just in your head"; this skill gives no clinical advice.

## 3.6 Emotional granularity and learning
- **Thesis (position)**: a richer set of emotion concepts lets the brain predict more specifically.
- **Mechanism**: unpleasant feelings are diagnostic only sometimes: they can indicate something wrong in the world, uncertainty, or that you are doing something hard. Part of managing emotion is telling these apart.
- **Evidence**: the idea is only suggested in this model; the granularity research is a separate literature (general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: more vocabulary does not replace action.
- **Does not hold when**: no new concepts are learned; labels alone do not change behaviour.
