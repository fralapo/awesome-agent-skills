# Chapter 10: Ideologies, Movements and Episodes as Cases with Contested Causes

## Core Idea
Ideologies and movements are easy to turn into morality plays. Treat each as a case: what the doctrine says, what the actors did, what conditions made it possible, who tells the story and what is disputed. This chapter holds short case sheets. Each has Thesis, Mechanism, Trade-off and "Does not hold when"; causes are listed as **contested** where historians disagree. Some cases are covered only in outline and political labels are used as descriptive categories, with no judgement on current parties. The collapse of ancient societies has its own skill (`history-geopolitics`); here only items that train source criticism are included.

## 10.1 Communism: from ideal to regimes
- **Thesis (position)**: a doctrine meant for advanced industrial societies was applied where there was no large working class, and the result diverged from the theory.
- **Mechanism**: the idea of shared property and rulers who govern for the people is ancient (utopias in philosophy and religious writing); it clashed with the human wish for money and power. Industrial capitalism in the nineteenth century (investment of trade profits, new inequality, factory work) gave it a modern form (ch03). In the first regime, a late-industrialising country forced the stages of modernisation (ch05.8); a party took the place of a working class. The doctrine then had to adapt: in a peasant country the revolution placed peasants at the centre and a party as guide; a campaign of collective farming in the late 1950s was followed by a famine that killed at least 15 million (an estimate treated as a lower bound), and the split with the first communist state followed. Elsewhere regimes ranged from a federation that went its own way to a harsh isolated dictatorship; large communist parties in Western Europe accepted democratic rules. Support for allied movements in Asia, Africa and Latin America drained the first state's resources (a regime that spent on army and allies at the expense of civil growth).
- **Trade-off**: a doctrine with strong appeal against inequality; in practice, concentrated power and planning failures.
- **Does not hold when**: "communism" refers to all of these as one thing; separate theory, party-states, planned economies and parties inside democracies.
- **Counterargument**: defenders argue that the regimes betrayed the theory; critics argue that the theory's disregard of incentives and pluralism made the outcome likely. Both are positions; ch03.5 and ch03.6 give the mechanisms.

## 10.2 Anarchism: a family, not one position
- **Thesis**: anarchy (the principle: no coercive rulers) and anarchism (the movements trying to bring it about) are different things, and the family spans both left and right.
- **Mechanism**: the nineteenth-century movement shared a target with a rival socialist doctrine (the owning class) but differed on the means and the aim: one aimed to capture means of production and pass through a dictatorship of the working class; the other aimed to abolish all centres of power (economic, cultural, religious and above all the state, whether authoritarian or democratic) and to go to a phase of free experiment: a decentralised society of federated groups of all sizes, based on mutual aid (cooperation as a factor in evolution). The "workers" in this view included peasants, labourers and the unemployed. Elected governments, in this view, still represent part of the people and impose the work of the many on the interests of rulers. Methods: peaceful organisation or insurrectionary violence. A twentieth-century strand, anarcho-capitalism (a market that replaces every public service; taxes as theft), is classed as right-libertarian; the paradox is that a politician who campaigns on it may take the top office of a state.
- **Trade-off**: a radical critique of concentrated power; unclear answers on how to provide public goods and enforce rules without a state.
- **Does not hold when**: "anarchist" is used as a synonym for violence or for the far left only.
- **Counterargument**: critics say stateless societies are unstable or dominated by the strong (position).

## 10.3 From a defeated empire to an authoritarian takeover
- **Thesis (position)**: the "they won legally" story is partly true and misleading; the takeover followed years of crisis and blame-shifting.
- **Mechanism**: military defeat in a world war led military and nationalist leaders to blame a "stab in the back" by internal enemies (an alibi that hid strategic errors and structural problems). The peace terms (loss of colonies, occupation of territory, army limited to about 100,000 men, large reparations) were harsh; nationalists used them to promise national dignity. Several causes combined in the hyperinflation: war debt financed by borrowing and money creation instead of taxes, large domestic budget deficits, reparations and the need to buy foreign currency, and the passive resistance of 1923 against the occupation, which the state financed by printing money; more money in circulation made the currency lose value, so imports became dearer and wages bought less. The price of a loaf rose from well under a mark at war's end (roughly half a mark) to about 160 marks in 1922 and hundreds of billions in 1923 (approximate, as reported). When two neighbours occupied the industrial border region in 1923, the occupation inflamed opinion; a small party in a southern region, led by a charismatic orator in beer-hall rallies, grew within the nationalist front. The head of government was appointed on 30 January 1933 by constitutional procedure; a vote is not a licence to remove opposition afterwards.
- **Trade-off**: a conspiracy story relieves leaders of responsibility; a structural story demands attention to institutions.
- **Does not hold when**: a single cause is offered (inflation, treaty, one man). Not covered here: the economic depression after 1929, the electoral steps and the emergency powers; consult other sources.
- **Position**: causal order is contested; the treaty's role in later events is much debated (see also ch03.7 on ideology).

## 10.4 A secret network and the capture of institutions
- **Thesis (position)**: an informal network that places members in intelligence, army, press, finance and politics can matter more than its formal size, and the central historical difficulty is proving what it caused.
- **Mechanism**: in a Western democracy of the late 1970s, a masonic lodge ran in secret; when its membership lists were found in 1981 they included heads of the intelligence services, a fleet admiral, the head of a financial police, politicians, ministry managers, university professors and chief physicians in large numbers (as reported, approximate), bankers, journalists, television executives and entertainers. The lodge's leader hinted that members numbered 2,500, so the list may have been incomplete (unclear). Many unresolved events of the previous decade (a "strategy of tension", coup attempts, state-linked massacres, a political kidnapping, governmental instability) were already shelved without culprits or masterminds. A "rebirth plan" aimed at reshaping the democratic order is attributed to the lodge. Credit and property deals were said to have benefited from members in banks. Individuals described their own membership as formal or social ("a gentlemen's club") while the leader described initiation rites; the parliamentary inquiry is the reference for such statements.
- **Trade-off**: documenting networks is possible; proving a network's role in specific events is harder.
- **Does not hold when**: used to explain everything ("a hidden hand") or to dismiss it entirely. Method: list documented facts (lists, rulings, inquiry findings), then claims that follow from them, and mark the gap.
- **Handoff**: no named individuals are discussed here; for any real person use primary court and parliamentary documents.

## 10.5 The decision to use new weapons: a debate in structure (first part only)
- **Thesis (position)**: the question "was it necessary?" cannot be answered without reconstructing alternatives and each party's information.
- **Mechanism**: two positions exist. One says the bombs shortened a war and avoided a costlier invasion (a spiral of "death and blood" for the other side). The other says they were unjustified, a crime against a nation that was already beaten and a demonstration of force. One analysis holds that the truth is not "in the middle"; the later phases of the debate are not covered here. The background: a forced opening in 1853 under naval threat and an unequal treaty humiliated an island state, which then modernised fast (constitution, industry, army) and expanded for half a century (a neighbour, a peninsula, a puppet state on the mainland); the dilemma was to maintain a war economy to unite a poor population or to give up the dream of empire; the militarists chose a larger war for iron and coal in a continental neighbour, and the sanctions and embargo that followed pushed it towards seizing oil (see ch06.3).
- **Trade-off**: an analysis of alternatives is hard but necessary; slogan positions are easy.
- **Does not hold when**: moral and historical arguments are mixed without saying which is being made.
- **Not covered in the sample**: the decision itself, the surrender timeline, and the postwar discussion.

## 10.6 A coalition of raiders: a label that became a cause
- **Thesis**: a modern label can create an apparent single actor from heterogeneous groups.
- **Mechanism**: the term "Sea Peoples" was coined in the mid-nineteenth century by a scholar studying temple reliefs, but the Egyptian texts name individual groups and do not use that term. The first explicit mention dates to about 1208 BCE (year five of a pharaoh), when raiders appeared as allies of western neighbours; a list names nine groups of debated origin (islands, western Mediterranean, Anatolia, Crete); a royal letter mentions a prisoner from a people "who live on ships". The widespread collapse of palace societies at the end of the Bronze Age is real; the claim that one invader caused it is debated (see `history-geopolitics`).
- **Trade-off**: a simple name helps teaching; it hides diversity.
- **Does not hold when**: the label is treated as an ethnic identity.

## 10.7 The siege of a dying capital: incentives behind a heroic story 
- **Thesis**: a famous defender may have been paid and promised titles, not moved by ideals, and a symbolic role may exceed a military one.
- **Mechanism**: in 1453 a capital with a small garrison faced an enemy with a very large numerical advantage (as reported, approximate); the Western powers had mostly forgotten it; the only concrete help came as a few ships, mercenaries and small units. The mercenary captain who commanded the defence was offered an island lordship, gold and honours; friendship with the ruler existed but did not imply blind loyalty. The sultan's navy was large, with ship counts that vary by source (as reported, approximate).
- **Trade-off**: a heroic narrative gives meaning; an incentive reading gives realism.
- **Does not hold when**: used to judge individuals' courage; the incentives and the courage are compatible.

## 10.8 Fragmentation invites intervention
- **Thesis**: a region of many rival small states with mercenary armies is easily invaded and exploited.
- **Mechanism**: in the late fifteenth century, an invasion of a peninsula of rival principalities began with about 30,000 men and strong artillery; some local rulers allied with the invader to protect themselves or use him against neighbours; a reputation for brutality ("fury") served as psychological terror to break resistance; the war began a series of eight conflicts there. The invader's dynastic claim was a pretext; the underlying cause was an opportunity created by division, plus the weakness of a neighbour's attention elsewhere.
- **Trade-off**: autonomy of each unit against weakness of the whole.
- **Does not hold when**: the local states are able to form a durable coalition.

## 10.9 "Mad" rulers: reading hostile sources
- **Thesis**: a story of a deranged ruler is usually a claim from the ruler's enemies.
- **Mechanism**: three first-century emperors have a literary image of pride, hatred of the senate, extravagance, cruelty, perversion and mental illness; all died violently, and their memory was condemned by the senate. Writers of the time and later were from the senatorial elite hostile to them. For one alleged incest, two nearly contemporary authors who hated him do not mention it; a biographer writing about eight decades after the reign does. The modern idea of "imperial madness" was constructed in the nineteenth century and applied to contemporary rulers. These texts are read with caution; they are often partisan.
- **Trade-off**: skepticism about hostile sources can lead to excusing real cruelty.
- **Does not hold when**: independent evidence (inscriptions, coins, archaeology) supports a claim.
- **Method**: ask who wrote, when, for whom, and what they gained; ask what independent evidence exists.

## Diagnostics
1. State the doctrine and the practice separately.
2. List conditions (defeat, inflation, fragmentation) and triggers.
3. Ask who tells the story and why; look for independent evidence.
4. Keep documented facts, inferences and mythic claims in three columns.
5. No collective guilt: a nation or ideology is not a single actor.
