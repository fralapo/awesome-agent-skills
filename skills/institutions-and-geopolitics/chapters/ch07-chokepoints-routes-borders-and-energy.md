# Chapter 7: Chokepoints, Trade Routes, Inherited Borders and Energy Dependence

## Core Idea
A route is a lifeline; a chokepoint is where a lifeline narrows to a width one actor can threaten. Borders inherited from empires carry disputes into the present. Energy exports and imports tie fiscal health to a few routes. This chapter gives the analysis units (route, chokepoint, bypass, dependence) and the cases. Statements about recent events are dated snapshots, flagged as **position** or unverified; use them as examples of mechanism, not as news.

## 7.1 Chokepoint logic
- **Thesis**: a narrow passage lets a weaker actor hold a stronger one at risk, and gives the user a reason to build bypasses.
- **Mechanism**: a blockade of an island needs a ring of ships; a blockade of a state whose coast lies behind one strait needs only to close the strait. Measures that matter: width (21 miles at the narrowest point for one Gulf strait; about 28 km for a Red Sea strait; about one and a half miles for a southeast Asian strait), share of flows (roughly a fifth of world oil and gas through the first; about two thirds of one large country's seaborne trade through the third, in one analysis), alternatives (pipelines, canals, longer sea routes) and who can interdict (navy, missiles, drones, mines, small boats, cyber).
- **Trade-off**: chokepoints make trade cheap and concentrated; the same concentration is the risk.
- **Does not hold when**: bypasses carry most of the flow or the interdicting actor depends on the same flow.

## 7.2 Mutual-hostage deterrence and its assumptions
- **Thesis (position)**: for decades, analysts held that a state would not close a strait because it would hurt its own exports and invite a response.
- **Mechanism**: a state that gets about a third of its government revenue from exports through the strait would suffer from its own blockade. One analysis (a snapshot, unverified here, about a conflict said to occur in 2026) claims that this assumption failed: once the regime felt it had nothing to lose, it used cheap mass-produced drones, small craft and mines to hold traffic hostage for months, and a larger naval force did not reopen it; the lesson drawn was that producers around the strait would have to reduce dependence on it. Treat this as one account of an event; check later reporting.
- **Trade-off**: asymmetric cheap tools reduce the value of a naval advantage.
- **Does not hold when**: the actor has not reached the point of regime-survival risk, where deterrence assumptions normally apply. The key variable is what the actor stands to lose.
- **Bypass logic**: exporting states build pipelines to ports outside the strait (one built an east-west pipeline during an earlier tanker war in the 1980s) and port capacity on other coasts; the cost is capital and a new vulnerability at the pipeline and the new port.

## 7.3 Canals: tolls, drought and flows
- **Thesis**: a canal is a shortcut whose capacity is limited by water, locks and politics.
- **Mechanism**: one canal in the Americas handles about 5% of seaborne trade and about 40% of one large country's container traffic in the case; the alternative route round the southern cape adds about 8,000 nautical miles and 18 days. During a drought the number of daily transits can be cut (a headline claim says the canal is "dying"; the mechanism is that the canal uses fresh water from a lake fed by rain). The same canal had value for rapid naval redeployment between oceans, as in a past blockade operation. The owner state gains toll revenue and leverage; handing the canal back to the territorial state after decades is an example of a strategic asset changing sovereign (1979 to 2000 in the case).
- **Trade-off**: a canal reduces travel time and depends on climate and maintenance.
- **Does not hold when**: new routes (an Arctic route, overland rail) become competitive.
- **Linked chokepoint**: when attacks on shipping at a Red Sea strait forced ships to use the long route round a continent, shipping costs and times rose; two canals and straits are linked, so a shock in one raises traffic in the other.

## 7.4 A tiny state with many bases
- **Thesis (position)**: a small state at a chokepoint can rent its location to many powers.
- **Mechanism**: a country on the western side of the Red Sea strait hosts the only permanent base of one superpower on the continent, plus bases of several other major states, including the first overseas bases of two Asian powers (as of 2023, with the highest per-capita concentration of foreign bases in the world). Reasons: all shipping between the Mediterranean (via a canal) and the Indian Ocean passes within 28 km, and the Gulf holds about half of known oil reserves and a third of production, so protecting the route matters to dozens of states. The small state earns rent and security, and risks being a theatre for others' rivalries.
- **Trade-off**: income and protection against dependence and loss of autonomy.
- **Does not hold when**: the small state's leaders cannot balance patrons or domestic opposition grows.

## 7.5 The "dilemma" of a trade-dependent power
- **Thesis (position)**: a state that imports most of its energy by sea through a narrow passage controlled by others feels permanently exposed.
- **Mechanism**: a large industrial state sends most of its seaborne trade and a large share of oil through one narrow strait; to push the first line of defence away from its coast it claims most of an adjacent sea (a "nine-dash line" covering about 90%), builds artificial islands with bases, and builds pipelines and land routes. Rival coastal states and a naval superpower reject this and refer to the 1982 law of the sea (12-mile territorial waters, 200-mile exclusive economic zones, free passage). The two maps cannot both hold, which produces risk of incidents.
- **Trade-off**: more depth and options at the cost of regional alarm and counter-alliances.
- **Does not hold when**: energy demand is diversified (domestic production, renewables, overland imports) or the strait is not interdictable.
- **Counterargument**: the state's claims also rest on history and nationalism, not only on supply security.

## 7.6 Energy exporters: strength and weakness at once
- **Thesis**: an exporter that funds the state from one customer region is dependent on that customer.
- **Mechanism**: in the case, a large exporter got about 40 to 50% of its government budget and about 30% of GDP from energy, about half of oil exports and nearly three quarters of gas exports flowed west; the customer region got about a third of its gas and a quarter of its oil from it (about a fifth of total energy needs; dated, pre-2022 estimates). Each side holds the other as a hostage; after a conflict the customer sought other suppliers and the exporter's revenue base shifted. The same text interprets many foreign-policy actions (wars in neighbouring states, annexation) by the calculation that the regional pipeline networks and a buffer belt with former republics matter for the budget (position). Another country, a big importer, is described as catastrophically dependent on oil and gas imports through chokepoints.
- **Trade-off**: revenue and political leverage against vulnerability to demand and sanctions.
- **Does not hold when**: the exporter diversifies customers (pipelines east, liquefied gas).
- **Counterargument**: explaining policy by pipelines alone omits security, identity and domestic politics.

## 7.7 Inherited borders from an empire's collapse
- **Thesis (position)**: when an empire is divided by victors for their own strategic needs, the successor states inherit lines that do not match peoples.
- **Mechanism**: in one analysis, a multi-ethnic empire that had suppressed nationalism was dismantled after a world war by two victors under a secret agreement (1916) that carved zones of influence; the victors held a land bridge to their colony and a canal; the resulting states combined rival communities (or split one), some under imposed monarchs or mandates. Consequence: repeated wars since, with many outside powers involved, millions dead or displaced. The counter-caveat: borders are only one factor; religion, oil, regime competition and outside interventions also drive wars, and several states with similar borders stayed stable.
- **Trade-off**: simple partition lines for the imperial power; later instability for the people.
- **Does not hold when**: used as the single explanation ("forever wars"); the same lines have held in some places.

## 7.8 Undemarcated frontiers and claim maps
- **Thesis**: a frontier through high mountains and glaciers is hard to mark, so a de facto line of control substitutes for a border and each side claims more.
- **Mechanism**: in the case, a frontier of about 3,400 km between two populous states has never been demarcated; the de facto line has three sectors, with claimed land the size of a small European country; one side issues stapled visas to residents of the area it claims, to avoid recognising the other's sovereignty; a small kingdom between them is said to have lost territory to creeping occupation and road building. A new official map extending claims became a point of friction. Both sides keep tens of thousands of troops at altitude. Infrastructure races (roads, bridges, airfields) increase the chance of clashes.
- **Trade-off**: ambiguity avoids a final concession; it perpetuates risk.
- **Does not hold when**: a settlement is reached by exchanging claims.
- **Another case**: a territory claimed by two nuclear-armed neighbours and bordered by a third; an attack by militants was said to have been followed by closed borders, suspended visas and a threat to restrict river flows (the case recounts a 2025 episode; treat as a dated snapshot). River water control as a threat is described by the target as an act of war; this links geography (headwaters) to coercion.
- **Trade-off**: escalation ladders in nuclear dyads are short.
- **Does not hold when**: the question is moral: this description of the dispute does not settle either side's claims (treat as description of a risk, not a verdict).

## 7.9 Demographic and climate-linked geography
- **Thesis**: where people live is a strategic fact; a population concentrated in a few cities or one coast exposes the state to blockade or disaster.
- **Mechanism**: examples here include a capital region with three in ten citizens; coastal plains holding most people; a country whose population fraction in cities is low. Disasters (earthquake, tsunami, drought) and shortages can create weaknesses that an adversary could use.
- **Trade-off**: concentration gives efficiency; dispersal gives resilience.
- **Does not hold when**: infrastructure is redundant.
