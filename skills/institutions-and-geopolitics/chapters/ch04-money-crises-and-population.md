# Chapter 4: Money, Crises, Inflation and Population Claims

## Core Idea
Crises recur because credit, expectations and institutions interact. This chapter gives the standard mechanisms (inflation measures, bubbles, bank runs, mortgage securitisation, deflation traps) and shows how institutional design (deposit safety, regulation, central bank behaviour, trade rules) changed the damage. A final section covers an old population theory and its policy misuse. All figures are dated case estimates.

## 4.1 Measuring inflation
- **Thesis**: a rise in prices is the same as a cut in purchasing power; it is measured by pricing a fixed basket.
- **Mechanism**: fix a basket of typical goods (rent, fuel, bread, tickets), price it each year, pick a base year, divide each year's cost by the base cost and multiply by 100: the price index. "Real" values are adjusted by the index; "nominal" are not. Some prices rise faster (tuition, health care), others fall (electronics).
- **Trade-off**: easy to compute; a fixed basket misses new products and quality changes, so adjustments (complex) are added.
- **Does not hold when**: spending patterns differ from the basket (the poor spend more on food and fuel).

## 4.2 Causes of inflation: demand-pull and cost-push
- **Thesis**: inflation is the result of more money than goods.
- **Mechanism**: **demand-pull** (too much money chasing too few goods); **cost-push** or supply shock (a scarce input, such as oil, raises the cost of everything that uses it and lowers production). A commodity price rises for ordinary reasons (more demand and a bad harvest) without general inflation. Deflation (falling prices) shows up for decades in one large economy; very high inflation in a resource-rich country came with economic mismanagement, political instability, falling export income and printing money to keep the economy moving.
- **Trade-off**: low inflation protects savings; slight inflation lets wages and debts adjust.
- **Does not hold when**: relating money supply to prices mechanically; as chapter 2 notes, near-zero rates and expansion of money did not produce high inflation in the mid-2010s.

## 4.3 Bubbles
- **Thesis**: a bubble is a price rise that diverges from fundamentals and depends on a growing supply of new buyers.
- **Mechanism**: easy credit and low rates raise demand; rising prices are read as proof of future rises; speculation draws in more buyers and prices rise faster (US home prices doubled between 2000 and 2006 in the case); the story "prices only go up" crowds out warnings; when buyers run out, the bubble bursts. Earlier examples in the case: a late-1990s internet-stock boom, and a seventeenth-century tulip mania. Warnings existed beforehand and lost against personal anecdotes of profit.
- **Trade-off**: speculation allocates capital to new ideas; the cost is borne at the burst.
- **Does not hold when**: the price rise is explained by demand and supply (population, income, a shortage) as in the chocolate case.
- **Position**: spotting a bubble ex ante is hard; do not claim that the warning was obvious.

## 4.4 How the 2008 crisis built up
- **Thesis**: a housing bubble turned into a systemic crisis through securitisation, bad incentives and opaque derivatives.
- **Mechanism**: investors seeking safe returns when government bonds paid little bought **mortgage-backed securities** (pools of many mortgages, shares sold to investors). Rating agencies gave top ratings, using historic data that did not reflect new lending standards. Demand for securities pushed lenders to create more mortgages, so standards loosened (subprime loans, no income checks, adjustable payments that ballooned). More risky bundles (collateralised debt obligations) were also rated highly. Credit default swaps, sold as insurance without backing capital, linked institutions. When prices fell, defaults rose, houses came back to a market without buyers and the web of unregulated claims made it impossible to know who was exposed; trading and credit markets froze.
- **Responses (as listed in the case)**: emergency loans by the central bank; a bank rescue programme (authorised at $700 billion, with about $250 billion spent on banks and later expanded to carmakers, an insurer and homeowners); stress tests of the largest banks that published which were sound; a fiscal stimulus of over $800 billion; and a reform law with a consumer-protection agency, exchange trading of derivatives and a plan for large banks to fail in a controlled way. Whether the reform is enough is contested.
- **Trade-off**: rescues stopped the cascade; they created moral hazard.
- **Does not hold when**: a single villain (borrowers, banks, regulators) is named: the official inquiry blamed failures of regulation and a faith in self-correcting markets.

## 4.5 Incentive concepts: perverse incentives and moral hazard
- **Thesis**: many crises are incentive failures.
- **Mechanism**: a **perverse incentive** has the opposite effect of the intention (brokers paid per loan made risky loans). **Moral hazard**: someone takes more risk because someone else bears the cost (lenders who sell the mortgage onward; "too big to fail" firms that expect a rescue).
- **Trade-off**: preventing moral hazard (letting firms fail) may cause a wider panic.
- **Does not hold when**: the actor bears the downside (owner-managers with their own capital).

## 4.6 The Great Depression as a banking, credit and trade failure
- **Thesis (position)**: the stock-market crash did not cause the depression; a weak banking system, deflation and policy mistakes did.
- **Mechanism**: the 1920s had credit-financed consumption of new goods, farm overproduction after wartime expansion and mechanisation debts, slowing car and housing sectors, and speculation. Only about three percent of households owned shares, so the crash itself could not explain the mass unemployment that started in 1930 and 1931. Many small banks lacked reserves; in 1930 a wave of bank failures spread across states; banks called in loans and sold assets; credit froze, money in circulation fell and prices fell (deflation). Falling prices led firms to cut costs by firing workers, who then could not buy; debts weighed more heavily. The central bank did not rescue banks and raised its discount rate in 1931; about 2,300 banks failed in that year, double the previous year. International layer: war debts and reparations (about $33 billion in the case) depended on American loans; when those stopped, trade collapsed; a major tariff law raised barriers and was met by retaliation; staying on the gold standard prevented currencies from adjusting (a large economy left it in 1931; the US did not).
- **Why not more stimulus**: the theory that would have justified deficit spending to put a floor under deflation was published only in 1936; the orthodox advice was to do nothing or to "liquidate". The president tried to cushion with persuasion of industry and a debt moratorium, which was not enough.
- **Trade-off**: rules that protect gold and balanced budgets give credibility but remove flexibility.
- **Does not hold when**: the same policy mix is applied to a country with floating currency and strong deposit insurance.
- **Counterargument**: other scholars weigh monetary contraction, trade and the gold standard differently; the order of causes is contested.

## 4.7 Malthusian population claims and their use
- **Thesis (position, criticised)**: population grows geometrically and food arithmetically, so misery is a natural check.
- **Mechanism**: as framed, humans are compared with animals checked by predators, weather, disease and starvation; high prices for food after a rise in demand lead to a "cycle of misery". The claim sits with data that does not match: world population stayed under a billion for millennia until about 1800 and then rose, with food output rising faster. It became an excuse for inaction: during a famine of the 1840s in a colony caused by a crop fungus, officials refused aid and let other crops be exported, justified by the idea that relief only delays the check and that the real problem was the people's character; about a million died. Hundreds of years of religious hatred were also at work.
- **Trade-off**: resource limits are real; treating them as natural law excuses politics.
- **Does not hold when**: starvation is a man-made distribution or policy failure (position; one line of argument is that famine is usually man-made).
- **Counterargument**: limits on land, water and energy can bind locally and ecologically; the dispute is about whether technology and trade move them (general knowledge).

## 4.8 The seventeenth-century general crisis
- **Thesis (position)**: a cluster of shocks (cooling, inflation, religious war) can interact.
- **Mechanism**: a cooling of about half a degree on average (the Little Ice Age; causes debated: volcanic activity, orbital cycles, solar minima) ruined harvests, lowered birth rates and raised famine and cannibalism reports; a "price revolution" from population growth and precious metals arriving from the Americas lowered the value of coin; a thirty-year war from 1618 to 1648 destroyed central Europe, with starvation among the deaths; it began as a dispute over a ruler's decree on religion and grew into a contest among states. Land seized and re-granted and forced conversion deepened the damage.
- **Trade-off**: a unified "crisis" label ties events together; it can hide differences between regions.
- **Does not hold when**: used to claim climate alone caused the wars; contemporary warming differs in cause and size (that analysis notes this).
