# Chapter 6: Geography as Constraint

## Core Idea
Geography does not decide history; it sets costs. Mountains, rivers, coasts, climate, distance and the placement of people and resources make some strategies cheap and others very expensive, and a state tends to keep meeting the same constraint under different regimes. The method: for each country, name the heartland (where people and wealth are), the barriers (what protects and what traps), the lifelines (what must flow in or out), and the frontier weaknesses. This is a **position-based** style of reasoning (sometimes called geographic determinism when pushed too far); each concept here has its counter-case.

## 6.1 Navigable rivers and a protected core
- **Thesis (position)**: a large interior with navigable rivers, a long coastline and weak neighbours gives a state a durable advantage.
- **Mechanism**: water transport is far cheaper than roads and rails (no construction and little maintenance, only ports and boats). A basin of calm, low-gradient tributaries that join one trunk river, navigable for thousands of kilometres (several times the navigable length of the main European rivers in the case), puts a huge agricultural area in one trade network. Oceans on two sides and weak neighbours reduce defence spending. The case credits this geography with the state's wealth, its currency's reserve role, its navy and its overseas bases (estimates: about 30% of world wealth, 20% of manufacturing, about 40% of world military spending, over 750 overseas bases; dated and approximate).
- **Trade-off**: low cost of security and trade; it also allows isolation or a taste for distant intervention.
- **Does not hold when**: used as the sole explanation; institutions (property rights, rule of law), migration and policy are also part of the explanation. A strong geography with bad institutions does not produce the same result.

## 6.2 Mountains protect and box in
- **Thesis**: a mountain ring defends a heartland and limits power projection.
- **Mechanism**: in the case of a high-plateau country, two mountain chains guard the core cities and make invasion a slow town-by-town fight; but they also separate peoples and languages, so any government must rule strongly (often harshly) to hold diverse areas together. In a long war with a flat-terrain neighbour, the defender repelled the first thrust in the mountains, but its own counter-offensive had to supply troops across the mountains while the opponent resupplied over plains and, with an embargo, lost the logistical contest. Expansion tends towards the direction with the least resistance (here westwards, across the mountains), while other directions meet harder terrain, strong neighbours, or steppe that cannot be held.
- **Weak point**: the most valuable resource area lies outside the protective barrier, on a plain open to a neighbour, and the sea exit is a narrow strait (see ch07).
- **Trade-off**: security for the core against fragmentation and exposure of the periphery.
- **Does not hold when**: modern air power, missiles and drones reduce the value of terrain.

## 6.3 Islands: resource poverty and the temptation of empire
- **Thesis (position)**: a densely populated island state without domestic resources must either trade or conquer.
- **Mechanism**: in the island case, most land is mountainous (about 73%); only about 12% is arable; population is crowded onto a few coastal plains; oil, gas, coal and minerals are almost absent; and the country is exposed to earthquakes, tsunamis and volcanoes (a nuclear power programme was halted after a disaster, raising import dependence). Distance protected it from conquest (two thirteenth-century invasion fleets were wrecked by typhoons) and allowed more than two centuries of seclusion. The same shortage drove a period of expansion in the first half of the twentieth century towards neighbouring peninsulas and mainland territories for food, coal and iron. Each conquest brought sanctions that cut trade further; when an embargo cut oil, military planners calculated a two-year supply and chose to seize oil-producing areas, which brought in a coalition they could not beat.
- **Trade-off**: security from invasion; dependence on imports and sea lanes.
- **Does not hold when**: a state can pay for imports through exports and has secure sea access (the same country after 1945 relied on trade and allies).
- **Position**: "the geography forced the war" is too strong; a geographic pressure is a constraint, and leaders chose among options.

## 6.4 Borders that follow neither terrain nor ethnicity
- **Thesis**: borders drawn by a departing power along religious lines, or administrative lines that split a people, leave the successor state with indefensible lines and standing claims.
- **Mechanism**: in one partition case, the border follows religion rather than rivers, mountains or ethnicity: it crosses marshes, desert, plains and high mountains. A long frontier with a larger rival, a capital very close to the front line (about 83 km in the case), the main river running through the middle of the country (so a retreat behind it gives up half the state), one port handling about 60% of cargo, and an ethnic group split by a colonial-era frontier whose co-ethnics rule the neighbouring state, create vulnerability on two flanks. A disputed mountain area has an unmarked section of the ceasefire line, which led to a high-altitude conflict of about two decades (1984 to 2003) in which many soldiers died of exposure; one side ended with the high ground and passes. Such a state seeks "strategic depth" by influencing the neighbour behind it.
- **Trade-off**: a clean religious or ethnic division on paper; permanent insecurity on the ground.
- **Does not hold when**: the frontier has been demarcated and accepted by both (most of the same border was).

## 6.5 A country of two halves: the population line
- **Thesis**: where most land is unfit for farming, most people cluster in a smaller part, and the empty part matters for strategy, not for people.
- **Mechanism**: a diagonal line across one large country puts about 94% of the people on about 43% of the land; the share has stayed nearly constant for a century even as population tripled and the economy grew explosively. East of the line: wide plains and a basin with rain and big rivers. West: a mountain chain that blocks monsoon rain, high plateau, deserts with extreme temperature swings, and desert expansion (estimate: thousands of square kilometres each year). Yet the state holds the west because it was the base of nomad invasions (a buffer), and because it holds water sources, minerals and borders.
- **Trade-off**: a compact core is easy to govern and to defend; its borders are long and the buffer is costly.
- **Does not hold when**: technology (canals, rail, irrigation) changes what can be settled, as the case's own comparison with an earlier westward settlement shows.

## 6.6 A small heartland with no depth
- **Thesis (position)**: when the main cities, ports and airports sit in a narrow coastal plain, a defence has no space to trade for time.
- **Mechanism**: in the case, the coastal plain is about 14 km wide at its narrowest, and a concentrated push from the hills could split the heartland; therefore the state keeps control of the high ground, relies on pre-emption, technology and outside patrons, and sees any hostile army near the plain as a threat. The same logic explains why territorial concessions, which look small on a map, can look existential to the state that makes them.
- **Trade-off**: a high-tech and high-vigilance defence; a standing conflict with neighbours.
- **Does not hold when**: neighbours commit to peace and recognition, which change the threat (some did).
- **Counterargument**: viewing the question only through depth omits politics and the claims of the people living in the contested hills. A geographic constraint explains a security concern; it does not settle who is right.

## 6.7 Poverty and geography: a continental case with many causes
- **Thesis (position)**: geography is one of several causes of slow development.
- **Mechanism**: (a) few navigable rivers and few natural harbours raise transport costs (claims not verified here); (b) a tropical belt allows mosquito-borne and other disease on a large scale, lowering life expectancy (about 60 years in 2022 in the case) and workforce health; (c) only about 43% urban (2024) means thinner markets and weaker states; (d) mineral wealth sits alongside low output. That analysis itself lists colonial extraction, the slave trade, continuing external exploitation and corruption as other causes and warns that a single lens oversimplifies; it also rejects racial explanations as pseudo-scientific.
- **Trade-off**: geography explains costs and not outcomes: other regions faced hard terrain and changed course.
- **Does not hold when**: the claim is used to argue that a region is destined to stay poor.

## 6.8 The temperate-island fortress
- **Thesis (position)**: a state separated from a continent by a narrow sea gains security and a freedom to choose between engagement and isolation.
- **Mechanism**: an island group off a continent is protected by water on all sides, has a mild climate, fertile lowlands and, in the case, energy reserves under the sea; a sea power could project force and build an empire at its peak spanning a quarter of the land and a fifth of the population. Today it must choose between a continental link and a global one, and its strategy relies on an ally's power, nuclear deterrence and navy (see ch08.6).
- **Trade-off**: security against dependence on sea lanes and trade partners.
- **Does not hold when**: air and missile threats and undersea infrastructure change the barrier.

## Diagnostics
1. Draw heartland, barrier, lifeline, frontier and exits.
2. Ask what the geography makes cheap and what it makes expensive.
3. Ask which constraint has been solved by technology (rail, air, drones) and which not.
4. List the non-geographic causes before accepting a geographic one.
5. Do not use geography to decide who is right in a dispute.
