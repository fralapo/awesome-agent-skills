# Chapter 9: Imperialism and Colonialism as Structures

## Core Idea
Empire is not only conquest; it is a structure that arranges territory, trade, law and institutions so that value flows from the controlled area to the controlling one. Its effects persist after formal rule ends, in borders, economies and state design. This chapter separates concepts that are used loosely (empire, imperialism, colonialism), gives the standard theories of lasting effects, and lists mechanisms and cases. The causal weight of colonialism for present conditions is **contested**; chapters 5 and 6 give counter-cases.

## 9.1 Definitions and the overlap
- **Thesis**: three terms overlap and are debated.
- **Mechanism**: an **empire** forms when one authority controls multiple territories, with a range from intrusive to hands-off. **Imperialism** is domination and subordination of one state over others, often motivated by land, resources or strategic position, and an unequal economic or power relation. **Colonialism** often implies settlement of people and cultural control as well as control of land and resources. The two latter terms have similar definitions and remain debated in geography and other fields. European colonialism ran from the fifteenth century to the present in some forms, but other empires (Chinese, Japanese, Mongol) also extracted resources and controlled local politics; the legacy is visible in the shape of states today.
- **Trade-off**: broad definitions capture continuities; narrow ones allow comparison.
- **Does not hold when**: a comparison treats all empires as identical; legal status, settlement and extraction differed.

## 9.2 Motive and means: why a wave of expansion came when it did
- **Thesis (position)**: industrialisation supplied both the motive and the means for late nineteenth-century expansion.
- **Mechanism**: motive: raw materials (cotton, copper, iron, rubber) for factories; prestige and nationalism (the state that has colonies looks great); markets. Means: before the late nineteenth century, Europeans in Africa were held back not by lack of will but by inability: tropical diseases killed Europeans in large numbers, an animal disease killed horses and stopped inland transport, and earlier firearms without horses were not decisive against local armies using spears and axes. Industrial technology (general knowledge here: medicine against fever, steam transport, repeating rifles) changed that balance. In a short period nearly all of a continent was claimed.
- **Trade-off**: a clear two-part model; it omits local agency and the many cases of cooperation and resistance.
- **Does not hold when**: the empire was not industrial (earlier cases, or Mongol and other land empires).

## 9.3 Forced opening of a trading partner
- **Thesis**: free-trade policy backed by force can reverse a trade balance and remove sovereignty.
- **Mechanism**: a large empire had a surplus in tea, silk and porcelain; a naval power found one product the other would buy, an addictive drug, and sold it in large volumes; a confiscation at the port led to a war; the treaty gave a trading port, treaty ports, a large indemnity and extraterritoriality (foreigners judged under their own law). The trade deficit did not reverse immediately; after another war and a huge civil rebellion, the foreign powers became dominant economically.
- **Trade-off**: gains to the traders and the state; humiliation and instability for the weaker state, which, in one analysis, blocked modernisation for a century (see ch08.9).
- **Does not hold when**: the weaker state had strong central capacity (see ch05).

## 9.4 Extractive design and its persistence
- **Thesis (position)**: colonial states were designed to control territory cheaply, maintain order, collect revenue and move raw materials outward; they were not designed for national development.
- **Mechanism**: infrastructure linked mines and plantations to ports and not regions to each other (this is why trade inside such regions stays low, ch05.7). Administration concentrated on a few corridors. After independence, the inherited state is thin outside them, and borders drawn for imperial convenience remain. Effects of a world war on empires: after one, a large multi-ethnic empire was divided between victors (ch07.7); in another case a departing power split a colony into two states along religious lines, leaving a ceasefire line, claims and standing wars (ch06.4).
- **Trade-off**: low-cost control for the colonial power; structural weakness for successors.
- **Does not hold when**: the colony had strong pre-colonial institutions that survived, or when settlement was intended (different dynamics).

## 9.5 Theories of lasting economic effects
- **Dependency theory (position)**: long extraction between colony and colonizer creates a pattern that is difficult to leave; after independence the former colony remains economically reliant on the former ruler.
- **World-systems theory (position)**: the world is divided into a **core** (wealth and power), a **periphery** (sends raw materials to the core and relies on it) and a **semi-periphery** (links to both).
- **Neocolonialism (position)**: a state is formally independent but its economic and political power is closely monitored and controlled by outside forces.
- **Mechanism common to all**: primary production (agriculture, mining, logging) generates little value; processing and manufacturing add the value, so the places that process capture more; resource-rich places can see their wealth extracted. A banana example: growers earn little, the plantation owners and distributors earn more.
- **Trade-off**: these frameworks name real mechanisms of unequal exchange; they can attribute all present problems to the past and ignore domestic choices.
- **Does not hold when**: former colonies that diversified (some did) or a state never colonised that is poor; then other causes matter.
- **Counterargument**: some economists emphasise institutions, geography and policy; they argue that colonial effects varied with the type of colonial rule and settler presence (general knowledge).

## 9.6 Layered colonial relations
- **Thesis**: one place can pass through several kinds of imperial relation.
- **Mechanism**: in the island example, the first outside power used it as a shipping port and fought the indigenous population (strategic location, not settlement); the next imperial power treated it first as a place to keep pirates from key ports and then as a place of migration where settlers farmed sugar and rice and changed land ownership (a subtler colonial relation); indigenous groups were able in some cases to claim land and charge rents, in others they were moved onto plantations. Later it passed to another empire and then to a rival government (ch07, ch08.7).
- **Trade-off**: the layered view avoids a single label; it complicates present-day claims about sovereignty.
- **Does not hold when**: used to assign legal title to any party (law and politics decide that).

## 9.7 Empire as a security network
- **Thesis**: much imperial expansion protected a route to a prized possession.
- **Mechanism**: the sea power's colonies in the Middle East grew from the need to protect shipping between the home islands and its richest colony; protectorates were acquired along the way, a canal built with foreign capital became a vital asset and a country was made a protectorate to ensure access (1888 in the case). Each new asset required more assets to protect it ("imperial overstretch").
- **Trade-off**: security of the route; cost and resentment.
- **Does not hold when**: a route can be bought or traded without ruling.

## 9.8 Violence at the periphery and at home
- **Thesis**: colonial wars often used methods later seen at home.
- **Mechanism**: pre-war decades included a massacre of a people refusing to give up their land (estimates of 24,000 to 100,000 deaths, 1904 to 1908, in the case), concentration camps in a colonial war (many civilians died of disease), closure of a university and killing of intellectuals in an Asian colony, repression of a rebellion by a conglomerate of colonial states, and boycotts. Some scholars see colonial wars as a training ground for later European war (position, contested). A theory that justified inaction in a famine used population claims (ch04.7); the colonial relation shaped the response.
- **Trade-off**: describing violence helps explain grievances; it does not by itself prove a causal chain to later events.
- **Does not hold when**: used to equate all colonial rule with the worst episodes; practices varied.

## 9.9 Informal empire and the modern order
- **Thesis (position)**: an order in which others' trade, currency and security run through one state can resemble empire without formal rule.
- **Mechanism**: see ch08.5. Allies accept leadership in exchange for protection and prosperity; the leader gains influence and a say in their foreign policy. Whether this is an empire (some use that word) or a consensual system is a value judgement.
- **Trade-off**: security and growth for many, asymmetry for all.
- **Does not hold when**: the word "empire" is used only as an insult; define the criteria (control, extraction, consent).

## Diagnostics
1. What kind of relation: rule, settlement, extraction, protection, influence?
2. What flowed and who captured value?
3. What institutions were left behind and what were they built for?
4. Compare with a place that had the same colonial rule and a different outcome.
5. Separate description of harm from claims about its present causal weight.
