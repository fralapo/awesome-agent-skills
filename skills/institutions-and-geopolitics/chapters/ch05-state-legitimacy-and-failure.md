# Chapter 5: The State, Legitimacy and Failure

## Core Idea
A state is a bundle: territory, recognition, an apparatus that can tax, police and administer, and acceptance by the people it governs. These can come apart. A flag and a seat in international bodies do not guarantee control of the land; control of the capital does not guarantee loyalty in the provinces. This chapter gives models for why states fail or fail to unite, and for why some regimes collapse suddenly. The cases are drawn from several regions; each is multi-causal and several claims are **position**.

## 5.1 Juridical versus empirical statehood
- **Thesis (position)**: a state can exist in law (recognised, borders protected) while it barely functions on the ground.
- **Mechanism**: historically weak political entities were conquered, absorbed or partitioned. After decolonisation, international recognition and the rule against changing borders let states with little authority beyond the capital survive. Many new states therefore became sovereign before strong institutions existed, instead of starting strong and decaying. Recognition protects the regime in office, so weak states can be durable as well as fragile.
- **Trade-off**: recognition prevents wars of conquest; it also removes the pressure to build capacity.
- **Does not hold when**: outside powers or neighbours ignore the norm (annexation, secession backed by force).

## 5.2 Why European states became strong: war and dense land
- **Thesis (position, contested)**: war between rulers over scarce, densely settled land pushed them to tax, raise armies and improve administration; where land was abundant, rulers had less reason to control every corner.
- **Mechanism**: in the model used here, European rulers competed for land and people, so war forced fiscal and administrative growth. Where population was spread thin across huge territories, authority could fade with distance, roads and courts were costly, and ruling remote poor areas did not pay. Colonial rule fitted this: it concentrated administration on capitals, ports, mines, plantations and corridors (a railway from a mine to a port), leaving the interior lightly governed. The state looked complete on the map but was uneven on the ground.
- **Trade-off**: low-cost control for the colonial power; a state design that does not suit the tasks a government is later expected to do.
- **Does not hold when**: a case has dense population and intense rivalry yet weak institutions, or sparse population with strong institutions. The thesis is an average tendency, not a law.

## 5.3 Independence raises expectations faster than capacity
- **Thesis**: inherited capitals, bureaucracies and armies were asked to educate, heal, defend and develop.
- **Mechanism**: governments received institutions built to extract and keep order, not to deliver services. The gap between promises and capacity creates grievances; those who hold the state can distribute jobs, contracts, land and security.
- **Trade-off**: patronage buys loyalty cheaply; it makes access to the state an all-or-nothing prize.
- **Does not hold when**: the state's revenue does not depend on one resource and offices are filled by rules.

## 5.4 High stakes make politics personal; ethnicity as a coordination device
- **Thesis (position)**: diversity alone does not cause conflict; tying political access to identity does.
- **Mechanism**: when courts, civil service and police are reliable, losing an election is not a threat to security. When institutions are fragile, control of government means control of jobs, security forces, land and revenue, so elections look like contests for survival. Politicians use ethnic networks (ready trust and mobilisation); the more politics follows those lines, the harder it is to build institutions that all trust, which weakens the state further. One large country's centre-periphery pattern (power and wealth near the capital, marginal regions) tied regional grievance to identity and fed repeated rebellions.
- **Trade-off**: ethnic mobilisation is effective for a politician and corrosive for a state.
- **Does not hold when**: many groups coexist with institutions that protect minorities (the case set mentions examples that did so despite colonial borders).
- **Counterargument**: some states with clumsy colonial borders stayed stable while a culturally homogeneous one collapsed; so the "artificial borders" explanation is partial. Resources show similar contradictions: one resource-rich country kept stable institutions, another saw rebellion, secession and intervention over the same kind of wealth.

## 5.5 Armies, coups and the weak civilian centre
- **Thesis (position)**: where the army is the only organisation with a national chain of command, communications and logistics, it has political leverage.
- **Mechanism**: in many young states civilian institutions were newer and thinner than the army; coups became a recurring feature. Some began as temporary rescues and became long rule.
- **Trade-off**: armies can hold a state together in a crisis; they rarely hand over power willingly.
- **Does not hold when**: civilian control is entrenched and the army is internally divided.

## 5.6 Resource wealth without institutions (a resource-curse sketch)
- **Thesis (position)**: mineral wealth does not make a country rich if the state cannot capture and reinvest it.
- **Mechanism**: one large country's recoverable mineral resources were valued, at 2023 prices, at over 360 times its annual economic output (estimate; dated). The dispute over mine areas draws in rebels and foreign backers. Resource exporters also suffer when prices fall or mismanagement and conflict cut output; one oil exporter went from stable to very high inflation as exports fell and money was printed (see ch04).
- **Trade-off**: resource rents are an easy revenue; they weaken the need to tax citizens and answer to them.
- **Does not hold when**: a country has strong institutions before the resource boom.

## 5.7 Why a nation of one language does not become one state
- **Thesis (position)**: shared language and sentiment are necessary and not sufficient for political union.
- **Mechanism**: in the case of a region that shares a language, mass movements, radio broadcasts that reached citizens across borders, and brief mergers (one lasted only a few years) all pointed to union. It failed because: (a) union means deciding who controls the army, foreign policy, parties and which capital has the last word, so each government feared losing its own country; (b) leaders of parties that preached unity became rivals once each held a state (two branches of one party fought over oil, water and legitimacy); (c) geography gives several separate cores (a river valley, another river basin, a coastal belt, a desert peninsula, a far-western coast) and no obvious centre, with several states holding a different asset (population, oil, a central location, holy cities); (d) colonial-era infrastructure connected each territory to overseas markets, so intra-regional trade stayed low; (e) foreign powers backed different sides.
- **Trade-off**: unity would have created a very large state; separation protects each elite and each local identity.
- **Does not hold when**: a union has a clear core (as unification of Germany around one state).

## 5.8 Why centrally planned regimes collapse: a multi-cause sketch
- **Thesis (position)**: a regime built on forced modernisation, extensive growth and a large security burden becomes brittle; reform can then trigger collapse rather than cure it.
- **Mechanism**: (a) a late start, with a political party forcing the pace of industrialisation that elsewhere took centuries; (b) **extensive growth** (more inputs, quantity over quality) when the economy needed **intensive growth** (productivity, quality); (c) heavy industry and military spending, plus support for allies abroad, took resources from civilian goods and left the country out of a later information-technology shift; (d) shortages and a shadow economy: queues of years for scarce goods, reselling at black-market prices, theft of materials and labour, a layer of private entrepreneurs outside the plan; (e) after a debt problem in the late 1970s and falling prices of exports, food became scarcer and imported; (f) when prices were brought towards market levels, empty shelves became normal; (g) pressure in the satellite states (workers' movements asking for lower food prices) could not be stopped; (h) reforms of openness and restructuring allowed criticism before the economy improved. The state ended in 1991 with a successor state.
- **Aftermath (case)**: assets once owned by "the people" were taken into private control by managers, officials and the well connected, at very low prices; public services such as health care and sanitation weakened because the governments that funded them were gone; male life expectancy fell from about 62 to about 57.5 in the 1990s in one case; inflation of 135% in one country and over 800% in another; many emigrated; a multi-ethnic federation became a battlefield, with ethnic cleansing and massacres. Some analysts describe the wars as rival ancient hatreds; this line of analysis holds that politicians used ethnic rivalry to gain power ("negative integration": define an "us" by dehumanising "them"). Democratic institutions and private enterprise also emerged, especially in one large country, and several states joined a regional union and an alliance.
- **Trade-off**: a sudden break can be a liberation; the cost falls on those least protected.
- **Does not hold when**: a reforming one-party state keeps control of the pace (a one-party state that reforms its economy in stages is a different case, only sketched in ch10.1).
- **Counterargument**: many explanations compete (external pressure, leadership errors, ethnic nationalism in the republics, a rival's arms race); the order of importance is contested.

## 5.9 Legitimacy and the myth of legal election as justification
- **Thesis**: a vote won by a party does not by itself justify abolishing the rule of law afterwards.
- **Mechanism**: the claim "the party won legally" confuses an electoral path to office with the right to remove opposition; the case in ch10 shows an authoritarian takeover after a chain of crises rather than after a clean majority.
- **Trade-off**: elections confer authority; rule-of-law limits restrain it.
- **Does not hold when**: a constitution allows emergency powers by vote (then check who can end the emergency).

## Diagnostics
1. Distinguish law (recognition), capacity (tax, police, courts) and consent (legitimacy).
2. Ask what the stakes of winning are: jobs, security, land.
3. Ask which organisation is strongest and whether it is civilian.
4. Ask what the state is for: extraction or service, and who built it.
5. For a collapse, list underlying, amplifying and triggering causes (see `history-geopolitics`).
