# Chapter 3: Political Economy: Capitalism, Socialism, Class and Inequality

## Core Idea
Political economy asks how political power and economic activity shape each other: who owns what, who decides prices and output, who gets the surplus, and how rules keep that arrangement in place. "Capitalism" and "socialism" are families of arrangements, not single systems; both are best used as descriptions of mechanisms, with the evaluation kept separate. The Marxian framework is presented as one analytical position (a conflict theory) with its criticisms, not as established fact.

## 3.1 Capitalism is variable, because states make markets
- **Thesis**: markets work differently in each place because governments facilitate, regulate or limit them.
- **Mechanism**: a market is a relationship between buyers and sellers who negotiate prices, not a place. A "free market" always has allowances for rules, courts, money and trade policy. Variants: mixed capitalist economies (regulated markets), and democratic-socialist or welfare models (market prices for goods, but the state limits swings in cost and ensures minimum access to health, education and food, funded by taxes). Which countries have access to which markets is a political decision (trade policy, alliances, transit rights).
- **Trade-off**: more state steering buys stability and a floor for citizens; it reduces flexibility and can create dependence on the budget.
- **Does not hold when**: a comparison picks the pure theory of one system and the worst practice of the other.

## 3.2 Mercantile capitalism versus industrial capitalism
- **Thesis**: capital lending and trade are old; the system that invests capital in machines to raise production of marketable goods is new and different in scale.
- **Mechanism**: merchant capitalism (borrow, buy, resell, repay with interest) existed across Eurasia and Africa. Joint-stock companies in the seventeenth century spread the risk of long voyages (own a tenth of ten ships instead of one). Industrial capitalism adds heavy up-front investment in machines whose output exceeds a person's, so owners recoup through volume.
- **Trade-off**: large growth in output and wealth, with long hours, low wages, child labour and workplace danger in its early phases.
- **Does not hold when**: a definition of capitalism is used that includes any trade (then the label explains nothing).

## 3.3 Why industrial capitalism started in one island state (a multi-cause account)
- **Thesis (position)**: it needed an agricultural surplus, labour released from land, a naval and commercial empire, and freer markets, plus a culture that valued risk and consumption.
- **Mechanism**: imported farming methods raised yields; higher grain prices rewarded investment; lower later food prices left wages with spare buying power, which rewarded cheaper manufactured goods (a positive feedback loop). The share of the population working the land fell from about four in five (1520) to about one in three (1800) and one in four (1850) in the case. Enclosure privatised commons, raised productivity and impoverished tenants, which created a labour pool. The sea power and colonial trade, including the slave trade, supplied capital and markets. A period of civil unrest weakened royal licences and monopolies, which freed markets. Writers popularised the idea that people are individuals, rational market actors and consumers whose appetites drive industry.
- **Trade-off**: growth bought with displaced farmers and coerced labour abroad.
- **Does not hold when**: used as a recipe: not every ingredient is transferable, and which ingredient was decisive is debated.

## 3.4 The industrial transformation was incremental
- **Thesis**: the Industrial Revolution was many small improvements by many people, not a few geniuses.
- **Mechanism**: a larger and healthier population (boiled drinks, calorie-dense New World crops after centuries of war and cold) had more time to tinker. Improved shuttles raised weaving speed; weaving needed more thread; spinning machines followed; linking machines to one power source (water, then steam) created factories. One cause of pressure was the demand for cloth in global markets. Living standards in 1800 were, by one scholar's estimate quoted, close to those of far earlier eras; by 1920 most workers were in shops, transport, mining and factories, measured time in minutes and used electricity-based and mechanised devices.
- **Trade-off**: it raised output per worker and gave states the means for conquest and war (see ch09 imperialism, ch08 world wars).
- **Does not hold when**: a "great man" story is needed to explain one invention (usually a chain of previous ones).

## 3.5 The Marxian framework as a position
- **Thesis (position)**: material production is the base; politics, religion, family and culture are a superstructure built on it.
- **Mechanism**: humans survive only by changing nature together (labour); the organisation of labour creates social constraints on top of natural ones. Modes of production (primitive communal, feudal, capitalist) combine **forces of production** (tools, factories, technology, skills) and **relations of production** (wage labour or not, property, how surplus is taken and who decides its use). In capitalism, workers do not own means of production, so they must sell labour; wages cover subsistence while the value they produce is higher; the difference is surplus (profit). Owners are pushed to cut wages and raise productivity; this tends to produce **crises of overproduction** (too much made for the wage-earners to buy). History moves when the forces outgrow the relations.
- **Trade-off**: a powerful way to see who gains from an arrangement; it risks explaining everything by economics.
- **Does not hold when**: ideas, nationalism or religion are the driving cause; critics say the base-superstructure link is too rigid.
- **Counterargument**: the predicted revolution did not occur in the most industrialised countries. One explanation offered: the owning class fragmented (managers, shareholders, owners of many kinds), unions won better conditions, and wide share ownership gave more people a stake. Another: states used welfare and regulation to absorb conflict (general knowledge).

## 3.6 Variants of socialism and how they became regimes
- **Thesis**: socialism is a choice and a plan, not a claim about nature; this makes it a different kind of argument from capitalism.
- **Mechanism**: early (utopian) socialists criticised capitalism but rejected revolution after the violence of an earlier revolution; revolutionary socialists accepted violence, some in elitist forms (a small leading group educates workers). The Marxian version claimed that class conflict was the real history and that capitalism was not natural. A planned economy sets output, prices and wages centrally and can prioritise social goals (how many nurses a clinic has) over demand. Without price signals the planners lack local information, giving uneven supply; one regional case relied heavily on primary production under planning while a neighbouring planned economy with more manufacturing did better; the richer capitalist half of the same nation did better still, helped by outside investment (case-based, one pair of examples).
- **Trade-off**: social guarantees and priorities against information problems and concentration of power.
- **Does not hold when**: "socialism" is used for everything from welfare states to one-party planned economies; separate them.
- **Position on the revolutionary regimes**: in this model, the states that called themselves Marxist moved wealth and power not to workers but to the revolution's leaders, which is the reverse of the theory; the labels were kept. See ch10 for the ideology cases.

## 3.7 Class and stratification: three explanations
- **Thesis**: stratification (a ranking of people by wealth, power and prestige) needs an explanation; each school gives a partial one.
- **Mechanism**: (a) the **functionalist** view holds that unequal rewards fill important and hard jobs. Its problems: it does not show how the system works, important jobs can be poorly paid (waste collection), highly paid jobs may be less important, and it assumes real equality of opportunity; (b) the **conflict** view holds that groups with control of resources keep the advantage and extract labour from those who have only their own; (c) **ideology** makes unequal arrangements feel fair (a peasant: "this is my place"; a worker: "he deserves it"). Ideology explains why inequality lasts, not why it begins.
- **Trade-off**: functionalism explains incentives; conflict explains power; neither explains the persistence of luck and inheritance.
- **Does not hold when**: used as ethics ("inequality is functional, so just").
- **Position**: structural features of how society is organised can limit success whatever the individual's effort; a meritocracy is a claim to test, not an assumption.

## 3.7b Inequality between and within countries
- **Thesis**: the industrial era opened a gap between countries and, in many (not all) countries, a gap within them.
- **Mechanism**: global wealth by one estimate was about $260 trillion, with a small share of the world's population in two northern regions holding about two thirds of it (dated estimate). In a ten-group illustration of world income, the richest fifth got most of every hundred dollars. In 1820 the richest countries were around three times richer than the poorest; the case gives about one hundred to one today (estimate). Drivers within countries: skill-biased technological change (technology complements skilled workers and replaces unskilled ones), globalisation that lifts the poorest somewhat and the richest much more, fewer unions, tax rules that favour the wealthy, very large executive pay ratios, race and gender. A Lorenz curve plots the cumulative share of income against the share of households; the area between it and the line of perfect equality measures concentration. In the US case the top fifth rose from about 43% of income in 1970 to over 50% in 2010 while the bottom fifth's share fell from 4.1% to 3.3%.
- **Trade-off**: some inequality gives incentives; extreme inequality wears social trust (position).
- **Does not hold when**: income and wealth are confused (income is a flow, wealth a stock). Check which one a claim uses.
- **Counterargument**: some economists stress that absolute living standards of the poor rose even as gaps widened; that is compatible with the data, and the choice between the two framings is a value judgement.
