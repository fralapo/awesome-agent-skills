# Chapter 2: Institutions: How Rules Generate or Constrain Outcomes

## Core Idea
Rules for choosing leaders, funding campaigns and issuing money do not just record what people want; they shape which options survive, who is rewarded and what the system can do under stress. This chapter treats an institution as a mechanism: input (votes, money, interest rates), rule, output (winners, policy, prices). The worked examples come from one federal republic (general-knowledge counterparts are marked), but the mechanisms travel. Most of the evaluation of "is the rule good" is **position**; the mechanisms themselves are checkable.

## 2.1 Plurality rule, single-member districts and the two-party outcome
- **Thesis**: where one seat goes to whoever gets the most votes, politics tends to settle on two competitive parties.
- **Mechanism**: with plurality ("first past the post") and single-member districts, second place earns nothing. Voters who fear "wasting" a vote on a small party move to the larger party closest to their views; donors and activists follow; the small party's share shrinks further. This is the idea known as Duverger's law (a regularity, not a strict law). The related **spoiler effect**: a small party that draws from one big party's side can hand the seat to the other side, so voting one's conscience feels risky. A winner can also be chosen by a small plurality: with many candidates, nine at 10% and one at 11% means the winner is rejected by most voters (illustration).
- **Trade-off**: clear winners, single-party governments and direct local representation, at the cost of leaving many voters with a representative they did not pick.
- **Does not hold when**: the country uses proportional lists, ranked-choice voting or multi-member districts (the same voters then support more parties); or when regional parties win locally (the law works per district, not nationally).

## 2.2 Two explanations for a party system: institutions versus voters
- **Thesis (position)**: a stable two-party system needs both an institutional explanation and a voter explanation.
- **Mechanism**: the institutional explanation points at the electoral rules (2.1). The voter explanation points at the voters: a large share of self-described independents is not a bloc; they hold very different views, most lean to one major party in practice, others sit outside the usual left-right axes or are disengaged. A new third party would have to unite people who agree on little. A two-axis chart (economic: more or less state involvement; social: change or status quo) helps place parties; multi-party systems fill the gaps and extend the edges.
- **Trade-off**: the voter explanation protects the rules from blame; the institutional one protects voters from blame. Treat both as partial.
- **Does not hold when**: an electoral reform in a comparable country changes the number of parties without changing voters (a natural experiment that favours the institutional account).
- **Counterargument**: some argue that even with proportional rules polarised voters would split into blocs that behave like two camps; whether that is better is a value judgement.

## 2.3 Parties as vehicles inside an existing system
- **Thesis**: where entering through a small party is hard, movements enter through a major party instead.
- **Mechanism**: candidates with views outside the party's centre run in its primaries or on its ballot line and win; the party's identity is reshaped from within. Both ideologically distant and quite moderate movements have done this. The cost to the movement is that it must accept the receiving party's label and coalition.
- **Trade-off**: low entry cost, but the party's brand and the movement's goals can drift apart.
- **Does not hold when**: the party controls candidate selection tightly (closed lists, strong leaders).

## 2.4 Founding conflicts: two visions of the state
- **Thesis (position)**: early party conflict in a new state often starts as a disagreement about how large and how commercial the central government should be.
- **Mechanism**: in the case used here one camp wanted a mercantile, manufacturing country run by an educated elite with a strong central government, patents and infrastructure, close to the leading trading power; the other wanted a mostly agrarian country of small farmers with small local government, distrust of concentrated economic power, and sympathy for the rival power. Each side's attitude to speech and democracy tracked its view of who could be trusted with power. The picture is not clean: members of the "more democratic" camp held slaves and supported a revolutionary government that turned violent.
- **Trade-off**: such splits give a young state two organised coalitions and a way to alternate power.
- **Does not hold when**: the contest is mainly about identity (religion, ethnicity) rather than economic design.

## 2.5 A two-stage election and the popular-vote gap
- **Thesis**: an indirect election built as a compromise produces results that differ from the popular vote in a predictable way.
- **Mechanism**: each state receives electors equal to its senators (always two) plus its House seats (by population), so small states get more electors per person (illustrative figures from the case: about one elector per 200,000 people in a small state, about one per 700,000 in the largest, a ratio near four to one; dated, check current figures). Almost every state awards all electors to its statewide winner (two states split by district). A narrow win in a big state captures every elector, and millions of votes for the loser in that state change nothing. The candidate with fewer votes nationally can win if narrow wins fall in the right states: five presidential elections in the case's history did so; in one, under 100,000 votes spread across three states would have reversed the result.
- **Origin**: drafted in 1787 as a compromise between direct popular vote (judged impractical and, for the slave-holding states, unattractive because a third of their population could not vote), election by the legislature (feared as too powerful) and state-based selection.
- **Trade-off**: protects smaller states and ties the result to a federal structure; it distorts the weight of votes and focuses campaigns on a few competitive states.
- **Does not hold when**: the winner has a large lead; then the two counts agree.
- **Counterargument**: defenders say a pure popular vote would make candidates ignore rural areas. The case notes that even winning all of the biggest cities would give only about a quarter of the national vote, so a national campaign would still need broad support (arithmetic; position on the consequences remains open).

## 2.6 Faithless electors and reform routes
- **Thesis**: the formal legal rules behind an indirect vote matter at the margin and are hard to change.
- **Mechanism**: a few electors in one cycle voted against their pledge; a later court ruling allowed states to penalise or void such votes. Abolishing the system needs a constitutional amendment (a very high bar). A workaround is a compact in which states agree to give their electors to the national popular winner once states holding a majority of electors join; it leaves the structure in place; its legality is disputed (position) and in the case it had not reached the threshold. Later legislation clarified the presiding officer's role in counting as ceremonial.
- **Trade-off**: workarounds are easier than amendments and also less certain.
- **Does not hold when**: a state's own law ties electors strictly to the statewide vote.

## 2.7 Money in campaigns: direct versus independent spending
- **Thesis**: regulation that limits money going to candidates pushes money into channels that are not limited.
- **Mechanism**: direct contributions to campaigns are capped and reported. Independent spending (advertising, events, support for groups) is not capped, since a 2010 high-court ruling treated it as protected speech for corporations, unions, nonprofits and later individuals. Two vehicles: outside committees that report their donors but can raise unlimited sums and may not coordinate with a campaign; and politically active nonprofits that need not disclose donors ("dark money"). In the case, of about $14 billion spent on federal campaigns in one cycle only about $4.7 billion was direct contributions; roughly a fifth of spending came from outside committees and around $2 billion from non-disclosing groups (dated cycle estimates). Rules that cap the political share of a nonprofit's budget are easy to meet by labelling spending as issue advertising.
- **Trade-off**: more speech and participation against less transparency and a stronger hand for the wealthy.
- **Does not hold when**: disclosure and coordination rules are enforced or when spending is publicly funded.

## 2.8 Where campaign money goes and why donors give
- **Thesis (position)**: money follows competitive races and incumbents, and big donors may buy access and agenda rather than votes.
- **Mechanism**: money flows to close races, since only there can it change the outcome; incumbents raise more thanks to name recognition (about 90% of incumbents seeking re-election win in the case); a congressional winner needs about a million dollars (estimate). Most is spent on advertising, staff and further fundraising. Small donors give for affinity, to stop the other side ("negative partisanship") or as "surrogate representation" to a candidate outside their district. For large donors there are three theories: legal bribery, access and agenda-setting, and networking with like-minded candidates. Political scientists, as reported in the case, find little direct evidence that donations buy specific votes but some evidence that they influence which bills reach a vote.
- **Trade-off**: money lets challengers be heard; it also favours those with donors.
- **Does not hold when**: spending is capped or races are not competitive. Evidence is mixed; treat "money buys votes" as unproven and "money shapes the agenda" as plausible.

## 2.9 Central bank independence
- **Thesis (position)**: those who control the money supply should be insulated from election cycles.
- **Mechanism**: a central bank (here created in 1913 as a federation of regional banks plus a governing board and a market committee) has two stated goals, stable prices and full employment. It uses: the discount rate for loans to banks (other rates follow it); reserve requirements (how much cash a bank must hold against deposits; fractional-reserve banking; a rule meant to prevent bank runs); and open-market operations (buying or selling government debt; large-scale purchases are quantitative easing). Lower rates raise lending and money in circulation; higher rates cool an overheated economy. Board members are appointed, not elected, so they can look beyond re-election.
- **Trade-off**: credible price stability against less democratic control.
- **Does not hold when**: the central bank's mandate is unclear or its independence is only formal. The case itself notes that after years of near-zero rates inflation stayed low, so the link from money to prices is not mechanical (as of the mid-2010s, dated).

## 2.10 Fiscal gridlock makes monetary policy the only lever
- **Thesis (position)**: when a divided legislature cannot agree on taxes and spending, the central bank is left as the only active stabiliser.
- **Mechanism**: fiscal policy (tax and spending) needs legislative agreement; monetary policy needs only a committee. Under divided government the second can be the only tool still moving. This concentrates economic management in an unelected body.
- **Trade-off**: speed and predictability against thin democratic legitimacy and limits of the tool (it cannot build a bridge or insure the unemployed).
- **Does not hold when**: government is unified and fiscal policy flows freely.

## 2.11 Liberalism and democracy as ideas (general knowledge)
- **Thesis (general knowledge)**: liberalism, in its classical sense, puts the individual, rights, limits on power and rule of law first; democracy concerns who decides. They can come apart: elected majorities can override rights (illiberal democracy); a liberal regime can limit the franchise.
- **Mechanism (general knowledge)**: constitutions, courts and a free press constrain majorities; elections constrain rulers; both are needed to avoid tyranny of one or tyranny of many. The word "liberal" shifts meaning across countries (economic freedom in one place, social reform in another), so clarify it before arguing.
- **Does not hold when**: a writer uses "liberal" or "democracy" as a label of approval for whatever they favour; ask what rule is being praised.
- Marked here as thin: a short general-knowledge sketch only.

## Diagnostics
1. Name the rule, the input and the output.
2. Ask what the rule rewards (concentration, compromise, loyalty).
3. Ask who gains and who pays; ask what changes if the rule changes.
4. Separate the mechanism (testable) from the judgement (position).
5. Compare to a country with a different rule, not to an ideal.
