# Chapter 05: Cosmology, Dark Energy and the Naturalness of Constants

## Core Idea
In this model the universe is studied by **combining independent probes** and by asking whether the values of constants look "arranged". Both rest on inference about models, not direct measurement. The chapter separates accepted results (expansion, acceleration is the best-fit description) from open and contested ones (whether dark energy changes, whether lumpiness mimics it, what explains the smallness of some quantities).

Fields: **Thesis**, **Mechanism**, **Trade-off**, **Does not hold when**. **(position)** = contested.

## 5.1 Expansion history from supernovae
- **Thesis**: distances plus redshifts of exploding white dwarfs give the history of cosmic expansion.
- **Mechanism**: supernova brightness tells how far the light travelled; redshift tells how much space stretched in that time. Together across many supernovae they give the expansion history. The surprise (late 1990s) was **acceleration**, expected to be deceleration since gravity in the original theory only pulls inward. A **cosmological constant** (vacuum energy) supplies the push; the name for whatever causes it is **dark energy**. The standard model with a cosmological constant and cold dark matter is the Lambda-CDM model.
- **Trade-off**: simple, fits a wide range of data.
- **Does not hold when**: you say acceleration was measured directly; it was inferred because models with dark energy fit better than models without it.

## 5.2 The smoothness assumption and its cost
- **Thesis**: the standard solution treats matter as perfectly smooth, which is only good above a few hundred million light years.
- **Mechanism**: with homogeneous, isotropic matter the equations of general relativity reduce to the Friedmann equations; matter density enters as one number. Real matter forms clusters, filaments and voids. Photons crossing them gain and lose energy; over large distances these should cancel.
- **Trade-off**: tractable; ignores structure that may matter.
- **Does not hold when**: structure feeds back on the average expansion; whether it does is the contested question in 5.3.

## 5.3 Timescape: lumpiness as an alternative to dark energy (position)
- **Thesis (position)**: differences in the flow of time between dense regions and voids could change the effective expansion rate and mimic acceleration, with no dark energy.
- **Mechanism**: time runs slower in dense, high-gravity regions and faster in voids, so voids expand more; as structure grows the void fraction grows and photons pick up more redshift in late times. A published analysis of a large supernova sample found a better fit than Lambda-CDM, with the gap largest for nearby supernovae where lumpiness is strongest. It uses unmodified general relativity, so is simpler in ingredients.
- **Trade-off**: favoured by Occam if only supernovae count.
- **Does not hold when**: other probes are included. Baryon acoustic oscillations and the microwave background independently indicate acceleration; structure growth matches Lambda-CDM simulations; flatness requires about 70% dark energy. The proposed time-flow differences (billions of years) are far larger than the consensus estimate (hundreds to thousands of years). Authors acknowledge supernova calibration needs more work. Verdict (position): probably not right, but a useful reminder to model the temporal landscape more carefully.

## 5.4 Several surveys, one universe: how combining probes works
- **Thesis**: no experiment measures dark energy; each constrains combinations of parameters.
- **Mechanism**: a probe maps parameters to an observable (galaxy distances, lensing shapes, supernova dimming, sound-wave fossils, the microwave background). The unknowns include the dark energy density now and its rate of change (an evolving equation of state), plus proportions of dark matter, ordinary matter, neutrinos and light. Each probe cuts away inconsistent universes. See ch01.9 on degeneracies and covariance. The question asked of data was "does dark energy change?"
- **Trade-off**: a tighter allowed region, but only as good as independence.
- **Does not hold when**: probes share galaxies or calibration (covariance).
- **Result (dated)**: a large survey that mapped galaxy distances hinted that dark energy may weaken with time. A second survey using many different probes was consistent with the standard picture, but **slightly favoured** a diminishing dark energy, and combined results narrowed the allowed region further. The hints were not ruled out, which would have signalled a bias in the first survey. This is **intriguing, not confirmed**. There is also a separate mismatch in expansion rate between nearby measurements and early-universe inference (the Hubble tension), unresolved here.

## 5.5 Fine-tuning and naturalness
- **Thesis**: a number looks "unnatural" when the mechanisms believed to produce it would naively give something vastly different, and only fine cancellations bring it down.
- **Mechanism**: two cases. (1) The Higgs boson mass should receive contributions from interactions at very high energies and so be huge unless they cancel precisely (**hierarchy problem**). (2) The cosmological constant is tiny compared with the expected vacuum energy of quantum fields, again needing near but not exact cancellation. In the standard model these are handled by **renormalisation**: add compensating terms and fix masses to measured values, so masses become free parameters (19 in the standard model) instead of predictions. Some founders of the theory called this a dippy, arbitrary procedure that neglects infinities and left them dissatisfied. Real physical evidence that field fluctuations are not mere maths includes the Casimir force (ch07).
- **Trade-off**: renormalisation makes the theory self-consistent and predictive within its range, at the cost of not explaining the values.
- **Does not hold when**: you treat the "virtual particles" of the calculation as literally existing; many physicists treat them as bookkeeping of field interactions.

## 5.6 The arrow-in-the-bullseye argument
- **Thesis**: surprise at a low-energy value is justified when almost none of the plausible deep-theory parameter space would produce it.
- **Mechanism**: imagine all possible UV theories as a barn wall; a blindfolded archer hits some spot. Hitting a random spot is unremarkable; hitting a tiny painted bullseye is not. The "prior" here is a **Bayesian prior**: a measure of our ignorance, not a physical process. If the fraction of theory space that yields the measured IR parameter is vanishingly small, something is arranged. Options: (a) the archer peeked: the UV theory is influenced by the IR result (a hidden link, new symmetry or mechanism); (b) many arrows: an enormous number of universes, we are in an extremely rare one with a survivable Higgs mass (anthropic reasoning).
- **Trade-off**: gives a principled version of "this looks too special".
- **Does not hold when**: the prior is poorly motivated; "natural" depends on what you consider the space of possibilities. Nature is not unnatural; the apparent unnaturalness signals missing physics. The unique-theory view (no choice) and the multiverse view differ on whether the arrow was one or many; redefining the multiverse as "the world" makes the second one singular again (position).

## 5.7 Constants and the pixel-size picture
- **Thesis**: each constant is also a conversion factor between theories (the Planck constant for quantum, the speed of light for relativity).
- **Mechanism**: ch03 shows that the Planck constant alone sets the colour of a star at a given temperature; a different value would shift all emission.
- **Trade-off**: useful for dimensional intuition.
- **Does not hold when**: a constant is treated as freely adjustable without the rest of physics changing (all else equal is rarely true).

## Checks
- Is an acceleration claim about the average expansion or local lumps?
- How many independent probes agree, and how correlated are they?
- Is "unnatural" defined relative to an explicit prior?
- Which option is proposed: peeking archer or many arrows, and what would test it?
