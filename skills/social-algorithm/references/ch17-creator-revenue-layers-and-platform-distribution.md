# Chapter 17: Creator Revenue Layers and Platform Distribution Models

## Core Idea
Platform ad revenue is the smallest and least controllable layer of a creator's income. In this model, a channel is an audience-and-trust asset first, then monetised through layers that pay more per viewer the further they sit from the platform. Separately, platforms differ in how they distribute content (follower graph versus interest graph), and that decides how a new account can grow. All figures below are **dated orders of magnitude for calibration, not benchmarks**; rates change by niche, country, season and platform rule.

---

## 1. The Revenue Layers, from Lowest to Highest Control
| Layer | Typical order of magnitude (dated snapshot) | Who controls it |
|---|---|---|
| Platform ad share | A few tenths of a euro to a few euros per 1,000 views (revenue per mille, RPM); a figure around 0.5 to 1 euro is common in some European-language niches, higher in finance, software and business | The platform and advertisers |
| Sponsorship | Usually priced per integration or per expected views, and often a multiple of the ad share for the same video | The creator and the brand |
| Affiliate and product placement | A commission on sales; depends on audience intent | The creator and the merchant |
| Own products (course, consulting, service, workshop) | Highest per viewer; needs a funnel (Ch 13) | The creator |
| Self-published goods (print-on-demand books) | Platform keeps a large share of the cover price (often close to half, rules change) | The platform |

- **Arithmetic check**: RPM times views divided by 1,000. At 0.6 euro per 1,000 views, 100,000 views earn about 60 euros. A small channel can therefore earn tens or hundreds of euros a month from ads alone; this is why Ch 11 treats ad revenue as a floor and the channel as a showcase.
- **Interpretation (position)**: advertiser budgets are said to be moving toward personal channels and away from traditional media because audiences trust individuals. The evidence is anecdotal; use it as a reason to build sponsor-readiness (Ch 11), not as a forecast.
- **Warning**: income claims in creator-education content are self-reported and usually promote a course; ask for the niche, audience size, time, whether the figure is gross or net and how many comparable attempts failed.

## 2. Interest Graph versus Social Graph
- **Social graph platform (follower-led)**: what you publish first reaches people who follow you; growth depends on an existing base and on shares. A new account starts with almost no distribution.
- **Interest graph platform (suggestion-led)**: the system shows each video to people whose past behaviour suggests interest, regardless of who follows you. In the cases reported, most views on such a platform come from suggestions (a figure around 95 percent for one short-video platform against about 70 percent on a long-video platform is quoted, unverified). The consequence: the first video can reach many people, and quality of the first seconds and the topic fit matter more than the follower count.
- **Signals commonly claimed to matter (unverified)**: rewatches, watching to the end, shares, comments and likes, roughly in that order of weight. Point scores that circulate online (for example 10, 8, 6, 4, 2) are folk numbers, not published rules; use only the order of importance as a hypothesis to test on your own analytics.
- **Practical rule**: on an interest-graph platform, test many short formats on one clear topic; on a follower-led platform, invest in the base, repeat contact points and shares (Ch 4, Ch 10).

## 3. Growth Mechanics That Recur
- **Collaboration**: a detailed, specific proposal sent from a professional address to an established creator in an adjacent niche, offering something concrete (a joint video, a guest slot, data). Generic requests are ignored.
- **Library effect**: a deep archive amplifies a viral video: viewers who arrive then watch many older videos. Consistency of topic makes the archive valuable.
- **Packaging**: title and thumbnail decide whether the video is clicked at all; a good video with weak packaging is invisible (Ch 12 on content-first).
- **Content versus algorithm (position)**: one view holds that the quality of the content accounts for most of a video's success (a figure of about 90 percent is quoted, a guess) and the algorithm only follows people's reactions; the rival view says distribution rules, packaging and timing are independent levers. The two are compatible: content is necessary, packaging and fit decide whether it is seen.
- **Done over perfect**: publishing a flawed video gives data; perfecting a script for months does not.
- **Delegation by value of time**: assign tasks by the value of an hour (editing, thumbnails, research) once the revenue layers above exist (Ch 3).

## 4. Business-Plan Questions for a Channel
1. Who is the one viewer (Ch 12), and what do they pay for outside the platform?
2. Which layer pays first (sponsor, affiliate, service) and which comes after (product)?
3. What is the realistic RPM for the niche and country, and what is the monthly ad income at current views?
4. What is the distribution model of the platform used, and which format fits it?
5. What does the channel need to show a sponsor (audience profile, comments, retention, past integrations)?

## 5. Limits
- Rates, share percentages and ranking claims are snapshots; platform policies change every year.
- This chapter gives no income guarantee. Cases that quote earnings from a small channel are single, self-reported and often tied to a paid course.
