# Chapter 1: Method: Evidence, Inference and Narrative

## Core Idea
A story of collapse is built from two very different layers: what the ground and the natural archives show, and what texts, epics and later retellings say. The first is physical but silent about intent; the second speaks about intent but is written by people with a stake. This chapter fixes the working method used in every later chapter: label each claim by layer, state how far it can be dated, and never let a vivid story stand in for a measurement. Much of the chapter is **position** about how to weigh evidence; where a mechanism exists (dating, proxy records) it is stated as such.

## 1.1 Two layers: material record and written or oral record
- **Thesis (position)**: keep an explicit split between what was found and what was told.
- **Mechanism**: the material layer is ruins, burn layers, arrowheads, bones, pottery, shipwreck cargo, soil and sediment, tree rings, ice cores. It shows that something happened (a fire, a drop in monument building, a diet shift) but not who did it or why. The text layer is inscriptions, administrative letters, sagas, chronicles, epics. It gives names, motives and quotations but is shaped by who commissioned it. A set of ruins means nothing until someone interprets it, and interpretation is where the disputes live.
- **Trade-off**: using only the material layer is safe but mute; using texts adds meaning at the price of bias.
- **Does not hold when**: a source is both (an inscription is an object and a statement); treat the object (where, when, how it was made) and the statement (what it claims) separately.

## 1.2 Whose voice survives: rulers, victors and what rots
- **Thesis**: surviving texts over-represent kings and durable materials.
- **Mechanism**: royal inscriptions flatter their commissioner (a ruler "tore the enemy with the claws of his grandeur"); they tell how a ruler wished to be seen, not what happened. Perishable records are missing: Angkor kept books on palm leaf, which need recopying about every hundred years, and none survives, so the stone inscriptions are nearly the only voice and they stop. Maya books were burned by a colonial inquisitor; very few survive. Ordinary people leave rubbish heaps, graffiti and graves, not statements.
- **Trade-off**: reading a ruler's text as a statement of intent is informative; reading it as a report of events is risky.
- **Does not hold when**: a royal claim can be checked against an independent record (a letter from the other side, a shipwreck cargo list, a burial); agreement between independent sources raises confidence a lot.

## 1.3 Silence is data, but ambiguous
- **Thesis**: an absence (no warning in inscriptions, no bodies at an abandoned farm) is a fact to explain, not a conclusion.
- **Mechanism**: Maya monuments fall silent city by city without ever mentioning trouble; art quality does not decline before the end. An inspector found an abandoned outer settlement in Greenland with animals roaming and no sign of fighting or burning. Silence can mean no crisis was felt, a taboo on recording failure, loss of the habit of carving, orderly departure, or loss of the record itself. Each reading predicts something different (graves, hoards left behind, doors closed, animal remains).
- **Trade-off**: filling the silence with the most dramatic story is easy and unfalsifiable; leaving it open costs narrative satisfaction.
- **Does not hold when**: silence is itself patterned (a monument left half carved, a hoard buried and never retrieved): that pattern is positive evidence of interruption.

## 1.4 A destruction layer does not name the destroyer
- **Thesis**: ash proves fire, not enemy.
- **Mechanism**: a burn layer at the same level across many sites suggests a common period, but burning can come from invasion, internal revolt, accidental fire, or deliberate abandonment. Arrowheads in streets show a fight, not whose. Attribution to a famous enemy is the convenient step: whole waves of destruction were long assigned to one raider group, while later work points to neighbours exploiting weakness at several sites and possibly to rebellion at others.
- **Trade-off**: naming a culprit makes a clean story; admitting "fire, cause unknown" is honest but unsatisfying.
- **Does not hold when**: there are inscriptions or letters from the attacked side naming the attackers, or weapon types and ship remains that are diagnostic.

## 1.5 Proxy records: what each measures and at what scale
- **Thesis**: climate and environment arguments rest on proxies; read each for what it actually records.
- **Mechanism**: pollen in lake and swamp mud shows which plants (forest, crops, desert scrub) grew nearby; sediment cores show erosion, flood sand and drought layers; tree rings are wider in wet years and narrower in dry ones; ice cores hold dust, salt and volcanic sulphate; cave mineral isotopes track past rainfall; bone chemistry shows what a person ate (land or marine food) and whether growth was stunted. Each has a spatial footprint (one lake is not a continent) and a dating error. A volcanic signal in ice does not by itself say how cold a given region became.
- **Trade-off**: proxies give independent, physical evidence; but combining several imperfect ones can produce false precision.
- **Does not hold when**: several unrelated proxies agree on timing and direction at the site itself; then the argument is strong even if each is individually weak.

## 1.6 Say what collapsed
- **Thesis**: "collapse" is not one thing; name the variable.
- **Mechanism**: candidates are the political centre (kings and court), the monumental building programme, long-distance trade, writing and record keeping, urban population, the whole regional population, a language or religion, the economy of a settlement. These move at different speeds. Maya royal institutions ended while commoners lingered for generations in some places; Angkor's court moved south while the old city kept pilgrims and farmers; a Greenland colony's records stop while people may have left rather than died.
- **Trade-off**: a narrow definition is precise but may miss continuity; a broad one hides what actually ended.
- **Does not hold when**: only one variable is documented; then say so and do not generalise to "the civilization".

## 1.7 Epic, saga and folklore are weak evidence with real uses
- **Thesis (position)**: oral literature can preserve a trace of an event but cannot date or detail it.
- **Mechanism**: epic poems about a besieged coastal city were composed centuries after the period and written down later; the real site shows repeated attacks, fire and repairs, so a composite of events is plausible but the poem's plot is not a record. Sagas were composed for recitation and written down long after the events; settlers' folk tales about the other people they met cast them as monsters or fools. Such texts show attitudes and what a culture remembered, not event sequence.
- **Trade-off**: ignoring them loses context; trusting them imports legend.
- **Does not hold when**: a detail in the story is corroborated by the ground (a described settlement type is found where the story says).

## 1.8 Numbers about the past are models
- **Thesis**: populations, army sizes, tax yields and city areas are estimates built on assumptions.
- **Mechanism**: city populations come from house counts times assumed household size; claims such as "one in a thousand people lived there" or "half a million in the empire" depend on the density assumed for unexcavated areas. Chronicle figures for dead or ships may be rhetorical. Quote ranges and the basis, and prefer ratios and trends (monuments per year, skeleton health, share of marine food) over absolute totals.
- **Trade-off**: ranges look weak but are honest; a single big number is memorable and fragile.
- **Does not hold when**: a count is of discrete physical things (carved dated monuments, shipwreck ingots); then it is a measurement of the record, though not of the society.

## 1.9 Multi-causal by default, with different roles
- **Thesis (position)**: separate underlying vulnerabilities, amplifiers and triggers.
- **Mechanism**: a structural weakness (fragile food base, dependence on one lifeline, a legitimacy contract tied to good harvests) is not a cause until a trigger arrives (drought, a plague in the supplier, an invasion). The trigger is often the visible event and the vulnerability the real explanation. A single villain account (an invading horde, a climate shift, a bad ruler) is usually one trigger told as the whole. When historians disagree, report each proposed cause with the evidence for it, rather than ranking by taste.
- **Trade-off**: multi-causal accounts are truer but harder to remember and to act on.
- **Does not hold when**: a case really has one dominant, well-dated cause (a sudden, localised catastrophe); then say that and show the evidence.

## 1.10 Output labelling convention
- **Thesis (position)**: tag every claim so the reader can see its layer.
- **Mechanism**: use four labels in answers: **Evidence** (found or measured, with where), **Inference** (reasoned from evidence, with the step), **Narrative** (told by sources or later retellings, not corroborated), **Debated** (credible alternatives exist; name them). State dating uncertainty in the same line. If a point comes from general knowledge rather than the cases covered here, mark it as such.
- **Trade-off**: labels lengthen answers.
- **Does not hold when**: a short question needs a short answer; give the label only to the contested claim.
