# Chapter 2: Models of Collapse

## Core Idea
Models are lenses, not laws. Each lens in this chapter explains some cases well and others badly, and each tells you what evidence to look for. In this model the useful move is to run several lenses on the same case and see which predictions the evidence supports. Most of the chapter is **position**; where an observed mechanism exists in the cases (a cascade, a feedback loop) it is stated, with the case in chapter 6.

## 2.1 Stress accumulation and trigger
- **Thesis (position)**: collapse is usually a long build-up of stresses released by a trigger.
- **Mechanism**: a society in a hard environment accumulates imbalances (population beyond easy food supply, eroded soil, a dependent trade lifeline, a ruling class that has grown costly). Under normal variation these hold. A sufficiently large shock then makes them fail together along existing fracture lines. Observable sign: several independent strains appear before the visible event (poor health in skeletons, abandoned slopes, repaired walls, rising tribute demands).
- **Trade-off**: the lens explains why similar shocks hit some societies harder; it cannot say in advance which stress was decisive.
- **Does not hold when**: the end was a sudden external destruction with no earlier strain; then the invader or disaster is the primary explanation.

## 2.2 Complexity, energy and diminishing returns
- **Thesis (position)**: a complex society needs a steady flow of surplus; if the surplus shrinks, complexity falls to what the remaining flow can carry.
- **Mechanism**: elites, specialists, armies and monuments are paid from farm surplus. In the Maya lowlands one farming household could feed perhaps five others, far less than modern farming; so the non-farming share was small and the whole system sat close to its limit. Add population growth and each further unit of output costs more (poorer slopes, shorter fallow). When the surplus falls, the cheapest thing to cut is the top: monuments, courts, long-range trade.
- **Trade-off**: the lens is simple and testable (look for surplus, effort per unit of food, share of non-producers) but can make collapse sound inevitable.
- **Does not hold when**: a society keeps complexity by taking surplus from outside (tribute, trade, conquest) or by technology that raises yield; then the relevant variable is the stability of that external source.

## 2.3 Cascading failure in coupled systems
- **Thesis**: tightly connected parts fail in sequence, and each failure loads the survivors.
- **Mechanism**: when one node fails, its work shifts to neighbours, which overload and fail in turn. Three examples at three scales: a water network where one broken embankment spills into the next canal; a region of palace economies where one fallen supplier starves the next customer; an alliance web where each state's army is committed elsewhere when attacked. Signs: failures cluster in time across places that did not interact directly; repair of one part breaks another.
- **Trade-off**: connection gives efficiency and mutual aid; the same connection carries the shock.
- **Does not hold when**: the parts are loosely coupled with spare capacity; then failures stay local.

## 2.4 Feedback loops and minimum viable size
- **Thesis (position)**: some systems need a minimum population or budget to maintain themselves, and below it decline accelerates.
- **Mechanism**: an irrigation city needs people to clear silt, repair banks and manage gates. If fewer people stay, maintenance lapses, floods or droughts hit, health worsens, more leave. The question "did people leave because the system failed or did it fail because they left?" is a loop, not a sequence. Look for the trigger of the first turn and for thresholds (a bridge rebuilt from temple stones, a moat choked with vegetation).
- **Trade-off**: the model explains self-accelerating abandonment; it is hard to measure the threshold.
- **Does not hold when**: maintenance can be shifted to a smaller core or a new site (a court relocating and leaving the old city to pilgrims); then decline is a relocation.

## 2.5 Optimisation makes fragility
- **Thesis (position)**: a system tuned for the usual year has no margin for the unusual one.
- **Mechanism**: the Maya built storage tanks and water control for ordinary droughts; the water network in a monsoon city handled one or two dry years. A drought lasting decades, or swings from drought to flood, exceeds the design range. Fixes made for one condition (new inlets to bring more water in a drought) become hazards in the opposite condition. The design envelope, not the average, sets the survival limit.
- **Trade-off**: margin costs labour and land now for a benefit that may never be needed.
- **Does not hold when**: extreme events are truly outside any reasonable design range; then the lesson is humility, not blame.

## 2.6 Simplification is not disappearance
- **Thesis**: most collapses are reorganisations to a simpler form, with continuity of people, memory and place.
- **Mechanism**: commoners moved into abandoned Maya palaces and kept venerating monuments (placing carved stones upside down shows the writing was no longer read); monks and farmers continued at Angkor; a strong state withdrew to its core and survived; people left a Norse colony by sea or crossed to the other culture. Evidence of continuity: reuse of buildings, graffiti, rubbish layers, the same names persisting in later languages.
- **Trade-off**: "continuity" can understate real loss (deaths, lost writing, lost skills); "collapse" can overstate it.
- **Does not hold when**: evidence shows death on a mass scale (unburied bodies, unbroken famine layers, a regional emptying); then continuity is not the story.

## 2.7 Compare survivors and the fallen
- **Thesis (position)**: the best test of a cause is to ask why neighbours under the same shock did not fall.
- **Mechanism**: in the Late Bronze Age two large states survived in diminished form: one by pulling back to a defensible core, the other by winning a decisive defensive battle and then declining slowly over decades (strikes over unpaid rations, fractured rule). Others vanished. Differences in geography (river valley with a single approach, interior stronghold), defence strategy and stored resources are candidate explanations. Beware of survivor bias: those who survive are studied for their strengths, and luck is invisible.
- **Trade-off**: contrasts add leverage; the sample is tiny and confounded.
- **Does not hold when**: the survivors were never subject to the same shock.

## 2.8 Fast event or slow process
- **Thesis**: separate the duration of the process from the duration of the visible ending.
- **Mechanism**: the Maya record fades over about a century or more, city by city; the Bronze Age destruction layers fall across a few decades in many places; a northern colony fades over decades with a last dated record and an archaeological end later; Angkor's land use declined for over a century before the traditional date of its fall. A "year of the fall" is usually the last record, not the end. Ask: what is the last dated object, and what is the evidence of life afterwards?
- **Trade-off**: precise dates are convenient for teaching; false for processes.
- **Does not hold when**: a single datable event (a siege with a recorded end) closes one institution; then give it, and keep the longer process separate.

## 2.9 Structure versus contingency
- **Thesis (position)**: individual decisions matter inside structural limits.
- **Mechanism**: a ruler's raid on a former ally turned a low-level ritual war into a total war and the loss of a capital for a century; a monarch's unsuccessful invasions drained the treasury and left no heir; a pacifist prince refused to fight and let civil war run. Such choices explain timing and who fell first; structure explains why the consequences were so heavy.
- **Trade-off**: personal stories are vivid and can overshadow structure.
- **Does not hold when**: the same outcome occurs in many polities with different rulers; then structure carries the case.
