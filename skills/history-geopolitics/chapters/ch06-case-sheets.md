# Chapter 6: Case Sheets

## Core Idea
Four cases, each as a sheet: **proposed causes**, **evidence** (what was found or measured), **narrative layer** (what is told but weakly supported), **what is contested**, and **what continued**. Dates and figures are estimates unless marked as measured. Where the evidence is unclear the sheet says "unclear". Do not blend sheets: the same word (drought, invasion, decline) means different things in each. An island society that overshot its resources is a frequently cited, still contested case but is not covered here; do not reconstruct it from memory without sources.

## How to read a sheet
| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Proposed cause | A cause someone argues, with its role: underlying (weakness), amplifier, trigger |
| Evidence | Physical or documentary find that bears on it, and where in the record |
| Status | Strong / plausible / speculative / debated (reading of the evidence, not a consensus) |
| Contested | Who or what disagrees, and why the record cannot settle it |

## 6.1 The Classic Maya lowlands (collapse about 760 to 910 AD)

**Scope.** A network of rival city-states (not an empire) in the forested limestone lowlands of today's Guatemala, Belize and southern Mexico, sharing writing, calendar and religion. The Classic Period brought rapid growth from the third and fourth centuries. The collapse of royal institutions and monument building is dated by the last carved dated monuments: from about 792 in one western city to about 909 in the last (a wave moving across the lowlands from the southwest).

**Setting that shaped everything.** Porous limestone with few rivers, water stored in underground sinkholes and plastered cisterns; thin soils, slash-and-burn farming with low-protein staples; no draft animals, no metal tools; long-range obsidian and jade trade with the highlands. One farmer fed perhaps five others, a very thin surplus.

| Proposed cause | Role | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Population growth beyond easy food supply | Underlying | Dense housing (hundreds of persons per square kilometre in a capital), hillside settlement, shorter fallow inferred | Plausible; density figures are estimates |
| Deforestation and soil erosion | Underlying/amplifier | Pollen showing near-complete forest loss by about 800; sediments and eroded hill soils at a valley city; limestone burnt for plaster | Strong for local cases; regional extent debated |
| Severe drought | Trigger/amplifier | Sediment records of a very dry spell about 760, described by some authors as the worst in thousands of years; linked to a solar-output low seen in ice cores and a shift of Atlantic winds | Plausible; timing, size and cause debated |
| Malnutrition and disease | Symptom/amplifier | Porous bones and tooth stress lines in a valley city from about 650, in elite and commoners | Strong at the site |
| Elite growth and competition | Amplifier | Nobles' own palaces during the boom; monument counts rising then falling | Plausible |
| Loss of royal legitimacy | Mechanism | Monuments stop; palace burned; neighbours claim the capital's title; an unfinished last monument at one city | Plausible; chain of cause debated |
| Warfare and fragmentation | Amplifier/symptom | Burned buildings and vandalised monuments; a centre fortified with reused temple stones; weapon points in great numbers | Plausible for the late phase |
| Refugee movement and chain reaction | Mechanism | Inferred; no direct count | Speculative |

**Evidence (material).** Dated monument series by city; skeletal health; pollen; sediment; a squatter midden in abandoned royal buildings at a capital; burned and vandalised structures at others; reused carved stones in a hastily built wall.

**Narrative layer.** The image of a sudden vanishing; inscriptions show no warning, which is a silence (1.3). Colonial-era Maya texts echo a darkened sun and drought in a way that looks like memory but is later, undated and poetic. The older story of widespread human sacrifice as the main trait of the society was shaped by outside propaganda; ritual killing existed but was limited and small-scale in most accounts. Population claims (tens or hundreds of thousands vanishing, "perhaps millions") are modelled numbers.

**Contested.**
- Whether a drought was the trigger or one stress among others; how large it was and what caused it.
- Whether the collapse was uniform; many accounts treat the lowlands as a whole, but regional sequences differ (a valley city kept 15,000 people a century after its king, and was empty by the twelfth century). Some northern cities carried on in the wider literature (not covered here).
- Whether a unified state would have helped (untestable).
- The scale and role of war: ritual, total, or late symptom.

**What continued.** People, not only the elite; commoners lived in and revered palaces and monuments; later people kept some texts orally; the forests regrew over the abandoned cities (some accounts say the lowlands were reforested only just before the Europeans came).

**Does not hold when (analogy).** A fragmented, stone-age, low-surplus society without draft animals is far from any modern economy; the useful part is the mechanism (thin surplus plus legitimacy tied to harvests plus a shock beyond design range), not the story.

## 6.2 Angkor and the Khmer Empire (ninth to fifteenth century AD)

**Scope.** An empire founded by a king crowned as god-king in 802, which ruled much of mainland Southeast Asia; its centre was a vast low-density city around a monsoon-fed lake system. Peak around the eleventh to thirteenth centuries, with the temple Angkor Wat built in the early twelfth century and a great rebuilding by a Buddhist king after a naval attack. Last inscribed king recorded in the 1330s; a period of silence in royal records; the traditional date of the fall to a Siamese army is 1431, after which the court moved south.

**Setting.** Rice farming with several harvests a year made possible by a hydraulic network: canals, embankment reservoirs (barays), possibly gates, fed from hills. High food yield and a heavy maintenance obligation. Elite Hindu culture on top of largely Buddhist or animist commoners.

| Proposed cause | Role | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Climate swing: multi-decade drought and flood extremes | Trigger | Tree rings: drought about 1330 to 1375 and again 1400 to 1425, with very wet years between; coarse sand in canals (floods); emergency inlets and blocked outlets | Plausible; local sediment and tree-ring data are strong; link to the exact failure is inferred |
| Hydraulic network overstrain and cascade | Mechanism | Hasty bridge made of temple stones whose river carved away its end; moat covered by swamp plants by the late fourteenth century | Plausible; whether people left first or the system failed first is unclear |
| Depopulation and feedback loop | Mechanism | Land-use intensity declining over more than a century; more tree growth; lower erosion and burning | Plausible |
| Military defeat by neighbours (Siamese and Vietnamese pressures) | Trigger | Loss of border provinces; a siege in 1352; a seven-month siege and sack in 1431; statues found in the Siamese capital | Strong that sieges occurred; role in abandonment debated |
| Religious change undermining the god-king | Underlying | Art shifts from Hindu scenes to Buddhist after 1200; Theravada state religion by about 1295; monks in the streets | Plausible |
| Extractive tax system and inequality | Underlying | Temple families competing to send tax; large labour levies; deforestation | Plausible |
| Succession wars and costly foreign campaigns | Underlying | 27 rulers, 16 legitimate; failed invasions of coastal kingdoms | Plausible |
| Epidemic or earthquake | Proposed by some | No direct evidence of a mass die-off | Speculative; some accounts argue against |
| Shift of trade to coastal ports | Proposed in wider literature | Not covered here | Debated; state as general knowledge |

**Evidence (material).** Stone inscriptions (up to the early fourteenth century); a Chinese envoy's report of about 1296 (describing a rich court, many monks, a recent war with Siam); tree rings, sediment cores, canal sands, reservoir modifications; the reused-stone bridge; looting evidence in Siam; the absence of valuables on site.

**Narrative layer.** The "lost city swallowed by jungle" (Angkor was never lost: monks and farmers used it); legends of a leper king; foreign guesses of Roman or Macedonian builders; the date 1431 as the end of the city.

**Contested.**
- Was Angkor abandoned wholesale, or did the court move while the city shrank over a long time? Some accounts argue for slow decline over more than a century.
- Cause-and-effect between failing water works and leaving.
- Whether road networks helped the enemy or the economy (both argued).
- Relative weight of climate, war and religion.

**What continued.** The Khmer state and people (the court moved to the region of the modern capital); the temples as living religious sites; farming nearby.

**Does not hold when (analogy).** Anyone drawing a modern lesson ("inequality plus variable climate") should note that the strongest evidence is for the water system and climate; the inequality link is inferred from inscriptions about labour and staff and from a religious shift, not measured.

## 6.3 The Late Bronze Age collapse of the eastern Mediterranean (about 1200 to 1100 BC)

**Scope.** Within roughly forty to fifty years a whole region of palace economies lost its cities: an Anatolian empire's capital burned; a trading port in Syria was destroyed; Greek palace centres fell; a Mesopotamian dynasty was toppled; Egypt survived badly weakened; Assyria withdrew to its core. Writing and trade systems broke; recovery took centuries in some areas.

| Proposed cause | Role | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Sea Peoples" raids and invasions | Trigger (some sites) | Royal inscriptions and reliefs in Egypt describing an armada and a battle in the Delta; a last letter from the Syrian port king saying enemy ships have come and his troops and ships are elsewhere; burn layers two metres thick at the port | Strong for some sites; their identity and origin unknown; role elsewhere debated |
| Land-based neighbours exploiting weakness | Trigger (other sites) | Greek centres attributed in some accounts to northern neighbours; Anatolian capital to northern groups; Babylon invaded by an eastern kingdom in 1158 BC after allies were busy | Plausible; attributions are inferred |
| Drought and famine | Amplifier/trigger | Pollen, sediments and cave isotopes showing aridity; letters: "I have no grain"; famine in a Syrian town | Plausible; extent and cause debated |
| Large volcanic eruption in the north Atlantic | Proposed trigger of the drought | Ice and tree-growth records of about two decades of cooling around 1100 BC (estimate, contested) | Speculative for the Mediterranean; date and effect debated |
| Breakdown of trade and interdependence | Mechanism | Dependency on bronze (copper and tin), loss of trade routes | Plausible |
| Iron weapons empowering small states | Proposed amplifier | Rise of massed infantry over chariot armies | Contested; may be consequence |
| Internal rebellion | Proposed | Not demonstrated; flagged as speculation | Speculative |
| Earthquakes, epidemics | Proposed in wider literature | Not covered here | Debated; state as general knowledge |

**Evidence (material).** Destruction layers in many cities, arrowheads in streets, bodies left where they fell; hoards buried and never retrieved (a smith's tools, silver under a bench); royal inscriptions; clay-tablet letters; a ship's cargo showing the trade network and its metal load; reliefs of captured foreigners with distinctive headdresses (varied, not one army); a long scroll listing the king's boasts.

**Narrative layer.** The Sea Peoples as an alien invasion force (the Hollywood version); the epic of the siege of Troy as memory of the era (a composite at best); the claim that they were a single leaderless refugee army driven by famine is a story that fits the evidence but is not shown.

**Contested.**
- Who the Sea Peoples were, where they came from, whether they were cause or symptom.
- Whether the same event or many separate ones; how the destruction layers sync (dating spread).
- The climate role, and the dating of the volcanic event.
- Whether iron was a cause or a result.
- Rebellion and system failure versus external attack.

**What continued.** Egypt (diminished); Assyria (retreated); a group settled in a southern coastal strip and left a name for the region; a poetic memory; iron-based economies and infantry warfare after.

**Does not hold when (analogy).** "A globalised world, same fragility" is the headline lesson; the Bronze Age network was a small number of palaces connected by elite trade, so the analogy is about mechanism (essential-input chokepoint, correlated shocks, loss of mutual aid), not scale.

## 6.4 The Norse of Greenland (about 985 to the mid-fifteenth century AD)

**Scope.** Two settlements (an Eastern and a Western one) founded from Iceland by a group of outlaws and settlers, at the edge of the habitable zone; at most a few thousand people (estimates run to over five thousand at the height); a cathedral, stone barns, a trade with the home kingdom in ivory. Contact with indigenous hunters from the north who spread south; short-lived voyages to the American mainland (one confirmed site, a repair station). The Western Settlement found empty about 1341 (no tax since 1327); the last written record is a wedding in 1408; by about 1435 archaeology suggests all gone.

| Proposed cause | Role | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Climate cooling (end of a warm spell, start of a cold period) | Underlying/trigger | Ice-core salt and sea-ice records; coldest winters of two millennia in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries; tree growth and plant dating in Iceland | Strong that cooling occurred; its cause and local size debated |
| Loss of the supply lifeline | Mechanism | Plague in the home port and kingdom (1349); pirate burning of the port (1393); no new bishop after 1378; no new iron in late layers | Strong for the sequence; effect on Greenland inferred |
| Fall of the ivory market | Amplifier | Elephant ivory entering Europe; tax in ivory valued highly in 1327 | Plausible; size debated |
| Environmental damage to the land | Amplifier | A farmhouse buried in sand; cleared shrubs and burned wood | Plausible; modern work finds careful management too |
| Inflexibility versus adaptation | Debated | Isotopes: marine share of diet up to about 80%; more seal bones; fertilising increased; yet European clothing persisted; no bone fish hooks | Debated |
| Conflict with the incoming indigenous group | Proposed | An attack recorded in 1379 (18 men killed); Inuit folk tales; trade in iron objects | Plausible for local events; weak as a main cause |
| Long hunting trips and a storm disaster | Proposed mechanism | Hunting up to 1,500 km away; a comparison to a fleet lost in a northern isle storm in 1832 | Speculative; analogy, not evidence |
| Emigration | Proposed | Young people leaving; closed doors; later reports; a ship blown from the American coast in 1347 | Plausible for part; no record of mass arrival |

**Evidence (material).** Isotopes and skeletons: stunted growth, worn teeth; midden layers showing blowflies in living quarters, butchered hunting dog and newborn calf, hooves and small game eaten; worn tools; textiles with preserved dye; ruins buried in sand; sagas and tax records; an inspection voyage report; bone and soil studies.

**Narrative layer.** The sagas' heroes and the eventful founders; "Greenland" as a marketing name; a man found dead in seal skins with a red beard as a symbol of assimilation (a single reported anecdote of the sixteenth century, unverified); the idea of unicorn horns; the Hollywood end of "frozen to death in their houses" and its opposite "sailed to Vinland" (undocumented). Folk tales of both sides.

**Contested.**
- How much the colonists adapted and why they stopped.
- Whether the settlement ended in death, flight or both; the Western Settlement showed no violence and animals roaming, so a hasty or orderly departure is suggested, but unclear.
- The weight of the supply link versus climate versus market.
- Size of the population.

**What continued.** The indigenous people of the island; some Norse loan words; memory in Iceland.

**Does not hold when (analogy).** An isolated colony in a polar edge is an extreme case of lifeline dependence; many modern regions have more options. The case is best used for the method of reading isotopes and refuse against a "they did not adapt" story.

## 6.5 Comparing the sheets
| Dimension | Maya | Angkor | Bronze Age | Greenland |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unit that failed | Court and cities | Capital and court, city shrank | Palaces and trade system | Colony |
| Shape in time | About a century, city by city | Over a century of decline, sieges | Decades, many places | Decades after a decline |
| Environmental factor | Drought, soil, forest | Monsoon swings, water network | Drought (debated) | Cooling, marginal land |
| Trade role | Patron and obsidian | Secondary in these accounts | Central | Central (lifeline, ivory) |
| Legitimacy role | Central | Central (religion) | Mentioned (rebellion) | Minor |
| External force | Rival cities | Siam, Cham | Sea Peoples, neighbours | Indigenous neighbours, pirates |
| Continuity | Commoners stay | Court moves, monks stay | Two states survive | Unclear |
| Main uncertainty | Role of drought | Order of cause and effect | Identity of attackers | Why adaptation stopped |
