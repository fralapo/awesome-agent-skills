# Chapter 13: Additional Case Sheets I: States, Empires and Institutions

## Core Idea
Ten further sheets, in the format of chapter 6: **proposed causes** (with role), **evidence**, **contested**, **continuity**. Do not extend a sheet beyond what it states. Dates and figures are estimates as reported. Roman politics are treated in depth in chapter 10; wars and campaigns in chapter 14; the outbreak of the First World War in chapter 12.

## 13.1 Athens and Sparta: why one side won (Peloponnesian War, late 5th century BC)
**What ended**: the sea power's empire and its war effort; its rival's land league won.
| Proposed cause | Role | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alliance structure | Underlying | one league multipolar, with land and naval partners; the other unipolar, with a hegemon that took over the league's finances and was overbearing; each had dissident members | Plausible |
| Supply and economics | Amplifier/trigger | the hegemon survived yearly devastation of its countryside via grain from the Black Sea and mines in the north; the grain route was cut by a northern campaign and then by a naval defeat | Strong for the last step |
| Foreign patron | Amplifier | a rich empire's satraps funded the rival's fleet in the 410s and opened a front on the Ionian coast | Strong |
| Overextension | Amplifier | an overseas expedition lost almost everyone; a "structural" reading rather than one leader's fault | Plausible |
| Internal politics | Amplifier | trials and exiles, an oligarchic coup in 411, a democratic restoration; the rival feared a revolt of its serf class and was cautious | Plausible |
| Image and propaganda | Amplifier | the "liberator" image lost after the destruction of a neutral island's population; the rival presented itself as protector of smaller states | Plausible |
**Contested**: whether a better peace was available in 411; whether the coup stopped an Athenian victory (said to be not testable); which factor was decisive. A common conclusion: overextension plus a wealthy rival with a patron, resentful allies and internal division; not predetermined. A comparison with 20th-century superpowers is analogy and not used here.
**Continuity**: the victor installed a short-lived oligarchy in the defeated city.

## 13.2 The British Empire's decline (1880s to 1970s)
**What ended**: the territorial empire and the great-power position; the empire was not conquered or overthrown by revolt.
| Proposed cause | Role | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Costs of holding the empire | Underlying | by about 1880 the return was said to be lower than investment at home; defence cost per taxpayer 2.5 times others' | Plausible; the figure is one historian's estimate |
| Rise of richer competitors | Underlying | two other states passed it in steel and industry by 1900 | Strong |
| Two world wars | Amplifier | wartime lending to allies, asset sales, debt to the great neutral lender; debt rose about elevenfold between 1914 and 1919 (as reported); in the 1920s 44% of spending went to interest; later a large wartime aid programme | Strong |
| Colonial nationalism and post-war expectations | Amplifier | dominion status 1867 to 1931; refused self-rule in the largest colony; a massacre of 1919; independence in 1947 with partition | Strong |
| A patron's pressure | Trigger | a Cold War opinion that favoured anti-colonialism; a canal intervention of 1956 stopped within 9 days under pressure including on financial support | Strong for 1956 |
| Prestige loss | Symptom/amplifier | the fall of a fortress colony in 1942; the 1947 admission it could not defend two allied countries | Plausible |
**Contested**: whether the decline was **chosen** (a negotiated withdrawal aiming at a common-defence commonwealth) or forced; how much a post-war loan shaped decisions; the policy of keeping the dominions in a shared defence sphere had mixed results (the largest former colonies left it). **Continuity**: 14 overseas territories; a commonwealth of former colonies, in practice a junior partner of the new leader.

## 13.3 Yugoslavia's break-up (1980s to 1991) (political background only)
**What ended**: a multinational federation.
| Proposed cause | Role | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strongman state without succession plan | Underlying | one ruler held the system together and repressed nationalism; the 1974 constitution decentralised power to republics and gave the collective presidency a seat for each unit; no viable successor | Plausible |
| Return of nationalism and loss of narrative control | Amplifier | after the leader's death historians and intellectuals wrote from national perspectives; an academy memorandum of 1986 blamed decentralisation and claimed an abuse of one nation in a province | Plausible |
| Economic hardship in a poor province | Amplifier | a minority share of 13% against 77% (1981 census); unemployment; emigration of one group | Plausible |
| Rise of a leader through mass rallies | Trigger | a party bureaucrat turned national leader in 1987, toppled leaders of two provinces and a republic, abolished two autonomies in 1989; other republics feared the same | Strong |
| Federal paralysis | Mechanism | the collective presidency rejected army intervention twice (Jan and March 1991); the leader declared "Yugoslavia is finished" | Strong |
| Exit of the two richest republics | Mechanism | walkout at a party congress (Jan 1990); multi-party elections (April 1990); secret arming | Strong |
**Contested**: whether the leader had orchestrated the 1987 rally (claimed by a rival, unclear); the role of a local Serb minority (about 600,000) in Croatia. The sheet stops in March 1991; the wars are **not covered**.
**Continuity**: not covered.

## 13.4 The fall of the Roman Republic (1st century BC) (see chapter 10)
Summary: proposed causes: erosion of norms (ignored vetoes, ritual manipulation), emergency measures, a conquest governor's army and need for land legislation, fear of prosecution, a debt and urban poor problem, weighted voting, provincial incentive structure, a lifetime dictatorship. **Contested**: moral decline versus structure (10.9). Not covered: the assassination and the attempt to restore the republic.

## 13.5 The third-century crisis of Rome
**What ended**: central control and a stable currency; the empire survived.
| Proposed cause | Role | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Civil wars and emperors made by armies | Underlying | many short reigns; one ruler took power by acclamation after killing a rival | Plausible |
| External pressure | Amplifier | invaders reached Italy; a ruler built a new wall around the capital because the old one, more than six centuries old, no longer enclosed the city | Plausible |
| Monetary debasement | Amplifier | a later law that coins must contain at least 5% silver | Plausible |
| Plagues, loss of trade and a split into three states | Amplifier | millions died in two epidemics; trade broke down; landowners turned from market crops to self-supply; city poor moved to estates and became tied tenants (chapter 13.10) | Plausible |
**Contested**: the order of events is obscure. **Continuity**: two rulers stabilised the empire for two more centuries; a barbarian capture of the city in 410 shows the weakness remained.

## 13.6 The end of a steppe empire and its successor states
**What ended**: unified rule; the successor khanates ended at different times (a general sequence from the 1260s to the 1700s).
| Proposed cause | Role | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Succession struggles | Underlying | the empire stayed unified about 33 years after its founder's death, then rival brothers fought over election by assembly | Strong |
| Distance and local rooting | Underlying | successors adopted local religion, coinage, titles and capital cities | Plausible |
| Loss of legitimacy | Amplifier | rapid successions, regional lords acting as rulers, a conversion tied to legitimacy, an end to assembly election | Plausible |
| Disasters and monetary mistakes | Amplifier | recurring famines in one region (almost every other year 1272-1357, as reported), floods, locusts; paper money printed at seven times the previous three years' volume in 1310 | Plausible |
| Disease on campaign | Trigger | a ruler's death during a campaign started the succession dispute | Plausible |
**Contested**: the empire is often said to have fallen with its founder (demonstrably wrong); dynastic legitimacy outlived real power; the last claimant of the throne was said to have lost it in 1502 but a branch in another region claimed the same title. Not covered: the long middle period.

## 13.7 The Inca Empire
**What ended**: a state conquered by a small force after an epidemic and civil war.
| Proposed cause | Role | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Epidemic before the conquerors | Trigger | in some areas the population may have dropped by as much as 90% (as reported); the ruler died of an unnamed disease and the capital was in ruins | Plausible; the disease and numbers are uncertain |
| Succession and dynastic structure | Underlying | the dead ruler's mummy kept his estates, so each heir needed new land and pushed expansion | Position |
| Labour system strain | Amplifier | a cumbersome labour system and a second capital created | Plausible |
**Contested**: the effect of the epidemic. **Source note**: a major indigenous colonial chronicle was sent in the only copy to the king and not seen for 300 years.

## 13.8 The Ottoman rise and the succession rule
**What it shows**: an expansion based on state capacity and a succession system.
- **Succession rule (position)**: all princes had an equal claim and the winner was expected to eliminate his brothers; fratricide was codified as a rule and credited with a century of stability; it also made civil war after a ruler without a designated heir almost certain. The salaried household troops decided the outcome by revolt, and demanded an increase in pay.
- **Military system**: a central salaried force (about 6,000 foot soldiers with firearms and 3,000 cavalry) with provincial land-grant cavalry on the wings, a light infantry screen, vassal contingents and artillery; centralised control of taxation and land-grant holders with a doubling of revenue.
- **Expansion**: conquest of the Mamluk sultanate and the holy cities in 1516-17 (1514 was the battle of Chaldiran against the Safavids) greatly enlarged the empire (as reported), brought control of eastern Mediterranean and Red Sea routes, religious prestige and a large treasury. A legend that the treasury was never refilled and the state went bankrupt only in the 19th century is a **legend** (1.7). The reading of the ruler's severity as stability policy is contested.

## 13.9 The Iranian empires' administrative tradition
**Claim**: a layered administration lasted across successive dynasties. **Mechanism**: training and specialisation; fixed surveyed taxation; coinage; roads and relay posts; separate appointment of civil governor and military commander so both would have to revolt together; independent inspectors reporting to the king; tolerance of local custom if it did not impede administration; direct local-to-centre contact. **Decline**: corruption, over-taxation, arrogance, elite luxury. **Continuity**: successive conquerors kept the system, and it "captured the conqueror". **Caution**: a number of "first in history" claims and the speed of the post (90 days against 5) are claims from admiring sources; treat them as position (1.2).

## 13.10 From slavery to serfdom (late Roman to 11th century)
**What changed**: a mass slave economy became a tied-tenant economy.
| Proposed cause | Role | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fewer captives | Underlying | late Roman wars were defensive; medieval wars local | Plausible |
| Plague, civil war and trade breakdown | Amplifier | estates turned to self-supply; free poor became tied tenants, bound to the land by law for tax reasons | Plausible |
| Weak state and revolt | Amplifier | slave flight and revolts; laws ordering seizure of runaways | Plausible |
| Church | Both | defended obedience, urged manumission, forbade enslaving fellow believers, owned many slaves itself | Plausible |
| Falling demand and technology | Amplifier | fewer markets and cities; watermills; landlords rent plots to slave families who keep surplus | Plausible |
**Contested**: a linear replacement (old view) versus an up-and-down process with slavery persisting beside serfdom until about the 11th century (newer view); the figures on the share of law concerned with slavery (about 46% in one kingdom's code, 24% and 13% in others) show prominence, not numbers of slaves. **Not covered**: the later history of serfdom.
