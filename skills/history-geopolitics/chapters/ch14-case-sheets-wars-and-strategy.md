# Chapter 14: Additional Case Sheets II: Wars, Campaigns and Famine

## Core Idea
Ten more sheets in the chapter 6 format, centred on **why campaigns and wars were won or lost**. The sheets keep the causal content and drop battle detail. Figures are as reported and are estimates. Mechanisms are in chapters 9 and 11; the origin of the First World War is chapter 12.

## 14.1 Iran-Iraq War, years 1982 to 1985 (the second phase)
**Question**: why did the side with the initiative and the larger population not win?
| Proposed cause | Role | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attacks on prepared defences | Underlying | mass infantry assaults without armour, artillery or air support against minefields, flooded zones and defence in depth; sandstorm; some local successes at surprise points (mountains, marshes) | Strong |
| Fragmented command | Amplifier | regular army, revolutionary guard and volunteer militia each with a separate line to the leader and competing for credit; an opponent said it could discern no pattern | Plausible (an opponent's claim) |
| Defender's mobilisation | Amplifier | army up from about 200,000 to about 500,000; elite republican guard from 10-20,000 to about 200,000 (as reported, timing unclear; mostly 1986-88); war economy | Plausible |
| Isolation and sanctions | Amplifier | no spare parts, so aircraft conserved; the other side had foreign arms and Arab finance | Strong |
| War on cities and shipping | Mechanism | bombing of cities, attacks on tankers and an export terminal (54 attacks against 18 in 1984); aim: demoralise and draw in major powers | Plausible |
**Contested**: the attacker's motive (punish the neighbour and spark a revolt among its co-religionists). **Continuity**: the war went on beyond 1985; the later phases are not covered here. Chemical weapons were used by the defender in these years.

## 14.2 The Imjin War (1592-98)
**Question**: why did a veteran land army that won in the field fail to hold a peninsula?
- **Premise**: a newly unified state with hundreds of thousands of warriors, a ruler who needed spoils for his vassals and wanted to go beyond his islands; conquered lands were to supply forces for the next push.
- **Failure**: defender's navy denied the sea lane; partisans attacked river supply barges; winter losses (about 150,000 reduced to about 53,000 in one phase); stalemate; summer foraging; peace talks with the large neighbour that was the defender's ally; a final blockade and night attack in a strait destroyed about 450 of 500 ships; the admiral died.
- **Consequences**: the commander who had not drained his resources won the next civil war in 1600, founding a dynasty that ruled for about 250 years; the two victors were weakened and a later dynasty fell in 1644 (as reported); the defender stayed independent but isolated. **Contested**: how decisive the navy was relative to winter and supply (the navy is usually credited); how much of the invasion reflected the ruler's ambition versus a plan to keep vassals busy.

## 14.3 The siege on a great river (1862-63)
- **Setting**: a bluff fortress controlled a river meander; a naval attempt failed for lack of troops, disease and falling water; land approaches ran through swamp and exposed rail lines.
- **Defender**: ambiguous command lines, a head of state who bypassed the theatre commander and told the defender to hold at all costs, a promised relief force that was too weak; the commander's preference to leave and fight in the open was overruled by a subordinate.
- **Attacker**: first campaign failed because the plan was obvious and a cavalry raid destroyed the forward supply base; five bypass attempts over the winter (canals, a pass, bayous) mostly failed (whether they were meant to succeed or were make-work is contested); the later crossing south and rapid march cut off the relief force; two assaults failed; siege, mines and starvation; surrender on 4 July 1863 with parole offered to avoid moving 30,000 prisoners; a second river fortress fell five days later; the Confederacy was split. Political generals intrigued for independent commands until the senior general restored unity.
- **Not covered**: the middle part of the campaign; whether this victory outweighed the other great battle of the same week.

## 14.4 Rome and the lands east of the Rhine (1st century BC to 2nd century AD)
Proposed causes for the absence of conquest: logistics (range of about 24 days), no strongholds or wealth to leverage, a low perceived return, the 9 AD loss of three legions, competing commitments (about 500,000 troops over about 7,500 km), cultural and legal distance, a preference for alliances and trade; consequences: later recruitment of allied peoples and the rise of successor kingdoms in the west (these later conquered Roman provinces and gave rise to modern states; not examined here). See 9.1, 9.4. **Contested**: how much of the failure was real constraint and how much Roman perception (modern settlement estimates are higher than Roman accounts).

## 14.5 Rome and its eastern rival (1st century BC to 7th century AD)
Proposed explanation of a stable frontier: a logistical and political equilibrium (9.3); the older view of a failed ideology is now considered heavily dated. A deep campaign in 114 AD took a capital and failed to finish the rival; later gains shifted the frontier east; the rival's civil war and centralisation led to a counter-offensive in 253 and, in 602-628, to a deep thrust during a Roman civil war, with only tenuous control. **Contested**: Roman motives for the 114 campaign (prestige; trade-route access, rejected by modern economic historians for lack of evidence; a defensive line); revenue comparisons are based on a predecessor state's figures (about 50-60 million bronze drachma, 100-125% campaign cost for a big army, as estimated) and are uncertain.

## 14.6 The Eastern Front, summer 1941
Proposed explanation of the early German success and its limits: encirclement on open ground; slow, exposed armour in forest and marsh; the defender's piecemeal commitments and poor intelligence; the attacker's logistic gaps (gauge conversion, too few railway troops, fuel falling with distance, no captured rolling stock); the head of state's reversal of his own order; the defender's industrial evacuation east. **Contested**: how much the evacuated plants contributed to the 1942 recovery (unclear). **Note**: the sheet covers a few weeks and says nothing about the rest of the war.

## 14.7 The central Pacific offensive, 1943-44
Method: seize bases for airfields, isolate and bypass others (9.6). Evidence of attacker advantage: shipbuilding gap (as reported); the first assaults were costly and the lessons were applied next time. **Contested**: the cost-effectiveness of bypassing and the timing of bypass decisions (not examined here). The defender's response: a new defence line; "willingness to die" is an interpretation.

## 14.8 The pre-1914 naval race
See 12.3. **Mechanism**: a doctrine that sea power determines national prosperity; a risk theory; a technology reset in 1906; an industrial-political coalition ("navy league", press, industry) and a minister with a veto on reduction; a fiscal squeeze; a counter-coalition. **Outcome**: Britain kept a margin; Germany failed to force an equal status, was diplomatically encircled and moved to its army. **Contested**: whether the race made war more likely.

## 14.9 The Great Northern War
Proposed cause of the loser's defeat: after an initial war in which the northern power beat three neighbours, its leader refused to make peace and invaded the large eastern neighbour, which turned a won minor war into a long one; diplomatic isolation (earlier it had kept western powers out; later everyone "piled up"); one opponent was also ruler of another state and dragged his own kingdom in. **Contested**: whether the loser ever had a real chance; one view says yes, only if it had stopped after the first victory.

## 14.10 The Irish famine, 1845-52
Winter of 1846-47 phase. Proposed mechanisms: a blight on a staple crop on which small tenants and landless labourers depended; food prices doubled; relief through workhouses designed to be so harsh that the poor would enter only as a last resort and which were overfull; evictions by landlords with police and soldiers; public-works roads that went nowhere (about 700,000 on works by the end of January 1847, as reported) with deaths from exhaustion; no fishing equipment because nets had been pawned; hunger among small landowners; armed attacks on food convoys. Not covered: exports of food, the debate on policy, death totals, emigration. **Contested** (general knowledge): responsibility of policy versus the blight.

## Brief notes (too thin for a sheet)
- **A first war between an empire and a trading power (19th century)**: only an imperial reply to a trade mission is considered; it shows a ruler's self-presentation of tribute and benevolence, a case of the reading of rulers' texts as reports (1.2).
- **Japanese militarism (1930s)**: a coup attempt aimed at direct imperial rule failed partly because the monarch did not want it, and that the post-war occupation kept the monarchy to provide continuity and stability.
- **A trading empire of West Africa**: on the evidence: an oral epic gives memory and attitude, not facts; the ruler's wealth is real but some travel details are probably exaggerated; four currencies (gold dust, gold nuggets held as a royal monopoly to avoid flooding the market, cowrie shells that later inflated, salt); a river navy by a successor state. See 1.7.
- **Viking expansion**: a raider chief given land turned into a ruler, built fortifications, codified law, married locally; his descendants were French-speaking Christians within a generation; another raider chief took a monastery's rents and founded a fortified harbour town. It shows assimilation (2.6).
