# Chapter 3: Environment, Climate and Resource Dependence

## Core Idea
Societies live inside environmental envelopes and build their institutions around them. This chapter treats climate variability, soil and forest, water systems, staple crops and marginal land as parts of one question: how large is the safety margin between the usual year and the worst year the society must survive? Most of it is **position**; attribution of any particular collapse to climate is debated and each case in chapter 6 gives the evidence and the dispute.

## 3.1 Variability beats averages
- **Thesis**: collapses follow extremes and swings, not changes in the mean.
- **Mechanism**: lowland Maya rainfall varies strongly year to year, so droughts were normal and infrastructure (cisterns, plastered reservoirs, raised fields) was built for them. A monsoon city's tree-ring record shows, after the mid-1300s, both multi-decade droughts and very wet spells alternating, with the driest and wettest years of the millennium clustered within a century. A system sized for one or two dry years, or for flood or drought but not both, fails when the pattern changes.
- **Trade-off**: designing for extremes costs capacity and labour.
- **Does not hold when**: the environment is truly stable; unusual in the cases here.

## 3.2 Drought as a threat multiplier
- **Thesis (position)**: drought rarely kills a society alone; it exposes other weaknesses.
- **Mechanism**: drought reduces harvest, which strains storage, which raises demand on rulers, which increases conflict and migration. Two cases propose a severe dry episode: the late Classic Maya (a drought in the eighth century linked in some accounts to a low in solar output and a northward shift of the Atlantic trade winds) and the eastern Mediterranean in the thirteenth and twelfth centuries BC (pollen, sediment and cave-isotope records of reduced rain; letters from hungry cities). Both attributions are debated (timing, size, regional footprint, cause).
- **Trade-off**: climate explanations give an external, non-moral cause; they can obscure social choices that made the society vulnerable.
- **Does not hold when**: records show comparable droughts survived earlier without collapse; then the question is what changed in the society.

## 3.3 Soil, forest and the slope problem
- **Thesis**: clearing for fields, fuel and building degrades the base that feeds the cities.
- **Mechanism**: in a Maya valley city, population grew, then spilled onto hillsides; pollen shows pines cut, then hill soils eroded into the valley bottom, lowering its fertility too; skeletons show more disease and malnutrition from about the mid-seventh century onwards. Lime plaster for monuments required burning limestone in great pits, consuming wood. In Greenland, clearing low shrubs and burning wood for heat (instead of seal blubber as neighbours did) exposed the land to wind and sand. Slash-and-burn needs long fallow; growth pressure shortens it.
- **Trade-off**: deforestation brings short-term gains (fields, fuel, building) for long-term loss.
- **Does not hold when**: erosion is natural or from another cause; check that the pollen and sediment layers follow settlement, not climate shifts.

## 3.4 Staple crops, storage and protein
- **Thesis**: a narrow food base is a dependency with a clock on it.
- **Mechanism**: maize is low in protein; in the humid lowlands it could be stored little over a year before it moulded. Terraced and raised fields are labour hungry. A rice-based economy supported multiple harvests per year while water control worked; a livestock-and-hay economy in the far north depended on a short grazing season and on imports of grain and iron. Dependence on one staple means one failure matters; short storage means a bad year cannot be carried over.
- **Trade-off**: specialisation yields surplus; diversity yields resilience at lower peak output.
- **Does not hold when**: the staple is complemented by robust alternatives (fishing, sea mammals, trade) that can be scaled up.

## 3.5 Hydraulic infrastructure: lock-in and maintenance
- **Thesis (position)**: large water systems create wealth and a maintenance obligation that cannot be missed.
- **Mechanism**: Angkor's reservoirs, canals and embankments (the largest earthworks before the modern industrial era in one account) allowed several rice harvests a year, fed a city whose farmland and temples interleaved, and required constant desilting and repair. Complexity of the junctions meant that one failure could ripple. Signs of strain: emergency channels built in a drought and later blocked or widened for floods, a bridge built from stones of temples, a moat overgrown with swamp vegetation.
- **Trade-off**: control of water yields surplus and legitimacy; it ties the society to an upkeep schedule.
- **Does not hold when**: the system is modular and cheap to repair, or the settlement can shrink to a part of the network.

## 3.6 Marginal land amplifies climate
- **Thesis**: at the edge of habitability a small shift in temperature or sea ice becomes decisive.
- **Mechanism**: a northern colony sat near the range limit for grain, hay and livestock. A warm spell before about 1300 allowed an imported European lifestyle; a cooling afterwards (proposed causes, all debated: low solar output, volcanic eruptions, even reduced human population after plague) shortened seasons, expanded sea ice that blocked fjords and ships, and increased storms. Ice cores show more sea salt, interpreted as stormier seas.
- **Trade-off**: marginal sites give access to unique resources (ivory); they offer no slack.
- **Does not hold when**: the marginal group is flexible in diet and technology and has a nearby model to copy.

## 3.7 Volcanic and solar forcing
- **Thesis (position)**: short abrupt climate shocks can come from eruptions; long swings from solar or ocean changes.
- **Mechanism**: a large eruption in the north of the Atlantic is proposed for the end of the Bronze Age (one estimate dates it around 1100 BC; tree-growth records from bog oaks and bristlecone pines show about two decades of suppressed growth). Both the dating and the effect on the Mediterranean are debated, and the claim that it caused the collapse is a hypothesis. A different eruption in the thirteenth century AD is one proposed trigger of later cooling.
- **Trade-off**: externally forced shocks are testable (match dates across archives); easy to over-fit.
- **Does not hold when**: dates in the archives differ by more than the claimed lag, or the signal at the site is absent.

## 3.8 Attribution: environment damage, climate, or both
- **Thesis (position)**: ask which part is self-inflicted and which is imposed, and do not assume one.
- **Mechanism**: the usual error is to pick either "ecocide" or "climate victim" for a whole case. Better: describe the pre-existing damage (deforestation, soil loss), the climate shift (with dates), and how damage lowered resilience to the shift. A case can include careful resource management (limiting hunting of an easily caught species) and damage elsewhere.
- **Trade-off**: both-and accounts are less dramatic.
- **Does not hold when**: evidence clearly excludes one factor (e.g. no deforestation signal in pollen).

## 3.9 Adaptation: what counts, what blocks it
- **Thesis (position)**: judge adaptation by what people did, not by what they wore or ate for identity.
- **Mechanism**: a long-standing story says the northern colonists refused to learn from their neighbours. Isotope analysis of bones shows the marine share of the diet rising to about four fifths late in the settlement; soil-fertilising effort increased; hunting avoided the most easily exterminated seal. At the same time, wool clothing in European fashion persisted into the fifteenth century, and iron tools were worn to stubs. A claimed cultural aversion to fish rests on missing fish bones, which may simply not survive. The result: adaptation was real and partial; what limited it is debated (identity, lack of materials, labour spent on long hunting trips).
- **Trade-off**: copying a neighbour's technology can cost identity and social standing.
- **Does not hold when**: the technology to copy is not available locally (no driftwood, no sea-ice skill, no seal-blubber stoves in a society with a different housing tradition).

## 3.10 Slow variables and fast shocks
- **Thesis**: slow changes (soil, forest, institutions) set the margin; fast shocks (drought year, storm, siege) decide when the margin is gone.
- **Mechanism**: a drought hits a densely populated region on eroded land harder than the same drought on healthy land. A storm that sinks an entire hunting fleet is catastrophic where men were all away on long trips. Ask for each case: which variable was slow, which was fast, and how long was the lag between them.
- **Trade-off**: slow variables are hard to see in the record; fast ones are easy to date.
- **Does not hold when**: only one of the two can be documented; then say so.
