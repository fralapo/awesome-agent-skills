# Chapter 10: Institutions and Crisis of a Republic

## Core Idea
The late Roman Republic is the case here for how a system of **unwritten norms and mutual vetoes** can work for centuries and then fail when actors stop restraining themselves. The chapter has two parts: how the institutions were designed (10.1 to 10.5) and how they eroded (10.6 to 10.10). Most points are **position**; the chapter covers the institutions in detail and the civil-war period in outline, but not the last year of the system (the assassination and the failed attempt to restore the republic; no claim is made about them). Roman writers themselves blamed moral decline; modern readings differ (10.9). Roman figures appear here by role, not by name.

## 10.1 One-year offices with two holders
- **Thesis**: the republic was designed against kingship: short terms, paired officials, a sequence of offices.
- **Mechanism**: the main offices were held one year at a time (to keep anyone from "getting comfortable" and seizing power) and by two chief magistrates at once. The career path was a ladder with minimum ages: a first office around age 30 gave a lifetime seat in the senate (about 20 holders a year in the late period, against four open places early on, when a few families monopolised them); an office running games, grain and buildings in the mid-30s; a judicial and military office around 39 (eight a year); the chief magistracy around 42. Years were named after the two chief magistrates. The senate was therefore made of ex-magistrates: a hint of electoral legitimacy and a strong hint of oligarchy.
- **Trade-off**: short terms prevent a king and also prevent continuity, so experience sits in the senate, not in the offices.
- **Does not hold when**: extraordinary commands or lifetime offices are created (10.7, 10.8).

## 10.2 The mutual veto and the alternation rule
- **Thesis**: shared power works only if both holders restrain themselves, and the system relied on convention for that.
- **Mechanism**: each chief magistrate could veto the other; a tribune of the common people could veto any magistrate. Both knew this to be a mutual-destruction situation, so they alternated who held the symbols of authority each month (in the field, each day) and mostly let the other lead. The single case recorded where a daily alternation shaped a battle: one commander wanted to wait and the other, on his day in command, attacked. Convention also covered the order of speech in the senate (strict seniority, backbenchers who could only vote by walking to a side), the date of festivals and the reading of omens.
- **Trade-off**: convention is cheap and flexible; it has no enforcement when someone ignores it.
- **Does not hold when**: a body outside the pair (a crowd, an army) enforces one side's will.

## 10.3 Agenda control, elections and legislation
- **Thesis**: power lay in who convenes, who presides and who frames the question.
- **Mechanism**: chief magistrates were elected months before taking office, arriving with a legislative programme; they convened the senate and the assemblies and presided over elections of their successors (so intimidation, bribery and deals happened "under their noses"). The popular assembly voted yes or no on a proposal after at least three market days, did not debate, and heard the convener's chosen speakers; attendance was reportedly low and crowds could be filled with supporters. The result is a system in which the pairing of senate approval and an assembly "rubber stamp" served whoever controlled the agenda.
- **Trade-off**: organised procedure prevents chaos and concentrates leverage in the presiding office.
- **Does not hold when**: a hostile presiding colleague blocks the process by veto or ritual calendar.

## 10.4 Weighted voting
- **Thesis**: the electoral design built oligarchy into the arithmetic.
- **Mechanism**: in the assembly that elected the highest offices, voters were placed in 193 blocks by wealth class and age; the noble families (about 6 blocks) and the richest equestrians (about 12) held roughly 9% of the blocks; the richest non-aristocratic class held 80; classes 2 to 4 held 20 each, class 5 held 30, and the propertyless, by far the largest group, held one single block. Blocks voted from the top down and voting stopped when a candidate reached an absolute majority, so if the elites agreed the poor never voted. A second assembly gave one vote to each of 35 geographic tribes with **hereditary** tribal membership; after generations of migration the tribes no longer matched where people lived, and four city tribes held the urban poor. Censors kept the lists and class assignments.
- **Trade-off**: weighting by wealth and age gave stability and elite buy-in; it left the urban poor and migrants with little legal voice and a street.
- **Does not hold when**: the question is whether the weights mattered in practice; the poor decided only when the elite split.

## 10.5 Offices as incentive: provinces, spoils and trials
- **Thesis**: the system paid its officials through the provinces, which created incentives for war and extraction.
- **Mechanism**: after a year in office the judicial magistrate was eligible for a provincial governorship allotted by the senate; the governor held supreme military and judicial power with little oversight; bribes were taken and commanders kept about a fifth of war booty (as reported); the officials needed to recoup the cost of their election. Rich or rebellious provinces were therefore preferred over poor, quiet ones. Courts were public and political: the judge chose the punishment, there was almost no prison system, and trials were spectator events with applause and fights. The Romans saw no difference between political and military talent, which may explain why orators received armies. The free grain for the city poor made grain provinces vital.
- **Trade-off**: paying officials through the provinces avoided a salaried bureaucracy; it made foreign policy depend on personal enrichment.
- **Does not hold when**: a governor is closely supervised, which the system rarely provided.

## 10.6 Norm-breaking and what answers it
- **Thesis**: once a prominent actor breaks a convention without penalty, others follow, and the equilibrium shifts.
- **Mechanism**: one chief magistrate wanted a land-distribution law for veterans and the urban poor. His colleague vetoed it before the assembly vote; a crowd shouted him down and drove him off the platform, and the proposer put the bill to the vote anyway, claiming not to have heard the veto. The colleague then tried obstruction by declaring festival days on the proposer's key voting days and reading omens against him, and the proposer, who held the chief priesthood, overruled him. In strict law both acts were illegal; the public went along, and the colleague withdrew to his house for the rest of the year. This is a pattern: veto ignored by popular force, ritual powers used as weapons, and a protest that costs the loser nothing because no one enforces the rule.
- **Trade-off**: a norm-based system adapts without amendment; it cannot punish the first mover.
- **Does not hold when**: courts or an independent guard can try the offender at once; here prosecution waited until the offender's term (and immunity) ended.

## 10.7 Emergency measures and their price
- **Thesis**: emergency powers solve the crisis of the day and create a precedent and a grievance.
- **Mechanism**: the senate could pass a "final decree" authorising the chief magistrates to protect the republic by any means (in effect martial law). In one conspiracy the officeholder executed prisoners without trial because he feared a wider plot and no time; support for the conspiracy collapsed and he was praised, then questioned about it for the rest of his life. Later, when armed gangs ruled the streets and the senate was paralysed, it gave one popular general an extraordinary command; he restored order but, as a result, one man held both military and civil authority, which the rules prohibited and which became a precedent. Another decree was passed when no chief magistrates were in office, so its legal force was doubtful.
- **Trade-off**: emergency powers can save the system and be used against it.
- **Does not hold when**: the emergency has a clear end and a review by an independent body; none is recorded.

## 10.8 Immunity, prosecution and the army
- **Thesis**: when leaving office means prosecution and the commander's soldiers depend on him for land, the commander has a personal reason not to leave.
- **Mechanism**: a governor who had conquered a large province controlled about 50,000 soldiers nearing retirement; the standard reward was land, which required legislation the senate did not provide; his conquests and promises of citizenship to local nobles also required senate acts; his enemies had sworn to prosecute him for earlier illegalities once his immunity ended. Two rules clashed (a governor with an army may not enter the city; a candidate must stand in person); normally the man resigned early and entered as a private citizen. Here a law passed by the tribunes allowed him to stand while absent, so that he moved from governor to chief magistrate and back and kept his immunity indefinitely; the opposing faction tried to repeal it and was blocked by a tribune's veto. Negotiation failed and the commander crossed the boundary with a single half-strength legion (about 2,500 men) which amounted to a declaration of war; the senate declared him a public enemy; his closest lieutenant refused to follow and left, taking no legion. In the civil war that followed, the first man to be left in charge of the city mishandled a street crisis (a tribune pushed a debt-cancellation bill; a crowd occupied the forum and brought weapons across the sacred boundary; he withdrew the army, then returned and killed hundreds of citizens); the later settlement involved the property of the defeated, which recalled an earlier civil war.
- **Trade-off**: soldiers loyal to a commander give the state an effective army and the commander a private lever.
- **Does not hold when**: the state itself settles veterans and shields officials from vengeance; then the army's loyalty stays with the state.

## 10.9 Moral decline versus structure (contested)
- **Thesis (position, ancient)**: the republic fell because it abandoned its customs of honesty, piety, discipline and service to the state; two turning points were named: the destruction of a rival city (no external fear, so no discipline) and the conquest of the east (wealth and luxury, offices sought for riches).
- **Thesis (position, modern)**: moral decline, if real, is secondary; structure explains more: constitutional weaknesses, concentration of land and rural bankruptcy, alienation of the lower classes.
- **Mechanism of the critique**: ancient writers could not criticise the institution without implicitly criticising the customs that built it, so they blamed individuals; they thought in terms of great men; ethical codes describe what a society aspires to, not what it does; the paradox is that the man who ended the system showed the virtues the customs praised (a "no true Roman" defence that works only after the fact); contemporaries on all sides believed they were acting for the republic; critics of reform held that power belonged to "the most virtuous", opposed the secret ballot and called popular reformers immoral, a partisan label.
- **Trade-off**: moral explanations are vivid and unfalsifiable; structural ones are testable and less satisfying.
- **Does not hold when**: a society's customs are the only constraint on its rulers (as in 10.2); then norms are structure.

## 10.10 A monarch in all but name
- **Thesis**: erosion often ends not with a coup but with a series of offices, honours and symbols that sum to monarchy.
- **Mechanism**: after the civil war the winner was elected chief magistrate while absent ("against the rules"), named dictator for ten years, granted a triumph over fellow citizens (illegitimate in kind, and in poor taste), given titles, a robe and floral crown of a kind associated with kings, a golden chair between the chief magistrates, a statue among the statues of the kings, and a power to expel senators for "bad morals", while the senate was filled with his allies. Opposition took small forms (a tribune who refused to stand as he passed; a group of senators who sounded out a lieutenant, who did not report it). One summary of such processes is "gradually, then suddenly."
- **Trade-off**: gradual accumulation avoids a single legal violation and a single point of resistance.
- **Does not hold when**: each step meets organised opposition early; then the trend stops.

## Using this chapter
For any republic or council system, ask: which rules are written and which are customary; who enforces them; what is the veto structure; how are officials paid; what are the rules for emergency; whose loyalty do the armed forces have; what happens to an official who leaves office. A comparison to a modern system is analogy and follows chapter 7.
