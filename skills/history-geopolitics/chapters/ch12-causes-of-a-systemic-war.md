# Chapter 12: Causes of a Systemic War: A Contested Multi-Causal Analysis

## Core Idea
How does a regional crisis become a general war between great powers? The case is the outbreak of the First World War. The chapter does not give an answer; it gives a **structure for holding several answers at once** and a register for each claim: **proposed cause / evidence / contested**. No single cause is offered. Most readings give more weight to the long background and the July decisions than to the war aims later claimed; the war-aims claims are marked as changing over time. Positions of whole schools of historians are named as positions, without names.

## 12.1 Layers of cause
| Layer | What it contains in this case | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Long fuse (decades) | industrialisation, urbanisation and inequality; ruling groups using nationalism and imperial expansion to channel popular tension; a newly unified great power wanting a "place in the sun" | Underlying |
| Medium term (years) | rival alliance blocs, a naval race, repeated colonial and Balkan crises, a decaying multi-ethnic empire | Amplifier |
| Trigger | an assassination in a contested province | Trigger |
| July decisions (weeks) | an unacceptable ultimatum, a guarantee of support, mobilisation timetables, a violation of a neutral state's territory | Mechanism |
| Symptom or later reading | war aims stated after the war began | Not a cause |

A one-line answer collapses these layers (compare patterns 1 and 20).

## 12.2 The long fuse: nationalism and social tension
- **Proposed cause**: ruling groups managed industrial-age inequality by promoting national pride and imperial expansion; this created competition between empires and popular cultures of rivalry (a jingoistic press, military education, toys and novels about enemies).
- **Evidence**: described as trends in culture and politics; no measurement of the effect on decisions is available.
- **Contested**: whether popular nationalism pushed governments or governments used it; whether war was seen as a practical tool (one position: the cultural and strategic mood treated war as a desirable instrument of social change and national interest).
- **Status**: plausible; the causal weight is unclear.

## 12.3 A rising power and its fleet
- **Proposed cause**: a newly unified state with a growing commercial class and strong social-democratic parties built an overseas empire and a blue-water navy; the strategy rested on a doctrine of sea power and on a "risk" idea (a fleet two thirds the size of the leading navy would force the leader to negotiate as an equal).
- **Evidence**: five naval laws between 1898 and 1912; a new all-big-gun turbine ship in 1906 that made existing fleets obsolete and reset the race; the leading navy's two-power standard and later a 60% margin; an alliance with an eastern naval power in 1902, an entente with the old rival in 1904 and with a continental power in 1907; the weaker side's debt doubled in 1900-1909 (as reported); by 1914 the leading navy had a margin of about 62% in new capital ships (as reported). Attempts at a naval agreement failed in 1912 over a neutrality clause and a naval amendment introduced the day before the negotiator's visit.
- **Contested**: whether the race pushed Britain into the opposing alliance (encirclement perception) or whether it had other reasons; whether it contributed to the war (it is said only that it "contributed to broader militarisation"); it also failed on its own terms because the fleet stayed in port during the war.
- **Status**: strong for the race itself, plausible as a cause of the alignment, unclear as a cause of war.

## 12.4 Alliance blocs and crises
- **Proposed cause**: a web of alliances and agreements, tested by repeated crises, made each crisis more dangerous: two crises over a north African territory (1905, 1911) intended to split two powers and having the opposite effect; an annexation in 1908 that angered the neighbouring state and the empire's former owner; a war in which a weak state lost a province quickly (1911), after which Balkan states formed a league and drove the old empire out of the peninsula (1912-13), then fought each other.
- **Evidence**: sequence of events; a power gaining a seaport it had been denied; a secret organisation with many officers and officials among its members.
- **Contested**: whether alliances **forced** states to fight. One recent position: no; one member of the bloc stayed neutral in 1914; many points in the crisis could have gone differently (an earlier ultimatum, a clear British position at the start).
- **Status**: the alliance web is strong as an amplifier; the claim of automatic entry is weak.

## 12.5 A weak multi-ethnic empire and a nationalist neighbour
- **Proposed cause**: an empire with many national groups and an elderly ruler, with a poor fiscal system (it paid off minorities and spent less on its army than a smaller rival), annexed a province and then did nothing structural about Slavic discontent; a young neighbour, independent since 1878 and nationalist ("where a member of our people lives, there is our state"), repressed its own minorities and was not widely trusted; the neighbour's goals could only be reached at the empire's expense.
- **Evidence**: annexation of 1908; the neighbour's doubled territory after the Balkan wars; a political murder of 1903 and an officer-led secret society in the neighbour.
- **Contested**: how far the neighbour's government was involved in the assassination; the evidence does not settle it.
- **Status**: strong for structural tension; unclear for attribution.

## 12.6 July: ultimatum, guarantee, mobilisation
- **Proposed cause**: decisions made in weeks. The empire's government was divided; one minister delayed by insisting on an ultimatum; the text was drafted to be rejected; it was delivered when the allied leaders were at sea; a guarantee of support from the main ally had been given beforehand ("blank cheque"). Great-power mediation proposals were refused. A "partial" mobilisation in the east turned out to be impossible in the general staff's plans; mobilisation in one army pressed the neighbour to do the same, because modern rail transport could bring enemy troops to the border in days; military timetables gave generals more influence than ministers. The offensive plan of the strongest army required passing through a neutral state, which brought in a great maritime power.
- **Evidence**: dates and texts of ultimatums, telegrams and mobilisation orders; a statement before the crisis that the allied power's first concern was to bring the maritime power into the alliance.
- **Contested**: whether the main ally intended to widen the war or tried to localise it; how much the east's partial mobilisation counted as a threat; what France and the east knew in advance.
- **Status**: strong for the sequence; contested for intentions.

## 12.7 Schools of explanation (positions)
- **Premeditation**: the strongest power's government and military elite had large aims and planned a war against two neighbours and pushed the empire to escalate. Evidence cited: war aims documents of 1914; contested because those documents come after the war began.
- **Single-state blame, other states**: another position blames the eastern empire for turning a Balkan war into a continental war; another blames the maritime power's naive diplomacy.
- **Sleepwalking**: miscalculation, misunderstanding and perceived insecurity; the strongest power thought the east and the maritime power would stay out and tried to localise the conflict.
- **Structure and mentality**: the culture of the time treated war as a rational tool; military inflexibility made "perceived military necessity" dictate politics, the opposite of the idea that war is politics by other means.
- **Trade-off**: each school illuminates one layer and fits the others badly.
- **Does not hold when**: new documents show a single decision (none is claimed).

## 12.8 War aims: a later layer, not a cause
- **Thesis**: aims stated during a war are partly consequences of the war and cannot be read back as reasons for starting it.
- **Mechanism**: the empire's government initially wanted a local war to reduce the neighbour and preserve great-power status; aims later broadened and then narrowed, ending in dependence on its ally. The strongest power's aims are the most debated: in early days talk of large annexations; a September 1914 programme with satellite states and a German-led economic bloc; a compromise peace offer in 1916 that did not give up the occupied territories; a parliamentary resolution in 1917 against annexations; a dictated peace in the east in 1918 that fulfilled aims there; hopes for annexations in the west into summer 1918. A third empire joined in 1914 for security against a northern neighbour and to cancel one-sided economic treaties, and used the war for internal consolidation, including the deportation and killing of Christian groups. A northern empire acted partly to protect its prestige after earlier defeats and a diplomatic humiliation in 1908, later aimed at the straits; after a revolution it proposed a peace without annexations.
- **Trade-off**: a war-aims list gives a clear picture and may mix intent with opportunity.
- **Does not hold when**: aims are documented before the war.

## 12.9 Why a long war and not a short one
- **Thesis**: the outcome after 1914 differed from the plan because of a failed offensive and the economics in chapter 11.
- **Mechanism**: one argument holds that after the failed offensive of 1914 it was impossible to win and that very grave mistakes were needed to lose: the high command did not reassess strategy, wasted manpower in the offensives of 1916, failed to maintain food production, badly managed its alliance, was governed by a military that could not run the economy, resumed unrestricted submarine warfare that brought in a great new enemy, and then gambled on a series of spring offensives in 1918 that cost the best units (about 280,000 casualties in two months and about 977,000 by end of July, as reported) without a decisive objective. The allies made mistakes too but had a margin. A neutral first-hand view, "was the war pointless?", depends on each state's aims and on what it could have obtained otherwise.
- **Trade-off**: counterfactual claims ("could have won") are easy to state and hard to test.
- **Does not hold when**: used to assign blame for the origin; the reasons for the long war differ from the reasons for its start.

## 12.10 Checks before writing a multi-causal origin story
1. Put each claim in a layer (12.1) and give its evidence grade (ch08).
2. Ask the "survivor" question: who was in the same alliance system and did not fight (one member stayed neutral).
3. Separate **decision** from **capability** (a guarantee, a timetable).
4. Mark aims stated after the war began.
5. Say which school each claim supports; keep two on the page.
6. Do not turn "structure made it likely" into "it was inevitable".

For any other systemic war, build the same five-row table and fill each row; if one row is empty, say so. Then run chapter 11 for what sustained the war and chapter 7 for any analogy.
