# Chapter 4: Trade, Interdependence and Cascading Crises

## Core Idea
Trade lets societies specialise and survive local shortfalls; it also turns a local failure into a regional one. This chapter treats a trade network as a dependency graph: which goods are essential, which links are single points of failure, which partners are correlated, and what happens when the lifeline's hub is damaged. Chapter 6 gives the case evidence; the models here are **position**, each with its counter-case.

## 4.1 Essential goods versus luxuries
- **Thesis**: the dependency that matters is the one for a good you cannot make or replace and that your tools, weapons or food system run on.
- **Mechanism**: a Late Bronze Age ship wrecked off the southern coast of Anatolia (about 1300 BC) carried around ten tons of copper ingots and one ton of tin, the ratio needed for bronze, plus glass, ivory, resins, fruit, and gold jewellery from at least seven lands. The luxuries show the reach of the network; the metals show its structural load: without a steady metal supply armies cannot be equipped and craftsmen lose tools. A common analogy compares tin then to crude oil now (an analogy, see 7.10).
- **Trade-off**: importing the essential good allows a large, specialised society; it makes the importer hostage to the route.
- **Does not hold when**: the good can be replaced by another material (bronze by iron, with the lag and cost of a technological switch) or stockpiled for years.

## 4.2 Single route, single source
- **Thesis (position)**: look for the chokepoint behind the whole system.
- **Mechanism**: in one account, tin came from far-eastern mines by donkey caravan across mountains to the cities of Mesopotamia and then by sea to Greece, a chain with no slack. Whether tin really came from one region is itself uncertain in the wider literature (other origins are debated); what matters for the method is to ask for each essential input how many independent sources and routes exist.
- **Trade-off**: a short chain is cheap and efficient; redundancy needs extra routes and stock.
- **Does not hold when**: there are alternative sources at slightly higher cost, which means the dependency is soft.

## 4.3 Export-commodity dependence and substitution shock
- **Thesis**: a colony or city that lives from one export is exposed to the buyer's market.
- **Mechanism**: a far-northern colony monopolised high-value walrus ivory (one boatload from about 260 animals was valued more than the cloth tribute of thousands of farms, and equal to hundreds of cows, in one document of 1327). Late in the period, higher-quality elephant ivory began to enter European markets via southern trade routes, and the value of the northern product fell (presented as part of the decline; its size is debated). Income from the export paid for imported iron, grain, timber and church goods, so a price fall cuts the lifeline.
- **Trade-off**: specialisation pays well while the market lasts.
- **Does not hold when**: export income was never the main input of the settlement (it could feed itself) or other exports (wool, cheese, sealskins) replace it.

## 4.4 Distance, lifelines and the hub
- **Thesis**: a settlement that cannot reproduce its tools, timber or institutions depends on its supply voyages, and those depend on the hub port.
- **Mechanism**: northern colonists could not make iron or large timber; one colonist built a ship from wooden pegs and sinew and sailed it home, then lost it on the next leg, showing the lack of materials. When the home kingdom's main port suffered a plague (a ship from abroad carried the disease, and mortality in the kingdom is estimated at 40 to 50 percent in some accounts, with chronicles saying more) and later was burnt by pirate raiders, supply voyages slowed to a trickle; the last bishop died around 1378 and no new bishop was sent; iron objects vanish from late layers and tools are worn to stubs. The plague did not reach the colony itself (according to some accounts), yet harmed it through the hub.
- **Trade-off**: connection to a rich centre allows colonies in impossible places; the centre's troubles are inherited.
- **Does not hold when**: the periphery is self-sufficient for the key inputs or has another hub.

## 4.5 Mutual aid works for local shocks, fails for common ones
- **Thesis (position)**: alliances and gift exchange insure against uncorrelated risk.
- **Mechanism**: Bronze Age states sent grain to each other in famine, exchanged craftsmen and married into each other's houses. If many are hit at once (a widespread drought, raids on every coast), everyone's army is tied down and no one can send help. The last king of a trading port wrote that his troops and ships were in distant allied territory when the enemy fleet arrived, and that appeal for aid was never sent. A neighbouring kingdom, whose allies were busy, was then invaded by its eastern rival and its king and sacred statue were taken.
- **Trade-off**: alliances let small states act big; they commit forces far from home.
- **Does not hold when**: the shocks are local and the allies' resources are free; then aid works.

## 4.6 Contagion through people
- **Thesis**: collapse spreads by movement of refugees and by raised competition for what remains.
- **Mechanism**: in the Maya lowlands collapsing cities would send refugees to nearby ones, straining them; as large polities atomised into small kingdoms, rivals took what they needed from neighbours; evidence of late fortification and burnt buildings appears at several sites. In the eastern Mediterranean, the "Sea Peoples" may have been a loose, diverse, leaderless confederation, perhaps in part displaced groups, taking jobs as mercenaries earlier and raiding later; this is one hypothesis among several (see 6.3). Evidence of movement: foreign pottery, new settlement of a named group in a coastal area, weapon types.
- **Trade-off**: people moving out relieves pressure at home and creates it elsewhere.
- **Does not hold when**: the evidence of in-migration is absent and the destruction can be explained locally.

## 4.7 Dependence on a distant patron
- **Thesis**: a polity propped by an external great power is vulnerable to the patron's fall.
- **Mechanism**: a Maya capital rose with the help of a distant, much larger city in central Mexico: a green obsidian trade, soldiers with distinctive spear throwers, an embassy shown on a pot. Accounts treat it as a partnership or a quiet takeover (unclear which; a conquest, a palace coup and a diplomatic intervention are all possible). When the patron fell under unclear circumstances in the sixth century, the capital was left alone among enemies and was overrun a few years later (context: its own king had attacked a former ally).
- **Trade-off**: patronage buys fast growth; it also hands control to someone else.
- **Does not hold when**: the client had already built independent power.

## 4.8 Trade as shock absorber versus transmission channel
- **Thesis (position)**: the same network lowers the cost of a local failure and raises the odds of a regional one.
- **Mechanism**: with open trade a harvest failure in one city is offset by imports; when many are in trouble, ships are lost, ports burn and the network that brought food does not. Ask: what share of food, metal or fuel passes through the network; what share is local; how fast could the society fall back on local supply.
- **Trade-off**: autarky is robust but poor; trade is rich but fragile.
- **Does not hold when**: the network has many alternative paths and the failing node is minor.

## 4.9 Reading the trade record
- **Thesis**: trade goods show contact and rank, not dependence; letters and cargoes are stronger.
- **Mechanism**: a cargo list shows goods in transit on one ship in one year; diplomatic letters show requests for grain, physicians, craftsmen; a foreign comb or ship rivet in a native site shows contact. They do not by themselves show that a society could not survive without the trade. Quote the specific find and what it establishes.
- **Trade-off**: finds are concrete; their interpretation is not.
- **Does not hold when**: large repeated quantities of an essential good (tons of metal) show systemic demand.
