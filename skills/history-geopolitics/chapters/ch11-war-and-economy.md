# Chapter 11: War and Economy: Blockade, War Economy and Punitive Peace

## Core Idea
A long war is an economic contest first and a battle contest second. This chapter uses the First World War as its main case (Allied sea blockade, the Central Powers' war economies, the famine winter of 1916-17, the peace settlement and its debts) and adds shorter cross-checks from other wars. Many of its claims are **contested**: the weight of the blockade, the size of German excess deaths, whether the reparations were payable, whether the settlement caused the next war. Figures are as reported and several are given as ranges. The chapter reports positions and the evidence each rests on.

## 11.1 Blockade: close versus distant
- **Thesis**: a blockade deprives an enemy of imports and exports; its form depends on technology and on the neutral states around the enemy.
- **Mechanism**: a close blockade posts light ships off the enemy's ports; mines, torpedoes and submarines made that too dangerous. The chosen form was a **distant** blockade: close the sea routes into the enemy's seas (a channel mined and patrolled, heavy ships based in the north) and treat the intervening sea as a no-man's land. International declarations had protected neutral shipping unless it carried contraband in three categories: absolute (only military, such as weapons), conditional (feed for horses, usable by an army) and free (cotton, fertiliser: not seizable, yet usable for uniforms and explosives). The blockader widened the lists as the war went on; the enemy answered with a submarine blockade of its own, which brought neutral states, above all one great neutral power, into the war.
- **Trade-off**: distant blockade keeps the fleet safe and is slow, leaky and legally contested.
- **Does not hold when**: the enemy borders many neutrals or holds conquered territory with resources; then the leak is the story (11.2).

## 11.2 Leaks, neutrals and substitutes
- **Thesis**: a blockade's early years are weak because of neutral intermediaries and domestic substitutes.
- **Mechanism**: from 1914 to 1916 the enemy's seaborne trade collapsed but it traded through neighbouring neutrals (exports from one great neutral to one northern neutral rose from about 0.7 million to 13.7 million dollars in a year while exports to the enemy fell by about 80%), used the resources of occupied territory and replaced fertiliser with a chemical process that covered about 70% of agricultural needs by 1915. The blockader pressed neutrals to restrict re-export, temporarily allowed food under the neutral power's pressure, and tightened only in 1916 and, decisively, after the neutral power entered the war in 1917. Pre-war expectations (an official's prediction that grass would grow in the streets of the enemy's ports within six months) were far too optimistic.
- **Trade-off**: pressure on neutrals shortens the war and risks turning a neutral into an enemy.
- **Does not hold when**: the neutrals themselves are blockaded or are weak enough to be coerced.

## 11.3 What the blockade did and did not do (contested)
- **Thesis (position A)**: the blockade starved the enemy into surrender; contemporaries on both sides held this view.
- **Thesis (position B)**: the blockade was one factor among several; most of the food crisis was due to the enemy's own policies and to the decision to fight its main suppliers.
- **Evidence for B**: before the war the enemy imported about 20 to 25% of its food and fodder (about 42% of fats); it went to war with its biggest suppliers (suppliers of about 39% of its wheat, with 20% and 12% from two others) and with its biggest export markets, so it lost revenue to buy food through neutrals; price caps led farmers to hoard or sell on the black market; about 9 million pigs were slaughtered in 1915 to free food and many were kept and sold illegally; mobilisation cut farm labour; planted area fell (wheat by 32%, rye by 23%, potatoes by 31%); a failed potato harvest in 1916 preceded the coldest winter; an official fat-import drop from 5,200 to 1,900 tonnes compares with a need of 2.6 million tonnes and a domestic production decline of 1 million tonnes; the army was fed (a study found no malnutrition among soldiers, and some sent food home); allied countries with open imports had higher civilian death rates than the enemy in one comparison (as reported); a partner empire's hunger was concentrated in one half whose political rival held back food; one study gave the blockade about 50% of the decline in food supply.
- **Evidence for A**: rations fell far below peacetime in the winter of 1916-17 (the daily figure for the main enemy is unclear) and to about 700 calories per person in 1918 in its partner (as reported); excess civilian deaths in the main enemy are estimated at 425,000 to 760,000, with the lower range considered more reliable, about 400,000 in the partner state, up to about 1.5 million in a third empire (excluding a genocide); tuberculosis deaths rose by about 160,000 above expectation; child mortality rose about 50%. The blockade was partly maintained after the armistice until the treaty.
- **Contested**: the blockade's contribution to the **military** outcome (less rubber, fuel, oil) may have mattered more than its food effect; the high command asked for an armistice because of the military situation, not hunger. After the war both sides found the blockade useful: one as a grievance (an unjust peace), the other as a naval success story.
- **Trade-off**: reading the blockade as decisive supports a narrative of betrayal or of sea power; the cases show it is mixed.
- **Does not hold when**: it concerns the later phase (1917-18) when the tightened blockade, the submarine war's failure and the loss of an eastern front acted together.

## 11.4 War economy and the failure of planning
- **Thesis**: a state that planned for a short war found its administration was the bottleneck in a long one.
- **Mechanism**: the Central Powers had done little to prepare for a long war. In the winter of 1916-17 a diet of black bread, turnips, a few potatoes and an egg per week led to the "turnip winter"; the bureaucracy of a **federal** empire could not centralise: one region refused to export to others and another sold its crops abroad rather than to its own partner territory; schools closed for lack of heat; thousands of children and many adults died. In another allied state the rail network was handed to the army and the rest was in disarray, so the capital starved although the country produced enough food; infant mortality doubled in the capital; strikes began in 1916 and by early 1917 only a few days of grain remained. By contrast one western ally with open Atlantic ports gave priority to food imports and never imposed real controls; a naval power with a strong central administration used rationing and increased production and avoided the worst. The decision to resume unlimited submarine warfare came out of this desperation (the aim: starve the opponent before being starved).
- **Trade-off**: central controls ration fairly and raise friction; markets allocate and fail in a siege.
- **Does not hold when**: a state has secure imports and little disruption; then planning matters less.

## 11.5 Financing the war
- **Thesis**: the way a state pays for a war determines its post-war position.
- **Mechanism**: the main enemy paid for the war with about 14% from taxation and the rest from war bonds and borrowing (the federal structure limited taxing power), which grew until debt service reached over 90% of government expenditure in 1918; leaders hoped that victory would pay. Allies relied on banks and empires: one lent over 500 million pounds to two partners, sold overseas assets, pressed private citizens to sell investments and buy government bonds and then borrowed from the great neutral lender (owing it over 4 billion dollars by 1918, imports from it up fourfold from 1914); total national debt rose from about 650 million (1914) to over 7.4 billion (1919), and in the 1920s about 44% of government spending went to interest (as reported). The financial centre shifted from one city to another; in a later war the same lender provided its partner with over 30 billion dollars in aid (as reported) and the debt was repaid only in 2006. In an older case, a vast steppe empire's successor attempted paper money and printed seven times more in one year than in the previous three, which produced inflation (chapter 14).
- **Trade-off**: debt finance spreads the cost and leaves a creditor with leverage.
- **Does not hold when**: the war is short, or victory delivers the expected spoils.

## 11.6 The peace settlement and the problem of reparations (contested)
- **Thesis**: a peace that ignores the economy of the whole region can create the next crisis.
- **Mechanism**: the settlement included territory, demilitarisation, occupation, mandates and an institution for international disputes; one clause assigned responsibility for the damage, added as a **legal basis** for reparations; the amount was left to a later commission, the treaty acknowledged that the defeated state's resources were not adequate for full reparation but required compensation for civilian damage. For the victors, especially the most damaged one, reparations were essential for reconstruction. Allied debts to the great lender had to be repaid in hard currency over 25 years, including arms supplied before its entry. A circle followed: the defeated state, in default, borrowed from the lender, paid reparations to one ally, which repaid another ally, which repaid the lender. The defeated state's undamaged industry was disrupted, so a potential trading partner was out of action.
- **Positions**: (a) A leading critic, an economist who left the negotiations, argued that Europe was one economic unit and that heavy reparations were economically unwise and politically dangerous; he proposed lower reparations, reduced or cancelled allied debts, an international reconstruction loan with currency reform and restored trade with the eastern state. (b) Later historians doubt that the treaty predestined the 1930s, doubt that reparations were too large to pay, and note that voters in the victorious states wanted punishment. (c) A third view: the treaty was a compromise that satisfied nobody. The evidence does not settle the question.
- **Consequence claimed (position)**: inflation, unemployment and loss of savings, street violence and weak centre parties benefited extremists. A causal chain from the settlement to a later regime is a **hypothesis**, not a finding; many other factors (a depression, domestic politics) are involved.
- **Trade-off**: a punitive peace satisfies publics and may block recovery; a lenient one may fail to satisfy victors who owe debts.
- **Does not hold when**: the victors can afford to forgive debts or the loser's recovery is not needed by anyone else.

## 11.7 War economics in other cases
- **Athens and Sparta**: the sea power's grain routes and overseas revenue let it survive annual devastation; only when the grain route was cut did it break; its rival's central heartland was secure and economically conservative; a rich empire funded the rival fleet (chapter 9).
- **Naval race**: German debt doubled between 1900 and 1909 (as reported); the chancellor concluded the state could not afford the biggest army and the second navy; the rival with a stronger credit base raised taxes through a constitutional crisis rather than yield; by 1912 the weaker side shifted to its army.
- **Iran-Iraq**: the aggressor shifted to a war economy (oil money to arms, mobilisation from 200,000 to about 500,000); the other side, sanctioned and unable to buy spare parts, lost air capacity; the war of the cities and the tanker war aimed at the enemy's economy and at drawing in other powers.
- **Trade-off**: each case shows the war was also a contest over who could keep paying.
- **Does not hold when**: a party has an external patron that pays (11.5).

Ask: what share of the enemy's food, fuel and finance comes from outside; who are the neutrals; what substitutes exist; who controls the home economy; how is the war financed; what do the creditors want afterwards. Keep claims about the blockade and the peace labelled as contested (ch01, ch12).
