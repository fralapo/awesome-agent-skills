# Chapter 5: Society, Elites, Legitimacy, War and Technology

## Core Idea
Environment and trade set the stress; institutions decide how it is absorbed. This chapter covers rulers whose authority rests on delivering good outcomes, elites whose incentives drive extraction, succession and religious shifts that change trust, war as symptom and as cause, and technology that shifts the balance between large states and small ones. Almost everything here is **position**, with the cases in chapter 6.

## 5.1 The ruler's promise
- **Thesis (position)**: a sacred ruler's legitimacy is a contract with natural outcomes.
- **Mechanism**: in Maya texts as often quoted, the ruler is asked to bring the sun and food; if he cannot, the people may decide he must go. At Angkor the king was both executive and a god-king through a ritual, so devotion to the state was religious service. When harvests and security fail, the ruler is the visible culprit. Observable: monuments stop, palaces are burned, unfinished carvings are left, former subordinates claim royal titles for themselves.
- **Trade-off**: sacral kingship mobilises labour and obedience cheaply; it ties the ruler's fate to the weather.
- **Does not hold when**: rule rests mainly on force or on a secular bureaucracy; then failure of harvests hits differently.

## 5.2 Leading indicators of legitimacy loss
- **Thesis (position)**: legitimacy leaves traces before the end.
- **Mechanism**: the Maya example shows dated monuments rising from about ten a year to over forty, then falling to nothing within decades. Neighbours begin to erect their own monuments with the capital's royal title. Nobles build separate palaces. Elsewhere, kings change religion repeatedly and ordinary people ask "is the king a god or isn't he?".
- **Trade-off**: such signs are easy to see in hindsight; their meaning at the time is unclear.
- **Does not hold when**: production of monuments stops for other reasons (lack of labour, change of fashion).

## 5.3 Incentives of extraction
- **Thesis (position)**: how surplus is collected shapes how the land is used.
- **Mechanism**: at Angkor each village temple was run by a local family that collected taxes, kept what it needed and sent the rest to the capital; their status depended on how much they sent, so they competed to enlarge taxable land and cut forest for fields. That produced rapid growth and also over-exploitation, resentment and deforestation. In the Maya cities, nobles other than the king began building their own palaces during the boom, adding to the cost of the elite class.
- **Trade-off**: competitive extraction drives expansion while land is available.
- **Does not hold when**: land is limited and extraction is capped by custom or law.

## 5.4 Labour mobilisation and inequality
- **Thesis**: monumental building is paid in peasant time.
- **Mechanism**: figures (estimates): a great reservoir needed the work of about 200,000 peasants for three years; a medium temple's inscription lists around 13,000 staff, including 600 dancers; the palace needed thousands of palace women. Peasants paid taxes to temples, did field work and could be conscripted to build. The cost is invisible in the stones; in the ground it appears as rising malnutrition, especially of commoners, and as strikes (a first recorded workers' strike occurred when tomb builders went unpaid).
- **Trade-off**: shared monuments give identity; the cost can break the contract.
- **Does not hold when**: building is a form of famine relief and keeps workers fed.

## 5.5 Succession and civil war
- **Thesis**: unclear or contested succession spends the state's strength.
- **Mechanism**: Angkor had 27 rulers, of whom only 16 had a legitimate claim, by one count; civil wars were constant. One king had his genealogy rewritten after being passed over by his father; a prince refused to fight his brother and went into exile, during which the rival and the neighbours ruined the state. Each civil war lowers revenue, destroys labour and invites foreign intervention.
- **Trade-off**: strong monarchs reduce disputes but concentrate risk in one person.
- **Does not hold when**: the succession rules are clear and accepted.

## 5.6 Religion changes and public trust
- **Thesis (position)**: when the state religion changes repeatedly, the ideological base of power weakens.
- **Mechanism**: elite Hinduism and a god-king gave way to a Mahayana Buddhist king who kept royal religious authority, then to alternating state religions, and then to a strict, egalitarian Theravada Buddhism spread by monks living in poverty among villagers; within about 70 years of that king's death the royal claim to divinity had faded. A system in which temples were the tax offices lost its revenue base when people stopped believing in the temples.
- **Trade-off**: an inclusive religion can be a source of stability for the people while undermining the old elite.
- **Does not hold when**: the new religion is adopted by elites too and they keep the tax machinery.

## 5.7 Fragmentation as resilience and as weakness
- **Thesis (position)**: many small states fight over scarce resources, but one failure does not take down all.
- **Mechanism**: Maya city-states resembled rival Greek or Italian cities: shared culture, constant contest; there was no unifying government. Some scholars propose that a unified state might have averted some of the crises; that is untestable. When a larger polity fragmented into small kingdoms under a drought, the only way for some to survive was to take from neighbours. Contrast: a unified empire of the Khmer had stored authority, but also a single capital that was vulnerable to a surprise attack on the lake.
- **Trade-off**: pluralism spreads risk; it blocks region-wide coordination.
- **Does not hold when**: environmental stress is regional and affects all units alike.

## 5.8 War: ritual, total, opportunistic
- **Thesis**: distinguish the kind of war before using it as a cause.
- **Mechanism**: Maya "axe wars" were small raids to take captives and kill a noble; occasional "star wars" were rarer wars of total destruction and led to the sack of capitals and the removal of kings. A king's low-level raid on a former ally started a total war. In the Late Bronze Age, neighbours invaded weakened states while the main defenders were committed elsewhere; at Angkor, a king's failed invasions of a coastal rival drained the treasury, while a maritime rival eventually sailed up the great lake and burnt the capital. Late Maya fortifications built with temple stones and spear points in the streets show war as a late symptom.
- **Trade-off**: war can be the trigger, the symptom, or the opportunity others take.
- **Does not hold when**: the war is attested but archaeology shows no destruction.

## 5.9 Technology and the balance of power
- **Thesis (position, contested)**: cheap weapons can empower small states and mobs against states built around costly ones.
- **Mechanism**: the Late Bronze Age powers depended on scarce bronze and on chariots; iron, hard to work but cheap and common, was spreading, and some argue it helped smaller polities and groups assemble large infantry forces, as cheap rifles destabilise modern regions (an analogy, not evidence). The counter: in the wider literature iron comes into general use after the collapse and may be a consequence of broken bronze supply rather than a cause. Evidence to look for: dated iron weapons before the destruction layers, battle tactics in reliefs, infantry versus chariot depictions.
- **Trade-off**: a monopoly on advanced weapons stabilises a state until the monopoly fails.
- **Does not hold when**: iron weapons appear only after the destruction.

## 5.10 Internal revolt as an invisible cause
- **Thesis**: some burnt cities may have been burnt by their own people.
- **Mechanism**: with famine and an angered sky, a population that can arm itself might rise; this is speculation. Archaeology can distinguish only a little (no foreign weapons, targeted burning of palaces, graves). This is a case where the honest statement is "possible, undemonstrated".
- **Trade-off**: including revolt widens the explanation; it can't be shown.
- **Does not hold when**: texts attest an attack by named foreigners.

## 5.11 Records, memory and skills
- **Thesis**: the loss of institutions that keep records and skills makes decline irreversible.
- **Mechanism**: Maya commoners moved carved stones upside down because reading was lost; after the Spanish suppressed the written tradition, later texts survive but their references are no longer understood. The Angkor stone-building tradition ends, and perishable books vanish. Without writers, craftsmen or engineers, even a revived population cannot rebuild the old systems.
- **Trade-off**: oral traditions carry stories; they do not carry engineering.
- **Does not hold when**: skills move with people to a new site.
