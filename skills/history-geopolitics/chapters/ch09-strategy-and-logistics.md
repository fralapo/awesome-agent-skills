# Chapter 9: Strategy and Logistics: How Wars and Empires Are Won and Lost

## Core Idea
Campaigns are decided less by the best battle than by what an army can feed, move, replace and keep supplied, and by what the conquered place is worth. In this model most strategic failures in the cases are failures of **radius** (how far supply reaches), **return** (what holding the ground yields), **coordination** (who commands) or **premise** (a plan that assumed the other side would stop resisting). Nearly all of the chapter is **position** built on case studies; figures are estimates as reported and are marked. Several cases (a naval war in the western Pacific, a siege on a great river, a pre-modern eastern campaign) are covered only in part; the chapter says where.

## 9.1 Logistics sets the radius of power
- **Thesis**: an army can act only inside the distance its supply can cover; beyond it, victories do not translate into control.
- **Mechanism**: a mid-sized Roman field army of about 27,500 men with horses and pack animals needed on the order of 56 tonnes of food and fodder per day (estimate); soldiers carried roughly 17 days of rations, so the operating range before regular resupply was about 24 days. Deeper reach required roads, forts, river transport and garrisons: a standing cost, not a one-off. A steppe army avoided part of this by grazing small horses on grass, keeping several remounts per rider and splitting into a fast column and a slow baggage body; but outside the grasslands it had to forage off the land and, for large multi-ethnic armies, rely on pre-stocked depots, cleared pastures, bridges and relay posts organised months ahead. A modern attacker fell short of the same limit differently: the rail gauge of the country had to be converted, too few railway troops served two competing missions (move the army now versus build high-capacity lines and supply dumps), no rolling stock was captured, and fuel deliveries fell as the distance grew.
- **Trade-off**: more logistics gives reach and costs money, labour and time; less gives speed and a fragile tail.
- **Does not hold when**: the terrain offers cheap transport (a navigable river, a coast under naval control) or the army lives off a rich countryside during harvest; then radius is set by something else (9.2, 9.4).

## 9.2 Control of the water lane denies the invader
- **Thesis**: whoever controls the sea or river corridor can starve an invading land army that won on land.
- **Mechanism**: in a six-year invasion of a peninsula, the invader won open-field battles quickly, but the defender's navy (cannon-armed ships, armoured ships, lures into narrow waters) kept the invader out of the sea lane on its flank, so reinforcements and supplies could not arrive by water; partisan militias ambushed supply barges on the rivers; winter killed or disabled most of the army (about 150,000 reduced to about 53,000 in one phase, as reported). In summer the invader foraged and stabilised; in winter it starved. Another example: a great sea power could survive annual devastation of its countryside because grain came from overseas, and it failed only when a rival fleet, funded by a rich empire, cut that route twice. The same logic gives a blockade (chapter 11).
- **Trade-off**: naval defence is costly to build and is decisive only if the opponent's plan depends on sea supply.
- **Does not hold when**: the invader can supply overland, or feeds its army from the conquered population; then sea control is secondary.

## 9.3 A frontier is a zone set by logistics and tolerance
- **Thesis**: imperial borders in the cases stop where the cost of reliable supply and administration meets the limit of local tolerance, not where ambition ends.
- **Mechanism**: in one reading of Rome and its eastern rival, the frontier was a zone, not a line: an **inner** frontier where an army and administration could be supplied within what locals tolerated, and an **outer** one where influence reached but a permanent presence would not be accepted. Cheap sea and river transport, together with client rulers, hostage-taking, interference in succession and marriage ties, extended control without garrisons; communication lag made client ties impermanent. A deep campaign that took the enemy capital failed to end the enemy state because the state was decentralised and could fall back to other seats, there had been no decisive field defeat to destroy its legitimacy, and beyond the river plains lay mountain passes the supply system could not cross. A later reversal came when the weaker side had a civil war and centralised under a new dynasty that built a logistic backbone and counter-attacked deep (a sack of a major city, then a campaign reaching two core regions of the rival, with only tenuous control).
- **Trade-off**: indirect control is cheap and slow; direct control is fast and fragile.
- **Does not hold when**: the target is a centralised state with one core that can be taken and a population that will switch loyalty (9.7).
- **(position)**: an older view reads the frontier as the failure of an ideology; the equilibrium view in this view is more recent. Both are readings of the same record.

## 9.4 Marginal return on holding ground
- **Thesis**: expansion stops when the next province yields less than it costs to hold.
- **Mechanism**: a northern territory offered little taxable wealth, no strongholds to seize, scattered autonomous tribes with no centre of gravity, and a rich frontier lay elsewhere; Rome's model of control (entice or coerce local chiefs by threatening their land and treasure) found no lever. Roman sources described endless forest, while modern estimates put the density of settlement higher than they say; the perceived value, not the real one, drove policy (general knowledge: perception can be wrong in either direction). After the loss of three legions the aim moved to containment: mobile defence, alliances, trade, support for tribal disputes. Punitive campaigns bought little and may have **militarised** the opponent. About 500,000 troops held roughly 7,500 km of border (about one soldier per 20 m, as estimated), and over a third of the army was already tied to that one sector.
- **Trade-off**: containment lowers cost and leaves a permanent opponent who later enters the empire as recruits, then as whole allied peoples.
- **Does not hold when**: the target holds a lever (a port, a grain region, a treasury) or a rival would take it otherwise (a defensive war of necessity).

## 9.5 Overextension and the second front
- **Thesis**: a power loses when it commits resources faster than it can replace them and then meets a second opponent.
- **Mechanism**: a sea power expanded its sphere, sent a large expedition overseas that lost nearly everyone, and met a rival backed by a rich empire that funded a fleet and opened a front on its coast. The proposed factors in that case are layered: alliance structure, economy, a foreign patron, internal politics, public opinion; none alone decides, and the war was not predetermined. A land power broke apart when its offensive wasted manpower, replaced no losses and met a new front; one estimate puts the German replacement problem in 1918 at about 200,000 soldiers needed per month against about 7,000 returning wounded plus about 25,000 new recruits (as reported).
- **Trade-off**: aggression can win quickly; the same speed hides the bill.
- **Does not hold when**: the second front is not independent (the same patron funds both) or the first expedition was small relative to resources.

## 9.6 Choose the nodes, isolate the rest
- **Thesis**: a stronger industrial power does not need to take everything; it takes the bases that matter and cuts off the others.
- **Mechanism**: in a central-ocean campaign the attacker seized selected atolls for airfields and anchorages, neutralised other garrisons by air and sea and bypassed them, leaving them unable to receive supplies and slowly losing strength. The first assaults were very costly (about 5,100 dead or missing against about 21,000 enemy dead in one phase, as reported) and the lessons (reconnaissance of beaches, preparatory bombardment) were applied at the next one. The attacker's production gap supported the method: by the end of 1943 it had commissioned many more large carriers, escort carriers and destroyers than its opponent (figures as reported, not rechecked). The defender fell back to a new defence line and fortified it.
- **Trade-off**: the method trades time and a few hard battles for fewer overall losses; it needs sea and air control.
- **Does not hold when**: the enemy's garrisons can still sortie or reinforce, or the bypassed positions hold something the attacker needs.

## 9.7 Centre of gravity: capture a capital or bypass a decentralised state
- **Thesis**: taking the capital ends a war only if the state is centralised and its legitimacy rests on the capital.
- **Mechanism**: Rome met opponents with many dispersed strongholds (a fortified chief's town can be seized, and the region's loyalty follows) and opponents with no strongholds at all; in the second case it had to build its own. A federation of tribes or a state with several seats can retreat and survive. A conquest of a single central capital by a river valley route succeeded against states with one core and failed against states that were not.
- **Trade-off**: knowing the centre lowers cost; guessing wrong costs a campaign.
- **Does not hold when**: legitimacy has moved to a religion or an institution rather than a place.

## 9.8 Unity of command and political interference
- **Thesis**: divided command costs more than a smaller force.
- **Mechanism**: in one defensive campaign, a theatre commander had nominal authority but not explicit command of the independent area commanders; the head of state bypassed him and moved units without telling him; a subordinate obeyed the order "hold at all costs" over the theatre commander's order to leave and was besieged; the relief army promised did not exist in the strength claimed (about 32,000 raw conscripts for a task that needed more). The commander's judgement was sound: a fortress can be retaken, a trapped army is destroyed. On the attacker's side political generals plotted for independent commands, and a senior general quietly redirected the units they raised until unity was restored. In another war, three forces (regular army, revolutionary guard, volunteer militia) each had their own line to the supreme leader and competed; an opponent said it could discern no pattern in their moves. Finally, a head of state reversed his own order within days, a "tacit admission" that the force could not do both tasks.
- **Trade-off**: central control reduces coordination loss and risks one bad decision; delegation allows speed and loses coherence.
- **Does not hold when**: the units are on different continents or the political aim itself is set by the head of state; then the issue is political, not tactical.

## 9.9 Mass, attrition and who can replace
- **Thesis**: when neither side can win a battle, the side that replaces losses and keeps trade open wins the war.
- **Mechanism**: in a long ground war between two states, one side's mass assaults on prepared defences (minefields, flooded zones, defence in depth, chemical weapons, helicopters) produced local gains and heavy losses; its manpower pool was large but finite; an international sanctions regime meant it could not buy spare parts and conserved its aircraft, while its opponent had foreign arms and finance and gained the advantage in the air, the cities and the tanker war. In 1941, a defender lost most of its armoured force in the opening weeks (about 2,300 tanks in four mechanised corps reduced to about 95 in one sector in three weeks, as reported), moved much of its industry east (dismantled, transported, many plants arriving late or incomplete) and fought the year with weapons already built; how much the moved plants mattered to 1942 is unclear.
- **Trade-off**: sustained attrition favours the richer side and is brutal for everyone.
- **Does not hold when**: a breakthrough creates a collapse faster than either side's replacement rate.

## 9.10 Tactical success can hide strategic erosion
- **Thesis**: a series of local wins can conceal falling stocks and rising dependence.
- **Mechanism**: a mechanised attacker's textbook encirclements succeeded on open ground and failed in forest and marsh, where slow tanks could not envelop and were drawn into traps; the early victories deceived leaders about shrinking tank and truck numbers. A later offensive broke through but cost the best units and gained no decisive objective; a plan that "sets the next objective after the previous one" has no end state.
- **Trade-off**: continued attack keeps the initiative; stopping admits limits.
- **Does not hold when**: the attacker has real reserves and a defined end state.

## 9.11 The premise that conquest pays for conquest
- **Thesis**: a plan in which each conquered region supplies men and material for the next is fragile.
- **Mechanism**: an invader planned for conquered lands to provide forces for the next push; when the first land was not subdued and stayed hostile, the chain broke. A nomadic empire did make conquest pay by moving craftsmen, using local depots and imposing tribute, but only after building an administration to collect and move it.
- **Trade-off**: a self-financing empire grows fast and is only as stable as its administration.
- **Does not hold when**: the conquered region truly yields surplus quickly (a rich, centralised neighbour).
