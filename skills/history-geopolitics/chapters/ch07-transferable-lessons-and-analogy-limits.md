# Chapter 7: Transferable Lessons and the Limits of Historical Analogy

## Core Idea
Past collapses give mechanisms worth testing against modern systems; they do not give forecasts. In this model a lesson is the mechanism plus the evidence that it operated, plus the conditions under which it would not. Every item below is **position**. The second half of the chapter lists the standard ways analogies mislead and a rule for using them safely.

## 7.1 Design for the extreme year, not the average
- **Thesis (position)**: survival depends on the worst variation the system must meet.
- **Mechanism**: Maya reservoirs and Khmer canals handled normal droughts; a decades-long swing exceeded the design range (2.5, 3.1). Ask: what is the worst plausible year, and what fails first?
- **Trade-off**: margin is costly in a normal year.
- **Does not hold when**: the extreme is not plausible or the cost of margin exceeds the loss avoided.

## 7.2 Maintenance is a permanent claim on the surplus
- **Thesis (position)**: infrastructure creates an obligation; neglect compounds.
- **Mechanism**: Angkor's water works needed constant desilting and repair, and a minimum population (2.4, 3.5). A once-productive asset becomes a liability when the workforce or revenue declines.
- **Trade-off**: spending on upkeep competes with visible new building (monuments stopped; upkeep failed).
- **Does not hold when**: the asset can be simplified or abandoned in stages without harm.

## 7.3 Slack buys time; efficiency removes it
- **Thesis (position)**: spare capacity is the price of surviving an unplanned event.
- **Mechanism**: a thin farm surplus meant any drop reached the elite quickly (2.2); a long chain for essential inputs had no buffer (4.2). Stores, secondary food sources and alternative suppliers lengthen the time to react.
- **Trade-off**: slack looks like waste.
- **Does not hold when**: the shock is so large that slack is exhausted anyway; then diversification matters more.

## 7.4 Map the lifeline and its hub
- **Thesis (position)**: list what you cannot make, and who supplies it.
- **Mechanism**: the northern colony's supply of iron, timber, grain and clergy ran through one port, which fell (4.4). The Bronze Age states depended on metal through long chains (4.1).
- **Trade-off**: duplicate suppliers cost more.
- **Does not hold when**: a hub is itself very resilient and the dependency is minor.

## 7.5 Alliances insure against independent risks only
- **Thesis (position)**: test the assumption of independence.
- **Mechanism**: allies cannot help each other if all are hit by the same drought or the same raiders (4.5). Ask: in the scenario that hurts me, who else is hurt, and can they still send help?
- **Trade-off**: alliances cost autonomy.
- **Does not hold when**: the allies are in different climatic and trade zones.

## 7.6 Legitimacy is a buffer that can be spent
- **Thesis (position)**: authority that rests on delivering outcomes loses protection when it stops delivering.
- **Mechanism**: monument rates, royal titles and religious loyalty are indicators (5.1, 5.2, 5.6). Frequent changes in the official ideology reduce trust.
- **Trade-off**: promising outcomes secures obedience in good years.
- **Does not hold when**: authority rests on other bases (law, procedure, voluntary membership).

## 7.7 Adapt early; separate identity from function
- **Thesis (position)**: copying a neighbour's survival technique is cheap before a crisis, expensive after.
- **Mechanism**: the northern colonists did change their diet and farming effort, but kept visible markers of identity (clothing); the evidence does not show a simple refusal (3.9). Ask what is essential to the group's identity and what is just habit.
- **Trade-off**: adaptation can erode the thing worth preserving.
- **Does not hold when**: the technique requires materials or skills not available locally.

## 7.8 Retreat to a core can be a winning move
- **Thesis (position)**: contraction is sometimes the most successful response.
- **Mechanism**: one large state withdrew from outlying territories to a defensible heartland and survived (2.7); a court left a vulnerable city for a safer region (6.2). Holding every holding is costly.
- **Trade-off**: contraction loses revenue and prestige.
- **Does not hold when**: the core depends on the periphery for essentials.

## 7.9 Leading indicators
- **Thesis (position)**: look for what changes before the visible end.
- **Mechanism**: skeletal health, land-use intensity, refuse composition, tool wear, repair of walls, hoards, monument rate, and whether neighbours claim your titles all move earlier than a capital's fall. A change in several is stronger than any one.
- **Trade-off**: indicators are noisy; false alarms occur.
- **Does not hold when**: the end is abrupt and unforeseeable.

## 7.10 The limits of historical analogy
- **Thesis**: an analogy is an argument about mechanism, never evidence about the future.
- **Mechanism (common failure modes)**:

| Failure | Example | Safer move |
|---|---|---|
| Scale mismatch | Comparing a few palace states to the global economy | Compare a mechanism (chokepoint), not size |
| Survivorship | Studying only famous collapses, not failures that were avoided | Include survivors and recoveries |
| Hindsight | Reading late signs as obvious at the time | Ask what a contemporary could know |
| Moral story | Collapse as punishment for greed or arrogance | Separate evidence from the moral |
| Single cause | One villain (climate, invaders, a leader) | Use roles: underlying, amplifier, trigger |
| Cherry-picked numbers | "One in a thousand people lived here" | Give ranges and the basis |
| Technology blind spot | Assuming modern tools change nothing | Name what is different (information, energy, finance, medicine) |
| Equating collapse with doom | Most are simplifications | Say what continued |
| Shared vocabulary | "Oil of the Bronze Age" | Point out where the analogy ends |

- **Trade-off**: analogies make abstract risks vivid; they carry false confidence.
- **Does not hold when**: the modern system is structurally similar in the specific mechanism (for example a cascade in coupled infrastructure), and you can test the mechanism directly.

## 7.11 A rule for using a past case on a present system
- **Thesis (position)**: translate the mechanism, test it, and state the disanalogies.
- **Mechanism**: (1) name the mechanism and the case evidence; (2) name the variables in the present system that play the same roles; (3) list at least three differences that matter; (4) give a test that could show the mechanism is absent today; (5) state what the history cannot tell you (probabilities, timing).
- **Trade-off**: the answer is more qualified.
- **Does not hold when**: the user only wants a metaphor; then label it as one.
