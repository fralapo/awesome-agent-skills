# Chapter 9: Cultural Evolution and Technological History as Analogies

## Core Idea
Historical cases give analogies for how technologies spread, how institutions respond and how shocks hit societies. An analogy is a position: it says "this case resembles that one in these respects". Each entry below lists what it would imply for AI, why it might not, and what would show the analogy wrong. History and culture are treated as *models*, not as verdicts. Everything here is a position; claims marked "general knowledge" go beyond it.

## 9.1 The collective brain (position)
- **Thesis**: human success owes more to social learning across large, connected populations than to individual intelligence.
- **Mechanism**: innovation is a product of population size and connectedness. Eurasia produced most first inventions of big technologies; it is the largest landmass, with the largest populations and an east-west axis along which ideas and people move. Institutions that interconnect populations preserve complex technology; technologies are gained and lost when connections or populations shrink (a projectile weapon appearing and disappearing in regions). A larger-brained relative species did not have this advantage, which implies that individual brain size is a weak predictor against group-level cultural accumulation.
- **For AI**: capability may depend on connectedness (tools, data, other models, people) more than on isolated model size; and a system's lead depends on how many minds can build on its output.
- **Against**: AI may not need cultural accumulation if training distils it directly; and the analogy sets humans, not machine collectives, as the learners.
- **Falsified by**: a single isolated system that out-innovates large connected communities across several fields.
- **Does not hold when**: the technology is one that does not need a wide base of complementary knowledge.

## 9.2 Why some technologies are absent (position)
- **Mechanism**: absence of a technology (a wheeled vehicle in one hemisphere) is better explained by lack of connected populations and demand than by lack of animals or intelligence; the wheel is known as toys, and dogs or llamas could have pulled carts.
- **Use**: when a technology is "missing" in a place, test the collective-brain explanation before an intelligence or resource explanation.
- **Falsified by**: finding the technology absent in large, highly connected populations with ample resources.

## 9.3 Psychology and institutions co-evolve (position)
- **Position**: a package of psychological traits (individualism, analytic thinking, trust in strangers, willingness to cooperate impersonally, low conformity) varies globally and can be traced to institutional history. One account: a church dismantled intensive kinship (it banned polygyny and cousin marriage out to distant cousins, and allowed inheritance by testament), producing small nuclear families, voluntary associations (guilds), urbanisation, charter towns, universities, contract law, and more idea flow, leading to industrial innovation; moral universalism then supported later movements such as the abolition of the slave trade.
- **For**: a causal chain from institutions to psychology to economy.
- **Against**: heavy contingency and many alternative explanations; correlation across regions; the account is controversial in the literature (general knowledge).
- **Falsified by**: regions with the same institutional change lacking the psychological shift, or with the shift arising earlier.
- **Use for AI**: institutions shape which minds are common; AI systems embedded in a society will inherit and reshape its institutions, not only its data.

## 9.4 Measuring selection needs many samples over time (mechanism)
- **Mechanism**: one genome carries many ancestors, but detecting selection on a particular variant requires large sample sizes across time to see its frequency change. Only in recent years have ancient-DNA sample sizes reached that scale. The method uses the "experiment of nature" of changing environments (farming, domestication, altitude) to see what is biologically significant.
- **Use**: evidence about long-term trends needs many time-stamped samples, not a few vivid cases. Apply to AI forecasts built from one or two benchmarks.

## 9.5 Shocks, not only trends, end empires (position)
- **Position**: disease and climate shocks are capable of causing mortality orders of magnitude above ordinary years. Baseline mortality in the ancient empire was perhaps 3-4% per year; a plague could kill 50-60% in affected regions, as in the later medieval pandemic. Population fell in the empire's capital from the largest city in the world to perhaps one tenth or one twentieth of its former size over several centuries. The evidence is pieced together from clues; no modern statistics exist.
- **Counterfactual (position)**: an empire hit by earlier pandemics survived; a similar shock might have led to a restructured empire with continuity in the core, as in the dynastic cycles of another large civilisation.
- **For AI**: tail events and slow environmental constraints (energy, climate, disease) can reshape outcomes more than the main technology trend.
- **Against**: external shocks are exogenous; AI risks are largely endogenous.
- **Falsified by**: reconstruction evidence that mortality was lower or that the polity's decline began before the shocks.
- **Does not hold when**: the question is about incremental technology adoption rather than collapse.

## 9.6 Institutional design and unpredictable succession (position)
- **Position**: in one historical case a powerful office changed hands through election every decade or so, often by a coalition of the previous holder's enemies, and each holder had discretionary power to replace governments. The result was chronic instability in nearby city-states: no tradition, no legitimacy, nothing to stop turnover. The political advice of the time was written for that specific context (a strong, stable leader with heirs as the remedy), not as universal cynicism.
- **For AI**: concentrated discretionary power with unpredictable succession is an unstable configuration; governance of powerful systems should consider succession, legitimacy and predictability.
- **Against**: a loose analogy; the instability came from the specific mix of arbitrary overthrow and inheritance rules.
- **Falsified by**: stable cases with the same structure.

## 9.7 A weapon built under wartime acceleration (position)
- **Position**: the technology was likely inevitable once the underlying physics was found, but wartime fear that an adversary was ahead accelerated the program greatly; secrecy was used in recruitment; practitioners felt the field had been "spiritual" before the war and wrestled with the implications; one view held that the weapon was dual: very destructive, and possibly ending large-scale war because it changed what states can defend with war. Deaths from war rose almost exponentially from the eighteenth century to the mid-1940s, then fell to a much lower plateau (the numbers cited: about 15 million in 1943 and 1-2 million per year afterwards; unverified and disputed; general knowledge).
- **For AI**: a race dynamic can accelerate a capability beyond what social and institutional adaptation can absorb; dual-use framing can support either restraint or continued development.
- **Against**: the physics of that case yielded a single, visible, state-controlled capability; AI is diffuse, commercial and dual-use in many small ways.
- **Falsified by**: finding that a diffuse, commercial technology was restrained by mechanisms analogous to the state-based control of fissile material (or the opposite).

## 9.8 How to use a historical analogy
1. List the *relations* the analogy needs (race, diffusion, secrecy, scarce physical inputs, who controls it).
2. List where AI lacks or reverses one.
3. State what observation would break the analogy.
4. Prefer several analogies that disagree over one that comforts.

## Checklist for this chapter
1. Does the analogy fit on mechanism or only on story?
2. Is the evidence many time-stamped samples or a few cases?
3. Are shocks (tail events) considered besides trends?
4. Which institutions would adapt faster than the technology, and which slower?
