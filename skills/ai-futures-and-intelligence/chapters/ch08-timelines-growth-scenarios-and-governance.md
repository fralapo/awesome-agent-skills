# Chapter 8: Timelines, Growth Scenarios and Governance

## Core Idea
Forecasts about advanced AI are bets on a few structural claims: how much compute would be enough, whether research can be automated, whether growth feedback loops reopen, and whether anything physical or institutional gets in the way. This chapter lays each out as a position with the arguments for, against and falsifiers, and treats the dates as dated. **All predictions are "(position)", written in the 2020-2026 period**; nothing here is a calibrated forecast.

## 8.1 Compute-based timelines (position)
- **Position**: estimate the compute needed by reference to biology: the brain at the low end, a brute-force replay of evolution at the high end, with a very wide range of orders of magnitude in between (the example used a roughly uniform prior across 10^25, 10^30, 10^35 operations in the 2000s). Update on the absence of clear signs of general intelligence, and on how fast available compute runs through the remaining orders of magnitude.
- **Mechanism**: with a uniform prior over orders of magnitude, about half the weight sits in the first half of the remaining range; if compute grows through that range within about a decade, that implies a large probability within the decade, conditional on AI being possible at all.
- **For**: forces explicit assumptions.
- **Against**: the prior is arbitrary; compute may not be the binding input (data, algorithms, reliability); brain-based estimates have large conversion uncertainty.
- **Falsified by**: the compute range being crossed without capabilities moving toward the target.

## 8.2 The growth-feedback argument (position)
- **Position**: economic growth historically accelerated through a loop of people, ideas and resources; about two centuries ago the loop broke because richer societies stopped having more children. If AI can do what humans do to advance science and technology, the loop closes again (more compute means more "workers" with ideas), which could yield explosive growth: centuries of progress compressed into a much shorter period. One forecast gave more than even odds (position, dated) that this century sees AI able to do all key tasks that advance science and technology.
- **For**: uses standard growth theory plus a plain extrapolation; if AI replaces idea-generation, the formal conclusion follows.
- **Against**: extrapolating accelerating growth to infinity is unreliable; complementary inputs (energy, physical experiments, institutions, scarce materials) can bind; automation of "all key tasks" is the contested premise; past forecasters have also been wrong in the pessimistic direction (resources, food).
- **Falsified by**: sustained task automation with no acceleration of the growth rate, or growth that tracks a physical input.
- **Does not hold when**: demand, not supply, limits growth; or when regulation and licensing decide adoption.

## 8.3 Physical doubling: robots, then biology (position)
- **Mechanism**: if AI can direct humans and existing robot makers (including a converted automotive industry), robot output could exceed that of human armed forces and factories within about a year; then, with falling costs from removing labour and scale, a robot-industry doubling time of months or less. A further, more speculative step considers biology (faster self-reproduction) or atomically precise manufacturing as rate benchmarks; biology is treated as relevant data, not a forecast.
- **For**: a stepwise cost argument.
- **Against**: assumes smooth scaling of supply chains, energy, minerals and regulation; physical deployment has historically been slower than software.
- **Falsified by**: observed deployment doubling times far longer than a year despite capable AI direction.

## 8.4 Intelligence explosion versus bottlenecks (position)
- **For**: replacing researchers with compute-limited AI workers shortens each successive doubling; software and hardware gains compound (see ch07 7.6).
- **Against**: output of a project is limited by its slowest component; some contributions (taste, direction, experiments) may remain human; frontier researchers report AI helps least on their core expert job.
- **Middle position**: partial automation can still give large gains because the automated parts (peripheral coding, literature survey, bespoke curricula) can be done at enormous scale and free experts for bottleneck work; the gain increases as the share automated grows.
- **Falsified by**: automation coverage rising for years with research throughput flat.

## 8.5 A rival, slower-timeline view (general knowledge)
- **Position (general knowledge)**: capabilities that appear soon may need much longer to become reliable in the economy: missing capacities (continual learning, long-horizon reliability, robust world models), physical constraints (power, fabs, data) and diffusion lags push broad automation out by decades. This view and 8.2 differ on whether the missing capacities appear soon and whether complementary inputs bind.
- **Use**: when a timeline is quoted, ask which missing capability it assumes and which constraint it assumes away. Do not attach specific figures to this view without checking them.

## 8.6 Choosing what to worry about under radical uncertainty (position)
- **Position**: predicting the long-run future is hard, so be picky: pay attention to issues that are big enough, likely enough to be soon, and where actions have predictable effects. AI qualifies; some risk areas do not. Misaligned AI is judged a real threat given how systems are designed today; stagnation and "move too fast" risks cut in opposite directions, and one cannot tell which dominates.
- **For**: avoids false precision about the million-year future.
- **Against**: a threshold of "predictable effects" can be used to dismiss anything inconvenient; neglect of low-probability, high-impact issues.
- **Falsified by**: later evidence that interventions chosen under this rule had no effect or backfired.

## 8.7 Social returns to innovation: do not quote a precise share
- **Thesis**: it is often said that innovators capture only about 2% of the gains they create. The estimate has weak foundations; plausible range reported (dated, approximate): 1% to 6%, central guess 2-5%, and it differs between marginal founders (probably small social returns) and real innovators (large).
- **Use**: do not use the figure as a premise for a policy conclusion; treat as "innovators capture a minority share".

## 8.8 Governance: who decides, and what gets locked in (position)
- **Selection pressure**: if AIs and AI-run firms evolve preferences through competition, those preferences shape the economy, as evolved human drives did; politics and regulation may select for AI-run institutions that outcompete others.
- **Lock-in and values**: powerful systems with arbitrary accidental goals would be a bad outcome even without hostility; "unfamiliar future" is not a reason for comfort.
- **Institutional frictions** (licensing, liability, human-only roles) can delay adoption but are mutable.
- **For**: ties AI governance to long-run selection and institutional theory.
- **Against**: assumes competition dominates coordination; states can and do restrict technologies.
- **Falsified by**: a durable regime in which competing developers agree on and enforce limits.

## 8.9 Reading a timeline claim: five questions
1. Which input is assumed to be the bottleneck (compute, data, algorithms, reliability, energy)?
2. Which analogy or prior does the date rest on?
3. Is the quantity forecast capability, deployment, or economic effect?
4. What observation in the next 2 years would change the number?
5. Who gains if the claim is believed?
