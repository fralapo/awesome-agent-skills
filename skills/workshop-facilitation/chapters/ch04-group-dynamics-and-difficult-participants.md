# Chapter 04: Group Dynamics, the Decider and Difficult Participants

## Core Idea
Disruption usually comes from an unmet need (control, recognition, space for expertise, clarity, or being heard). Prevent it with ground rules and structure, handle it with a visible parking lot and small tasks, and escalate privately and only last. Decisions stay clean when one named **decider** makes the final call and everyone else's input is gathered silently and treated as information.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The decider**: one accountable person (product or project owner, or whoever owns the decision) chosen before the workshop. They attend the sessions where decisions are made, vote last or not at all, and may ignore the tally. Named up front, they break ties and manage resources. A strategy or goal workshop with an absent decider produces work nobody can approve.
- **Votes inform, the decider decides**: the vote shows where the wind blows. Two conventions appear:
  - decider does not vote, chooses afterwards, free to ignore the result (goal and question rounds);
  - decider gets extra or special votes (for example four dots against two for others; one extra dot to rescue a question that must be addressed even if less voted; a super vote at the end).
  Pick one, announce it before voting, and use it consistently.
- **Parking lot**: (1) introduce it at the start and say what it is for; (2) when a challenge threatens to derail, acknowledge it ("so the software is outdated"); (3) write it on the visible list, with the person's name; (4) say when and how you will address it (end-of-day slot, a lunch one-to-one); (5) **keep the promise**, even if nobody raises it again. A flat "not relevant" makes people angrier. Two goals: people feel heard, and they can let the thought go. Ask disruptors to write their own topic on the sheet.
- **Four participant types (a practical heuristic, not validated psychology)**: *alpha*, wants control and validation, give visible roles and recognition; *beta*, knowledge-driven, give space to share expertise; *gamma*, process-oriented, give clear tasks and direction and avoid putting them on the spot; *omega*, resistance-oriented, ask for their input and make room for new ideas.
- **Six-step escalation for disruptors**: (1) ground rules and explicit buy-in up front, including that people will feel overwhelmed; (2) listen actively, disruption often means "I feel unheard"; (3) parking lot; (4) probing questions ("what is behind that question?") which validate the asker and buy thinking time; (5) easy tasks (placing notes, handing out dots) to turn them into allies; (6) last resort: talk privately, take notes, state the impact, and only then ask them to leave.

## Step-by-Step: A Derailing Moment
1. Acknowledge in one sentence what you heard.
2. Ask whether it belongs to the current goal. If not, park it with name and return time.
3. If the same person continues, ask a probing question, then give a small task.
4. If it continues, speak privately at the next break.
5. Revisit every parked item before closing; record the outcome in the output document (ch10).

## Group Size and Large Groups
- Small group (up to ~10): one room, one board, silent-then-share.
- 30 or more: do not run one open discussion and do not go round the room (too slow). Split into **parallel breakouts** of about five (for example six groups of five) at table level, then one spokesperson per group reports. Isolated groups avoid early ideas contaminating everyone else. Breakouts suit ideation, inspiration and concept generation. Add buffer time and give the sharing-back a time structure. This approach is reported to work for 50, 100 and 200 people.
- Reduce hierarchy effects by silent generation, anonymous notes, and having the senior person speak last.

## Politics and Power
- Capture power dynamics and red flags in the client snapshot (ch01).
- Together-alone brainstorming gives everyone an equal voice; anonymous exercises suit senior or high-stakes rooms.
- Pre-frame sceptics before the session; do not hide an agenda.
- A persuasive person pulling the group to a pet topic: park it; write the chosen challenge as a visible statement.

## Common Mistakes
- Ignoring the disruptor; sarcasm.
- Parking topics and never returning to them, which costs trust.
- Letting the decider be absent, or voting in public before others (anchoring).
- Treating the vote as binding; treating the messy middle as failure.
- Keeping 30+ people in one open discussion.
- Taking sides as facilitator.

## Checklist
- [ ] Decider named; voting convention announced?
- [ ] Ground rules and parking lot introduced at the start?
- [ ] Easy tasks ready for restless participants?
- [ ] Return time set for each parked item?
- [ ] Large group: breakouts of about five, spokesperson, buffer?
