# Chapter 07: The Design Sprint, Day by Day

## Core Idea
A design sprint is a time-boxed sequence that moves a team from a vague challenge to a tested prototype with one decider. Each exercise's output feeds the next, so the sequence is the method. It works because it diverges fully (silent, individual), converges by voting, decides with one accountable person, and tests with real users before anything is built. The facilitator manages time, process and energy; experts supply domain content.

## Formats
| Format | Shape | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Original | five days, Monday to Friday | Friday is the user test |
| Compressed | four days: Day 1 define + sketch, Day 2 decide + storyboard, Day 3 prototype, Day 4 test | decision-makers needed only on days 1-2, which is the main reason to compress; the fifth day may be a retrospective; many exercises are under half their original length |
| One-day workshop | morning: goal and questions; afternoon: sketching | no prototype; for training or alignment |
| Remote | half-day sessions over two weeks | see ch09 |
Never cut user testing to save time; it is the reason for the sprint. Never run it as a brainstorm with no decision step.

## Preparation (before day one)
1. Confirm availability; some roles are needed only on the first two days.
2. Make sure the decider attends.
3. Set up the room and materials: sticky notes, dot stickers, markers, big whiteboards or wall space; no hunting for supplies on day one.
4. Pre-research the day before: rough problem area, likely map direction, long-term goal and questions, a few lightning-demo examples. In remote work pre-fill boards from pre-workshop interviews (ch09).
5. Facilitator: read the method, sleep well, spend about an hour on what is new in the field, frame the run as an experiment. Treat the prep plan as a basis for improvising, not a script.
6. Tell the team the five principles (ch03).

## Day 1: Define and Start Solutions
1. **Expert interviews** with HMW notes (reframe problems as open questions, not solutions); silent dot vote on HMWs; cluster.
2. **Long-term goal** ("two years from now, best case") and **sprint questions** ("Can we...?" risks), note-and-vote, decider chooses (ch05). Keep goal and top three questions on the wall all sprint.
3. **Map** (about 45 min), see below.
4. **Lightning demos** (20-30 min solo research, then presentations; one note per big idea).
5. **Four-part sketch** (notes, ideas, Crazy Eights, solution sketch), ch06. In a four-day compressed format the sketching takes the afternoon of day 1.

### The Map
- Frame with four loose placeholder columns: Discover, Learn, Use, Goal.
- Actors on the left; keep one main persona (a customer, plus a B2B buyer or delivery person if relevant).
- Write the end point at the far right as an **observable outcome** ("buys in the online store", "uses again").
- Fill touchpoints from Discover onward; record **physical actions only**, no emotions or motives.
- **Target**: cluster the HMW notes from interviews on the map; where they concentrate becomes the sprint focus.
- The moderator starts writing early and rubs out, rather than asking "what should we do?" on a blank page. Contradictions are fine; the map is a targeting tool. Do not spend hours; do not chase a correct map.

## Day 2: Decide and Storyboard
1. **Decision sequence** (reveal, heat map, speed critique, straw poll, super vote), ch06.
2. **User test flow**: each person writes six action steps (clicks, taps or movements), one per note; tip: write the first step from the map and the last from the map and sprint questions, then fill the middle. Read aloud 30-60 s each (decider first or last). Vote: one dot each, decider two (one confirms the baseline flow, one adds a step from another flow), about 5 min. The winning notes become the row of storyboard cells and pre-fill them; this cut storyboarding from up to 3 hours to about 1.5.
3. **Storyboard** (about eight cells plus spares): put the six flow notes inside, one note may need several screens; fill first and last cells first, then the middle, then key screens; one or two key screens carry most of the idea, spend time there. Reuse drawings from any concept, including unchosen ones. Include only what the test needs (skip login if untested). No new ideas now; leave no open questions so the builder need not look anything up. Time-box with shorter boxes than expected. Give the drawing to one person so the facilitator faces the group; if two people debate on the side, give them a side task (copy, content).

## Day 3: Prototype
Believable and high-fidelity on the surface, facade only, built in about a day; a human can stand in for the back end (Wizard of Oz). Short prototypes limit sunk-cost bias: more time makes the team defend the work instead of learning from it. Build only what answers the sprint questions; fake the rest (click-through slides, or a human reply). Do not prototype for weeks, do not add screens that make the build harder.

## Day 4: User Testing
- About 5 participants. Interviewer in one room, the team watching via camera from another (remote: two people per session, interviewer and note-taker).
- Colour-coded notes (good, bad, confused) per person, grouped by step.
- A failure repeated across three of five users is a clear signal.
- Interviewers ask open, non-leading questions ("walk me through what you are thinking") and never supply the answer.
- Limit feedback per session; one earlier run produced about 200 notes that took hours to process. Consolidate on the same board.
- Recommended from the original format: retrospective on the last day if time allows.

## Facilitating a Multi-Day Sprint
Keep energy up and positive; reassure often because the process feels unnatural; be firm but fair about time (people over-run on sketches); never turn your back on the group; stay tactful, you share close quarters for days. The decider decides; the facilitator must not inject their own suggestion. Close with a shared fun activity.

## Common Mistakes
- Waiting for consensus; discussing concepts during reveal.
- Starting the storyboard from empty cells (invites new ideas and long debate).
- Describing an interface verbally instead of showing a visual example.
- Skipping the decider; over-collecting feedback; two head-to-head prototypes on a first run.
- Treating the sprint as a perfect first run.

## Checklist
- [ ] Decider attending days 1-2?
- [ ] Materials, room, pre-research done?
- [ ] Goal and top three questions on the wall for the whole sprint?
- [ ] Map done in about 45 min, physical actions only?
- [ ] Test flow voted before storyboarding?
- [ ] Prototype scope limited to the test?
- [ ] About five test users, notes colour-coded?
