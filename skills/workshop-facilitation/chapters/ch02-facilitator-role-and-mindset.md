# Chapter 02: The Facilitator Role, Mindset and Questioning

## Core Idea
The facilitator owns **how** a group reaches a decision and stays neutral on **what** is decided. The value is meaningful, efficient work with an actionable outcome. Facilitation means "to make easy": sequence and visualise the conversation, manage friction and time, and take the burden of collaboration off the team so people can use their expertise. Success is measured by whether the team wants you back, not by how much was produced.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Process expert, not content expert**: responsibility for the decision stays with participants. Be a guide, not a consultant or a hero; draw out the group's ideas instead of showing yours.
- **Eight facilitator qualities**: visualise information; guide rather than consult; stay neutral on content; use questions as the main tool; adapt; be authentic; manage energy; give clear instructions (ch03).
- **Question-led discovery**: questions create more value than answers, and people act on ideas they reached themselves. Ideas that arrive from outside feel foreign and get resisted. Clients usually hold the answer; they lack objectivity because emotion clouds judgment.
  - Reflect a question back as a question. Ask "what would you like me to focus on?" instead of "here is what to do".
  - Keep asking until the client understands their situation better than before; then ask whether the problem is worth solving and what solving it is worth.
  - Worked example: asking "do all the elements feel the same size?" let a client conclude the key element needed to be bigger.
- **Eliciting criteria**: before producing anything, draw out the four or five criteria the client will judge the work by. Start broad (what do you want from this and how will you use it?), narrow to a specific deliverable, drill back out to check it fits, iterate. Ask about uses, not finished products; clients often know what they want only when they see options. Use open questions framed as "I" statements about your own work ("I have done X; is that what you are looking for?"); they are easier to answer than "what do you expect?". Surface personal, brand and company filters.
- **Priming (the facilitator's own state)**: the facilitator's mental state shapes the room more than technique. Do a deliberate routine before the session, not a warm-up during it: free journaling, walking the room and imagining who sits where and what they feel, choosing one anchor word to carry in, a short empathy-evoking piece to move attention from your own fear to the room, a body reset (one big belly breath, a full rib-cage exhale). Choose the priming to fit the room and keep several options ready.
- **Unshakable adaptability**: the highest-value skill is an internalised way of working that adapts live (attendance changes, the client arrives late, people switch to remote), not memorised formats. Learn a method as taught first, then adapt it; hesitation costs credibility. Keep a broad toolkit so you can switch.

## Step-by-Step: Staying Neutral and Useful
1. **State the role** at the start: you manage the process, you will cut discussions and park topics, you do not take sides. Get a verbal yes.
2. **Do not reveal opinions** on content. If the facilitator must also be the decider or a voter, announce the role switch out loud.
3. **Visualise**: write what is said on a board, notes or a digital canvas so it can be built on and corrected. Never turn your back to the group; have someone else scribe or draw (ch03).
4. **Ask, do not tell**: replace statements with "what else?", "what is behind that?", "who else has something?"; close with "is there anything else?" to move on without telling people to stop.
5. **Be transparent about your agenda**; hidden agendas destroy trust.
6. **Prime before the session** and arrive open, not defensive: people sense your state.
7. **After the session**, note what you would change; volunteer to facilitate often.

## Variations
- **Personal style**: fast thinkers can reveal live, reflective ones prepare between sessions; let your personality set the pace rather than copying a framework rigidly.
- **Giving voice to junior or marginalised participants**: write their point down, ask why, then bridge to a deeper understanding.
- **Pre-framing sceptics**: name the person with institutional knowledge and ask them to suspend disbelief for the session.
- **First-time facilitator**: read the method before running it, sleep well (multi-day formats are physically demanding), keep two or three lightning-demo examples ready, frame the run to the team as an experiment ("let us see if it works"), expect timing mistakes, and plan a shared fun activity at the end. See ch07.

## Common Mistakes
- Answering immediately; the reflex closes discovery. Presenting a solution before the client states the problem in their own words.
- Entering to prove expertise or to rush the agenda; the group mirrors that energy.
- Trying to change people's moods; let the angry stay angry and work with it.
- Checking the phone before creative work; treating participants as obstacles.
- Optimising for knowing exercises by heart, for output alone, or adding burden through complicated instructions and tools.
- Relying on one technique for every group; hiding weak points of a framework.

## Checklist
- [ ] Neutral on content, and the role stated to the group?
- [ ] Questions prepared for the brief and for each phase?
- [ ] Priming routine done; one anchor word chosen?
- [ ] Plan B ready for late arrivals, absences, switch to remote?
- [ ] Scribe or drawer assigned so you can face the room?
- [ ] Tools and instructions as simple as they can be?
