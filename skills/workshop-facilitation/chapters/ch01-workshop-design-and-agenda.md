# Chapter 01: Workshop Design and Agenda

## Core Idea
A workshop is designed backwards from an outcome, not forwards from a list of favourite exercises. Capture what the client really needs, agree goal, context and constraints, then build the agenda on a fixed phase skeleton (collect, choose, create, commit) and fill it from a small repertoire of exercises timed around the group's energy. Most failures trace to mismatched expectations or an overloaded agenda, not to a bad exercise.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Client snapshot (capture before designing)**: situation; primary expectations (what the client says); secondary expectations (unstated motives, for example a sponsor who needs to look good); power dynamics and red flags; budget; deadline; constraints (remote, hybrid, language, time zone). Ask about the secondary layer on purpose, it shapes the design more than the stated brief.
- **Three agreements, in order**: (1) **Goal**, the one thing the session or piece must achieve; (2) **Context**, format, platform, duration, audience and the action people should take afterwards; (3) **Parameters**, the sandbox: rules, what it must do and must never do. Without these three the team guesses.
- **Phase skeleton (the 4 Cs)**: **Collect** (dump every topic, challenge, idea; no solutions, no commitments) -> **Choose** (decide what this session addresses; park the rest in a visible parking lot with a plan to revisit) -> **Create** (everyone generates solutions for the one chosen challenge, written as a visible statement first) -> **Commit** (solutions become owned actions: what, who, when). Describe the agenda to the client as phases (day 1 = collect + choose, day 2 = create + commit), not as a list of exercise names. The skeleton also works for a single section of a long workshop.
- **Diverge, messy middle, converge**: group decisions move from many options, through a "groan zone" of tangents, dropping energy and dominant voices, to a narrowing decision. Show this curve at the start; it makes the mess feel normal. Teams that hit the middle without support abandon or postpone the project. Support the middle with silent writing and turn-taking (see ch04).
- **Emotion curve**: draw the expected mood over the day (happiness versus frustration on the y-axis, time on the x-axis), mark the predicted dips, and point at the relevant dip before each exercise so low moments read as planned. Start strong, end strong; one planned dip in the middle is acceptable.

## Step-by-Step: Designing a Workshop
1. **Clarify the outcome**: what exists at the end that did not exist at the start (a goal, three chosen experiments, a roadmap, a tested prototype)? If the answer is "team building" or "a good discussion", push back; those are by-products.
2. **Run the client snapshot** and the three agreements. Translate coded words ("energetic", "innovative") into concrete references by asking what they mean until you can point at an example. See ch02 for the questioning style.
3. **Choose the participants**: mix people who execute with people who manage; the **decider** must attend (ch04). Keep outcome-producing workshops small (see Numbers).
4. **Lay the skeleton**: assign each phase a time window, then pick one or two exercises per phase from the cheatsheet picker. Put the most important work in the morning.
5. **Plan fewer exercises than feels right**; a crammed agenda overwhelms people and overruns. Add buffer, especially for larger groups.
6. **Set expectations before, at the start and during** (see below).
7. **Prepare materials and the room**, a worked example for every exercise, and a plan B for broken plans (ch02, ch03).
8. **Design the end**: decide what the output document looks like and who owns the follow-up before the day starts (ch10).

## Setting and Checking Expectations
- **Before**: contact each participant (a short call, or a pre-activity in the workspace) and ask what they expect; compare answers and follow up on outliers. A written pre-brief 2-7 days before (your role, the schedule, the tools, a short welcome message, a 5-10 minute pre-activity, a reminder the day before) is reported to raise engagement markedly. Use the call when the group is small or political, the written route when it is large.
- **At the start**: state purpose, goal and agenda. Each person writes their ideal end point on a sticky note, reads it out, and the notes go on the wall.
- **During**: revisit the notes after lunch or whenever the group drifts ("this is what we said this morning").
- **Role statement**: say that you will cut discussions and park topics, and get a verbal yes. Say that people may feel overwhelmed and ask for explicit buy-in. A signed social contract helps hold sceptics to the rules.

## Numbers and Rules of Thumb
| Topic | Guidance from the material |
|---|---|
| Group size | 3-10 for cross-functional exercises; about 7 for retrospectives; about 8 for outcome workshops, each extra person adds roughly an hour of discussion |
| Focused work per day | about 3 hours of genuinely focused work; rest is breaks, transitions, energy |
| Repertoire | keep about 10 reusable exercises, swap about half per session |
| Example sequence for one day | Collect (sailboat) about 45 min; Choose (vote, turn the winner into a "How might we") collect + choose about 1 h; Create (lightning demos about 45 min, then 10-for-10); Commit (action board) |
| Short formats | 1 h problem-solving jam; 90-120 min growth or goal workshop; 2 h design-thinking intro; one-day sprint (morning goal and questions, afternoon sketching) |
| Large groups | add buffer; use parallel breakouts (ch04) |

## Variations
- **Time-poor**: run one phase only, usually Collect + Choose, and schedule Create + Commit separately.
- **Senior leaders, high stakes**: skip forced icebreakers; use individual, anonymous exercises so people open up on their own (ch02).
- **Unknown group**: open with a short, low-risk icebreaker (name, first job, one lesson, written on a note and shared in small groups) and a quick creative-confidence demo (a 45-second drawing of a neighbour, then debrief the feelings).

## Common Mistakes
- Accepting "you are the creative, make it up" as a brief; giving a deadline without agreeing it.
- Assuming people know the purpose; leaving mood to chance; taking the dips personally.
- Choosing exercises with no link to the phases; treating team building as the outcome.
- Running past the energy window; overloading the day.
- Describing the agenda as a script instead of phases, so any change feels like failure.

## Checklist
- [ ] Outcome stated as something that exists at the end?
- [ ] Goal, context, parameters agreed with the client?
- [ ] Secondary expectations and power dynamics asked about?
- [ ] Decider named and confirmed to attend?
- [ ] Each phase has a time window and at most one or two exercises?
- [ ] Group size within range; buffer added?
- [ ] Expectations collected before, written at the start, revisited mid-day?
- [ ] Follow-up owner and output format decided?
