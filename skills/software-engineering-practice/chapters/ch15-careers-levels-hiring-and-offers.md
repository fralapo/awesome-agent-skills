# Chapter 15: Careers, Levels, Hiring and Offers

## Core Idea
Engineering careers inside organisations are shaped by written levels, relative ratings, committees and who is able to see your work. Hiring is a set of imperfect measuring instruments run by people under time pressure. Most of what follows is mechanism plus practitioner judgment. Organisations differ, change their rules often, and publish few numbers, so every rule of thumb here is a dated position until checked against the employer's own documents. This chapter is not legal, tax or employment advice; contracts and local rules decide the details.

## 15.1 Two ladders, one choice (position)
- **Thesis (position)**: most senior engineering careers split into an individual-contributor (IC) track and a people-management track. Choose by the work you want to do every day, not by the title.
- **Mechanism**: at high levels an IC is judged on technical direction and impact across several teams, without direct reports. A "tech lead" is often an informal role on top of a normal level, not a separate rung. Influence without authority does not scale indefinitely: to grow the reach of your decisions beyond what you can review yourself, you usually need to multiply other people's work, which is what management does. A manager is assessed largely on the growth of the people on the team, so a good manager has a stake in removing obstacles to their reports' promotion.
- **Evidence**: practitioner observation across large organisations, not a controlled comparison.
- **Trade-off**: the IC path keeps you close to the code but can cap reach; the manager path widens reach but moves time from building to coordinating and to hard conversations.
- **Does not hold when**: the organisation has a single ladder with equal status for both, or when a role is explicitly hybrid. Ask how the company actually promotes people in each track.

## 15.2 Levels define expectations, not hours (position)
- **Thesis**: at each level the expectation changes in kind: from following a plan, to owning a feature, to shaping a problem with ambiguous requirements, to setting direction for a group. Hours worked do not move you up a level.
- **Mechanism**: the written level description lists scope, autonomy, and the kind of design and communication expected. Promotion cases are judged against that text by a committee that may not know you, so the evidence must match the description, not the feeling that you are doing more.
- **Practice**: keep a running record of decisions you made, designs you wrote, people you unblocked and outcomes you measured, with dates. Ask your manager directly what is missing for the next level, and get the answer in writing.
- **Trade-off**: documenting every cycle takes time that could go into the work itself.
- **Does not hold when**: the company has no published levels. Then the rule becomes: agree the expectations explicitly with your manager before the cycle, and record how they were met.

## 15.3 Relative ratings and calibration
- **Thesis**: in many large organisations the rating you receive is relative to peers in a calibration meeting, so your result depends partly on the people compared with you.
- **Mechanism**: a typical cycle gathers a self-assessment, peer feedback and a manager review, then a calibration process compares people at the same level across teams and assigns ratings within a budget. Because the budget is limited, the same strong work can produce a weaker rating in a stronger group. Rating ladders with a fixed distribution make a single weak cycle less damaging than a pattern of weak cycles, and formal improvement plans are usually the last step before exit, not the first warning.
- **Practice**: find out how calibration works in your company, who speaks for you, and what evidence reaches that meeting. Make your impact visible to people outside your team, because they often decide.
- **Trade-off**: relative systems can reward visibility and political skill over quality; absolute systems can drift toward grade inflation. Neither is free of distortion.
- **Does not hold when**: the company does not use relative ranking. Then the important question is the criteria, not the curve.

## 15.4 Visibility, team matching and internal moves (position)
- **Thesis**: impact that nobody sees does not count much in promotion, and changing team is often a legitimate way to restart growth.
- **Mechanism**: in large organisations finding a team for an open position is a matching problem. When qualified candidates outnumber open roles, matching becomes the bottleneck, and internal moves are easier than external ones because the new team already knows your work. A stall of one or two years in the same scope usually costs momentum; practitioners report that moving sooner than they did was the decision they regretted most.
- **Practice**: write down the outcome of each project in one line that a senior reader can repeat. Talk to managers in other teams before you need them. Ask whether the new role builds the next level's skills, not only whether it is interesting.
- **Trade-off**: moving teams resets relationships and context, and a new team may not value your previous work.
- **Does not hold when**: the current team is growing you well. Moving for novelty alone is a weak reason.

## 15.5 Interviews measure something specific
- **Thesis**: an interview is an instrument. Its quality depends on what it is designed to measure, how consistently interviewers score, and whether the signal is independent of résumé, referral and first impression.
- **Mechanism**: structured interviews with a fixed rubric, independent scoring before discussion, and questions tied to the job give more reliable signals than free conversation. Typical loops combine a phone screen, one or more technical rounds and a behavioural round. Problem-solving rounds that reward recalling a known trick mostly measure preparation time; rounds that ask the candidate to reason aloud about an ambiguous problem, state assumptions and test them, measure more of what the job needs.
- **Practice for candidates**: practise reasoning aloud, not only producing an answer. Choose the language you can write fluently under pressure: syntax you have to look up consumes attention needed for the problem. For behavioural questions, use a clear structure of situation, action and result with numbers. In hypothetical questions, ask about the goal before proposing a solution.
- **Practice for interviewers**: score on evidence written during the session; avoid rounds that longer preparation can buy. Give the candidate a realistic work sample where possible.
- **Trade-off**: work samples take time from candidates and interviewers and can exploit free labour if they are long or unpaid.
- **Does not hold when**: the role is one where a specific, well-known technique is part of the daily job; then testing it is legitimate.

## 15.6 Interviewing the company
- **Thesis (position)**: you are evaluating the employer too, and the most useful information comes from questions about how decisions are made, not from the pitch.
- **Mechanism**: ask how on-call is shared, how code review works and how long reviews take, how a decision was made recently and who disagreed, what happened to people who left, how performance is measured and what the typical promotion timeline is. Vague or defensive answers to these are signals in themselves.
- **Red flags**: unpaid trial work beyond a short, bounded task; no code review; feedback reserved for people who are leaving; a team that cannot describe its own outcomes.
- **Trade-off**: asking hard questions can cost an offer with a weak employer; that cost is also information.
- **Does not hold when**: you have no leverage at all and must take the first available job. Then the same questions still help you plan your next step.

## 15.7 Signals that others can check
- **Thesis**: recruiters and hiring managers look for signals they can verify without trusting you: shipped work, public contributions, referrals from people they trust, and a clear account of results.
- **Mechanism**: a referral typically moves a profile past the first filter, because someone vouches for it and the screen becomes a check rather than a sort. Public work verifies itself: a code review on someone else's repository shows how you reason in public. Automated screens often parse keywords from the text of a document, so a scanned image or heavy layout may be invisible to them.
- **Practice**: keep a CV of one or two pages with measurable results, reordered for each job description. Contribute to projects others use, starting with documentation or a small bug, and keep the evidence. Apply in volume only to a short list of targets you researched; spray-and-pray applications without preparation rarely pay back.
- **Trade-off**: public work takes time and exposes mistakes; the best signal is slow to build.
- **Does not hold when**: your field relies on confidentiality (defence, some finance) and public work is not allowed. Then rely on references and anonymised case descriptions.

## 15.8 Offers: compare net, total and risk
- **Thesis**: an offer is a bundle with different risk. Compare the net value after local taxes, the cost of living, and the expected value of variable pay, with its uncertainty stated.
- **Mechanism**: fixed base pay is predictable. Bonuses depend on targets and on the company's results, so ask for the target percentage, the typical payout over recent years and how it is calculated. Equity in a private company is illiquid and its value depends on future events; treat it as a lottery ticket unless you can reason about its terms. Equity in a listed company vests over time and can be worth more or less than the grant suggested on the day you sign.
- **Practice**: ask for more than the figure you want, because the final number usually lands between the first offer and the request. Negotiate the whole package, including notice period, start date, remote terms and learning budget. Reject an unserious first offer calmly and in writing.
- **Trade-off**: pushing too hard can cost an offer, and a fixed prize with a long notice period can lock you in.
- **Does not hold when**: the market is very tight for the employer and the candidate has little bargaining power; then securing a fair base is often the priority.

## 15.9 Contracts: intellectual property and confidentiality
- **Thesis**: read the intellectual-property and confidentiality clauses before signing, and declare pre-existing personal work in writing.
- **Mechanism**: some contracts claim ideas or projects linked to the job, or started while employed, even in your own time. Declaring existing projects before you start makes their status clear. Confidentiality agreements usually continue after leaving: you may describe your experience in general terms but not disclose details. Taking code or documents to a competitor is treated much more seriously than discussing general skills.
- **Trade-off**: a precise declaration may restrict a hobby project; a vague one may create later disputes.
- **Does not hold when**: the contract is under a jurisdiction with different rules. Check the document itself and, for anything material, a qualified lawyer. This chapter does not interpret contracts.

## 15.10 Restructuring and layoffs
- **Thesis (position)**: sudden layoffs are usually decided by budget and portfolio changes, not by a verdict on the individual, but they still deserve preparation.
- **Mechanism**: when a company cuts cost, whole teams or product lines can close regardless of individual performance. Competition inside the organisation can reduce solidarity, which makes shared documentation and cross-team relationships more valuable. In a sector-wide downturn a single dismissal weighs less in a job search, because recruiters see the same pattern elsewhere.
- **Practice**: keep a current record of outcomes and references; know your notice and severance rights in your contract; maintain relationships outside the company before you need them.
- **Trade-off**: preparing for the worst can drain attention from the current role.
- **Does not hold when**: the layoff follows documented performance problems; then the feedback history is the right place to look.

## 15.11 Hours are not impact, and visibility is a skill
- **Thesis (position)**: working long hours is not a merit signal; visible outcomes are. Presence and late hours can still be rewarded informally, and the pressure to match peers can cost more than the workload itself.
- **Mechanism**: managers see what is reported, reviewed and discussed. A person who closes tasks and writes down outcomes is easier to promote than one who is seen late in the office but cannot explain results.
- **Practice**: write short, dated summaries of outcomes for your manager; plan one or two moments a quarter when your work is shown to a wider audience; set limits on hours and review them with your manager.
- **Trade-off**: visibility work takes time and can look like self-promotion if overdone.
- **Does not hold when**: your team's standards are truly based on working hours. Then the conversation is about the culture, and changing team or company may be the realistic option.
