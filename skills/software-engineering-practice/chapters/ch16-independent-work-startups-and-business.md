# Chapter 16: Independent Work, Startups and Building a Business

## Core Idea
Working for yourself, or building a company, changes what you are paid for, who carries risk and how you know a plan is working. The mechanisms below are general. They do not give tax, legal, investment or regulatory advice, and national rules for self-employment, company formation and tax change often. Verify any figure against the current official source and a qualified adviser before relying on it.

## 16.1 Time you sell is not time you work (position)
- **Thesis (position)**: a freelancer's billable hours are only part of the working week. Pricing must cover the whole week, not just the hours of delivery.
- **Mechanism**: sales, proposals, administration, bookkeeping, maintenance of tools, learning and unpaid support all consume time that nobody invoices. Employees have these costs paid for them, along with holidays, sick pay and training; a self-employed person funds them from the same income. A rate that looks generous per hour can yield a modest income once the non-billable share is subtracted.
- **Practice**: measure a month of real time, split it into billable and non-billable, and price from the gross figure: target income plus benefits you fund yourself plus overhead, divided by the realistic billable hours, not the theoretical ones.
- **Trade-off**: pricing for the full cost may lose some price-sensitive clients; that is a choice about which clients to serve.
- **Does not hold when**: you sell a fixed deliverable with a known effort; then price the project, not the hour.

## 16.2 Packages scale better than one-to-one time (position)
- **Thesis (position)**: one-to-one consulting has overhead that is larger than its stated duration. Packages, templates and recorded or repeatable deliverables spread that overhead.
- **Mechanism**: each call carries scheduling, preparation, follow-up, invoicing and context switching. A fixed package with a defined scope and delivery method lets the cost of preparation be reused.
- **Trade-off**: packages can feel less personal, and scope creep moves the cost back to you.
- **Does not hold when**: the client's problem is unique and cannot be scoped in advance.

## 16.3 Concentration is a single point of failure
- **Thesis**: depending on one large client or one sales channel is the most common way independent businesses fail suddenly.
- **Mechanism**: a single client's decision to stop, pay late or go bankrupt removes most of the income at once. A single marketplace or platform can change fees or ranking, and a business built inside it inherits those changes. Late payment and dependency on a few payers also erode cash even when the revenue looks healthy.
- **Practice**: track revenue by client and by channel every month; set a ceiling on the share of revenue from any one of them; hold a cash buffer large enough for a few months of costs; check payment terms before accepting work.
- **Trade-off**: diversification takes effort and can dilute focus; a single anchor client is often how a small business starts.
- **Does not hold when**: the anchor client is contractually committed for a long period and the cash flow is predictable. Then the risk is when the contract ends, so plan that date in advance.

## 16.4 Cash business and equity business: a lens, not a category
- **Thesis (position)**: some businesses earn in proportion to the time of the person running them (a cash business). Others are built to be sold or to scale without the founder's time (an equity business). The distinction is a way to see which kind of plan you are making.
- **Mechanism**: in a cash business, stopping work stops income, so the main constraint is hours and the main lever is price. In an equity business, value is created by systems, product and team, so the main constraint is capital, time to build and the market's willingness to buy.
- **Practice**: decide which lens your plan uses and be explicit about the switch. A freelancer who wants to build a product must plan the product's time separately from client work.
- **Trade-off**: an equity business usually needs an upfront investment of time or money before any income, and its outcome is less certain.
- **Does not hold when**: a business mixes both models, which is common. Use the lens to ask questions, not to classify.

## 16.5 Company form: limited liability is not a free lunch
- **Thesis**: choosing between operating as an individual and forming a company changes personal liability, administration, tax treatment and how profits reach you. Each choice has rules that vary by country and change over time.
- **Mechanism**: an individual enterprise usually leaves the owner personally liable for business debts. A limited company limits liability to the capital invested, but adds administration, minimum capital in some systems, and in many countries two layers of tax when profits are distributed to the owner as dividends after corporate tax.
- **Practice**: separate business and personal accounts from the start; keep invoices and receipts; set an owner's salary or drawing that the business can sustain; check current thresholds for registration and reporting with a local accountant.
- **Trade-off**: the simplest form is easy to start but exposes you more; the company form protects more but costs more to run.
- **Does not hold when**: you are in a jurisdiction where the rules are unusual. This section gives no figures on purpose, because they change.

## 16.6 Problems before products
- **Thesis**: an idea is not a business. The first test is whether people with the problem will talk to you, use a prototype and pay something.
- **Mechanism**: interviews with people who have the problem reveal which parts of the idea matter and which were assumptions. A demonstration of a working prototype shows whether the thing is understood. The first paying customer is the first real test of willingness to pay, and it often changes the product.
- **Practice**: list the assumptions the plan depends on; for each, define the cheapest test that could prove it false; run the tests in order of how much the plan depends on them.
- **Trade-off**: validation takes time and can feel like delay when building feels productive.
- **Does not hold when**: the regulatory or technical risk is the main question. Then a feasibility study comes first.

## 16.7 Venture money and most startups failing
- **Thesis**: the venture model accepts that most investments fail, because a few returns are expected to pay for the rest. Bootstrapping, meaning growth funded by revenue, fits businesses that aim for steady income; it may not fit businesses that need global scale quickly.
- **Mechanism**: venture funds diversify across many companies and need a small number of very large outcomes. A founder who accepts venture money therefore accepts a plan designed for large outcomes, with dilution and expectations to match.
- **Trade-off**: venture capital can buy speed and reach; it also brings governance, reporting and pressure toward growth at any cost.
- **Does not hold when**: the company is not seeking scale or a sale. Choose the funding model after you know the goal, not before.

## 16.8 Reading a funding round
- **Thesis**: the headline valuation matters less than the terms that decide who controls the company and who gets paid when it is sold.
- **Mechanism**: a round can sell new shares (primary money into the company) or buy existing shares (secondary sale to shareholders), and the two have different effects. A lead investor often negotiates protective rights such as veto over certain decisions. Liquidation preferences decide the order of payment at a sale. Board composition decides who can remove management. Investors in early stages often weigh the team's background more than the idea, which means founders' track records shape the terms.
- **Practice**: ask a lawyer and an experienced founder to explain each clause; model the outcome of a sale at several prices; know what dilution each round creates.
- **Trade-off**: rapid funding reduces the founders' share and can shift control.
- **Does not hold when**: the company is bootstrapped and takes no outside money.

## 16.9 Exits, acquisitions and what happens to people
- **Thesis (position)**: most acquisitions are described as successes by the buyer and experienced as a change of control by staff, users and founders.
- **Mechanism**: in an acquisition, employees are often offered positions with the buyer. Founders are frequently offered a payment tied to staying for a period (an earn-out or retention). The product may be integrated, reduced or closed. Deep-technology companies often take years to become profitable and are frequently acquired rather than listed; this is a common pattern, not a measured rule.
- **Practice**: read retention and earn-out terms as carefully as the price; decide in advance what you would do if you were not retained; keep the customers' data portable.
- **Trade-off**: a sale provides certainty of some return and ends the independence of the product.
- **Does not hold when**: the company has been acquired on a clear, public-interest basis with binding commitments. Still check the commitments in writing.

## 16.10 Pricing software: subscription or one-off (position)
- **Thesis (position)**: subscription suits software that is continuously changed and served; a one-off purchase suits software that is finished and used locally.
- **Mechanism**: subscriptions create recurring revenue and an obligation to keep delivering value. Vendors with a subscription have an incentive to raise prices once customers are locked in, and this can erode trust. One-off licences shift the work to new versions and upgrade decisions.
- **Trade-off**: a subscription can fund ongoing work; a one-off can feel fairer to users but leaves the vendor with less stable income.
- **Does not hold when**: the customer's use is occasional or the product needs a backend service. Then a usage-based or hybrid model may fit.

## 16.11 Reputation and networks built before you need them
- **Thesis (position)**: reputation and networks are built by giving value over time, and they are slow to build and fast to lose.
- **Mechanism**: public work, open source, talks and writing are evidence that others can check, and the people who see it may later recommend you. Anonymous accounts rarely build the trust that a named person does. Consistency across time matters more than one spectacular result. One-to-one mentoring is a different skill from teaching in general, and volunteer communities often depend on one or two people whose departure ends them.
- **Practice**: contribute before you ask; share what you learn in a form others can use; keep a record of what you have given; widen your network through people you already work with.
- **Trade-off**: visibility exposes you to criticism and to unpaid requests.
- **Does not hold when**: your field or your situation makes public work unsafe or not allowed. Then build reputation through peers and written references.
