# Idea Pipeline, Originality and Team Systems

Practitioner notes on how ideas are produced on demand (a staged pipeline), what "original" really means, and which repeatable systems let a team innovate without waiting for intuition. Extends "Generate Ideas" in `concept-and-insight-in-design-practice.md`, the idea-generator role and "Antenna Building" in the visionary role. Process frames (design thinking, design sprint, agile) are in `problem-discovery-and-framing.md`; the look is the art director's, the craft the graphic designer's.

## The Four-Stage Idea Pipeline
Ideas arrive more reliably when the stages are separated and none is skipped.
1. **Clarify.** Have the requester restate the request until it is clear to both sides; build shared understanding, then reduce the problem to **two or three specific keywords**. Precise keywords ("fragile", "inherited") steer later stages; generic ones ("quality", "modern") steer nothing. Skipping this step is the most common cause of a wasted session.
2. **Saturate.** Feed the mind with material **far from the problem**: art, music, literature, film, photography. Filter what you take in through the keywords and collect anecdotes, images and references that touch them. Distance matters; the nearest references (competitors, the category) only reproduce the category.
3. **Incubate.** Stop working. Think about the problem briefly before sleep and then leave it; use routine, low-attention activities (showering, walking, running) so the subconscious can recombine the material. Ending the session right after saturation, with no break, wastes the stage that does the combining.
4. **Capture.** Write the idea down at once (notebook, voice note, waterproof paper). An unwritten idea evaporates, and the idea that arrives in the shower is the one least likely to come back.

Rules of thumb:
- If no answer comes, wait and run the loop again later; it is a pipeline, not a force-feed.
- Run the pipeline per keyword pair when the problem has several angles.
- Keep incubation a scheduled step with a return time, otherwise it turns into avoidance (the distinction between deliberate distraction and procrastination is in the visionary role).
- Concept and execution produced at the same instant leave no room for discovery; the pipeline puts distance between them.

## Originality Is Relative and Usually Imported
- **"New" is relative to the audience.** Define who will see the work first; what is new to them decides what counts. "Never seen before" is rarely achievable and is the wrong target.
- Most original work **borrows a familiar idea from a different field** and presents it in a new way: fashion from architecture, photography from cinema, cinema from musical theatre. Look outside your own discipline for the idea, then adapt it.
- **Reintroduce an old idea in a new context**: same idea, different place, medium or audience.
- **Big/small**: make something normally small appear big, or the reverse, to force a fresh look (a scale shift, see the Scale Shift type in the wit checklist).
- **Exercise: photograph the overlooked.** Take a close-up lens to what nobody looks at (the back of a keyboard, cracks in pavement, the printing on the underside of a package). The new viewpoints become raw material for the saturation stage.
- Assuming an idea must be wholly invented is a mistake; the aim is a distinctive recombination (see `art-director` ch08 for disclosure of lineage).

## Systems Over Intuition (Team Innovation)
A team innovates repeatably when it owns systems for three skills, built in this order:
1. **Problem-solving system**: a structured way to frame and attack a big problem.
2. **Decision-making system**: explicit rules for choosing, instead of the loudest voice or intuition (criteria written first, see the strategy ladder in `creative-process-habits-and-review-culture.md`).
3. **Idea generation and validation system**: how ideas are produced (the pipeline above) and tested before being built.

How to install them: study a structured method (a design sprint is one example), attend hands-on training, or visit a team that already runs the process. Prefer options you can schedule and control, and give the team the method itself so they apply it to daily work rather than depending on an outside facilitator.

Common mistake: relying on delivery rituals alone. Agile cycles organise how a product is built; they do not teach a team to solve big problems or to start new projects. Pair them with the three systems (frame comparison in `problem-discovery-and-framing.md`).

## Small Lessons from Early Failures
- A **sketch is a thinking tool, not a precise drawing**: a messy sketch that carries the idea is enough at the start. Validate and develop an idea beyond its first version; ideas never tested stay at version one.
- **Sequence of experience**: a pack, a booklet or a product opening can be designed as a sequence with a tuned pace, so there is a moment of anticipation before the reveal.
- **Simplicity as the goal**: a product that disappears into its context can be the whole brief; test every added element against that.
- Presentation tone is part of the work: evidence outperforms boasting (portfolio side in `graphic-designer` ch13).

## Common Mistakes
- Starting to generate before the request is restated and reduced to keywords.
- Saturating with the category only.
- Ending the session at saturation, with no break.
- Not capturing ideas immediately.
- Chasing "never seen before" instead of a fresh recombination for a defined audience.
- Treating agile delivery as an innovation system.

## Checklist
- [ ] Request restated; two or three precise keywords written
- [ ] Far-away material gathered through the keywords
- [ ] Incubation step scheduled with a return time
- [ ] Capture tool within reach
- [ ] Audience defined before judging what is new
- [ ] At least one idea imported from another field
- [ ] Problem-solving, decision and validation systems named for the team
