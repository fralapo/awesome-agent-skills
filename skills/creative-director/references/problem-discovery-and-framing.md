# Problem Discovery and Framing

Practitioner notes on the first phase of creative work: finding out what the problem really is before any visual or verbal idea is made. Extends "Frame the Problem" in `concept-and-insight-in-design-practice.md`. Translating the result into a look belongs to `art-director` (ch05); craft belongs to `graphic-designer`.

## Creative Kickoff: Three Things Before You Design
1. **Goal in one sentence**: what the project is and what it must do. More than one sentence waters down the solution.
2. **Diagnose the problem with three questions**:
   - **Who**: list the top three audiences, then choose the most important one.
   - **What**: the key message and the feeling the audience should have; if there are many messages, prioritise.
   - **Where and when**: the context and the stage of the buying cycle. Awareness needs a hook; ready-to-buy needs a clear call. Format shapes timing (a pre-roll ad must hook in about five seconds; a theatre piece can take its time).
3. **Narrow the exploration**: set creative parameters (what it can look and sound like) and ask how the client will decide (price, results, quality or rapport); use that criterion as the priority in the pitch.
Translate coded words (epic, elegant, energetic) into concrete examples and simple rules.

## Ask "Why Now" and Classify the Business Goal
- Business goals fall into revenue, repositioning (a new target market or a new product) and awareness. Ask why now before designing.
- Use specific language for business aims rather than "cool" or purely aesthetic arguments; make the creative process visible by breaking it into concrete, sequential steps.
- **Competitive audit in two directions**: vertical (direct competitors) and horizontal (unrelated industries solving a similar customer problem).
- **Customer research**: read reviews and forums, good and bad.
- **Empathy test**: would you advise the client to go elsewhere if that served them better? Empathy means acting in the client's interest even against your own, not only stepping into their shoes; ask a sequence of concrete questions until you can build for their frame.
- Do not assume the audience; ask and count answers (a form or survey whose keyword counts decide what to make next).

## Find the Root Cause
- **Problem tree**: visible effects are the branches, the problem is the trunk, causes are the roots. Dig down with repeated "why" until a root cause can be changed; the cheapest fix is often at the root. Fix the smaller obvious problem first, then ask whether a bigger systemic cause sits beneath it.
- **Three questions before accepting a stated solution** ("we need a new platform", "the site is not getting traffic because it is not pretty"): do we know this is true? Who else believes it and what does the research say? Who makes the final decision? Surface assumptions and mental models early; misaligned decision-makers are a red flag.
- **Reframe**: change the question to change the solution ("make the lift faster" versus "reduce the frustration of waiting" leads to different ideas). Solutions can be out of the box and need not mirror the problem.
- **Empathy grid** (three columns: task, gap, opportunity): describe a typical day of the audience, list their concerns, turn one pain into an opportunity written as a solution.
- **Watch people do the task** before designing the solution; stay close to the person and their story. Your intuition fails when users differ from you.
- Define the organisation by its purpose, not its product; ask what the audience gets done, not what is sold.

## Triage Under a Deadline
1. Ask why the date matters (board meeting, press, arbitrary choice). Do not accept an arbitrary date as fixed.
2. If the date is real, identify the primary audience.
3. New product for new users: prioritise a polished, focused first impression. New feature for existing users: deprioritise visual polish, they already trust the product.
4. Cut scope in a planned way, not feature after feature until the product no longer makes sense. Explain what the work involves to set realistic timelines.

## Choosing a Process Frame
| Frame | What it is | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| Design thinking | A mindset and toolbox: empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test, looping back; exercises chosen per project | Understanding users and framing the problem |
| Design sprint | A fixed step-by-step recipe with roles and timing that validates one idea and yields a prototype | A team with no time to design its own method; deciding whether to build |
| Agile sprint | An execution cycle building a validated product from a backlog | Building, not ideating |
Usual order: design thinking, then sprint, then agile. Use design thinking as the top-level philosophy and add a recipe per job. Use plain, "cold" process language with business stakeholders.

**Why such projects fail**: sold as a turnkey innovation system; workshops and posters that never become features (a persona on a wall is not a decision: every artefact must translate into features); one-off training with no knowledge transfer; top-down guideline documents never used; expectations of results in weeks. What works: a tailored facilitation team others can book, one or two pilot teams on an important product, a multi-year plan, principles taught so the team can adapt the steps.

## Concept Sketching (Group Method, About 60 Minutes)
1. Run short demos of how others solved the problem.
2. Draft on one sheet with a plain pen; the final concept on taped sheets with a catchy title, in marker, with words, annotated by sticky notes. Starting with a marker makes people finish too early.
3. Work individually and in silence, about 45 minutes, with time calls at intervals.
4. Hang concepts anonymously, then vote in a separate step. Anonymity removes bias toward the boss's idea. Ugly is fine; words matter more than drawings; the goal is combining what works, not originality.

## Facilitation Principles
- Challenge assumptions explicitly before solving; bring designers in at the start as collaborators.
- Let wild ideas survive brainstorming; treat ideas as priorities, not as good or bad.
- Replace "perfect" with a minimum viable version and iterate; show early iterations (logo work often takes 20 to 50 iterations).
- Inclusive means everyone is heard, not that everyone gets their way.

## Checklist
- [ ] Goal in one sentence; why now answered
- [ ] Who, what, where-when written; audience prioritised
- [ ] Client decision criterion known
- [ ] Stated solution tested with the three questions
- [ ] Root cause traced; assumptions listed
- [ ] Deadline reason and primary audience known
- [ ] Hand-off to the art director: concept in one sentence, audience, single feeling, filters
