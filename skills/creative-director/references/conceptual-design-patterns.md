# Conceptual Design Patterns

Practitioner notes on what makes a visual idea a concept, and how to reach one for covers, posters, logos and short films. Extends "Make It Memorable" in `concept-and-insight-in-design-practice.md` and the idea-generator role. Execution goes to `graphic-designer`; look and system to `art-director`.

## What a Concept Is
- A concept is a **hybrid third meaning** created when two elements combine into something neither says alone (A + B = C). The viewer solves it, and the solving is the reward.
- **A juxtaposition is not a concept**: a face plus a fruit is only a collage. If removing one element leaves the other meaning the same, there is no concept.
- Image plus caption is the basis of memes: each element alone fails.
- **Puzzle sweet spot**: too hard frustrates and viewers give up; too easy needs no effort. The right one makes the viewer smile in the mind. Test with an outsider who must say what the idea is.
- A surface gimmick is not a concept; check that a hybrid meaning exists.

## Formula Library
Recurring formulas (a pattern library; study award collections and analyse why each succeeds):
1. **Semiotic combination**: A + B produces meaning C.
2. **Hidden in plain sight**: a negative-space shape inside a logo, found after the first read.
3. **Two-reads image**: one image shows two things (a falling city that is also a symbol in its gaps).
4. **Copy inversion**: take a known phrase or cliché and flip it to suit the audience.
5. **Ambient or interactive**: use an existing environment or a physical action (a fold-out page) to carry the concept.
6. **Sequence concept (motion)**: find the moment where the meaning changes at the reveal (a dial that stops to show a single bullet).
7. **Hidden image / double exposure / silhouette**: study references for the conceptual device, not the look.
8. **Dual-image mark**: a symbol that reads as two things and holds both (see `graphic-designer` ch08 for the check).

## Concept-First Process (Covers and Similar Briefs)
1. **Read analytical sources** (critical essays, themes, conflicts), not only the plot summary. You cannot communicate what you have not understood; do not make up meaning.
2. **Reduce to one universal truth** or two or three words; one way to start is a universal conflict (person against nature, against another person, against time, against themselves).
3. **Find the nuanced angle** for this particular piece.
4. **Sketch the idea, then 3 to 5 ways of showing it.** Avoid literal illustration of a character; show the conflict (two hands about to separate). Negative shapes can tell a second story.
5. **Gather references for the device** (double exposure, silhouette, hidden image) and photo material (shoot your own if needed); cut out and push contrast.
6. **Only then design the system** (type, grid, colour) around the idea. Starting from decorative patterns or botanicals and hoping meaning will emerge is the commonest failure.
7. **Test with an outsider**: they must say what the idea is.
For type-only covers the typography is the expression: each item gets its own type character and a shared link (colour, grid). Heavy high-contrast images hold attention at thumbnail size; hand-made texture signals human care; the front cover is the hero.
Brief constraints (few colours, no photography as the core, mandatory logo and barcode, finishes and materials) are part of the idea space.

## Logo and Symbol Concepts
Concept for a mark starts with a broad unfiltered list of metaphors, then decomposition and combination (procedure in `graphic-designer` ch08). A logo should work as an icon, not as an illustration.

## Poster and Key-art Concepts
- Reduce the product to its core and let a single mark or image carry the poster (restraint beats spectacle).
- When the brand mark exists inside the story world, advertising and in-world product reinforce each other.
- Hierarchy by revelation: a small line that needs leaning in to read creates engagement.
- An ambiguous image raises a question that holds attention.

## Quality Gates for a Visual Concept
- Is there a third meaning, and can an outsider state it?
- Does the puzzle sit in the sweet spot?
- Is meaning traceable to the content, not invented?
- Does it survive the format (thumbnail, shelf, small size)?
- Does form support it (a clever idea does not rescue weak form)?

## Common Mistakes
- Calling a collage a concept.
- Describing plot features instead of theme.
- Breaking a word into parts that read as two different words or none.
- Puzzles so complex that viewers give up.
- Choosing style before the idea.
