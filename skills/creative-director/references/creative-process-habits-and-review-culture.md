# Creative Process Habits and Review Culture

Practitioner notes on how creative people keep a repeatable process, set direction, and run reviews. Complements the visionary role ("Sustaining a Creative Practice") and the creative-leader role. Visual-side critique is in `graphic-designer` ch07 and `art-director` ch04.

## Build a Personal Creative Formula
A repeatable process gives predictable results and confidence. Discover yours by answering in writing:
- Where do I feel most creative?
- How do I generate ideas?
- How do I judge good from bad?
- What triggers me (a routine, a playlist, a place)?
- What conditions were present in my last flow state? (Total absorption, merged action and awareness, altered sense of time, loss of self-consciousness.)
Then:
- **Pre-performance routine**: a fixed sequence before each task; notice that performance drops when it is interrupted, and plan for that.
- **Positive knowing over positive thinking**: trust a process you have tested.
- **Explain it to someone**: teaching your process, and explaining the reasons behind a critique, forces you to name your criteria; teaching can also restore confidence when stuck.
- A formula is not anti-creative; rejecting any formula is a common error.

## Make First, Wait Never
- Ideas come from doing the work and talking about the problem, not from waiting for inspiration or collecting references. Start by researching and making; the idea appears in the process.
- **Explain the problem out loud** even before you have an idea; putting it into words often surfaces one.
- Look at the brief or material until it tells you what it wants to be, then step back and let it lead.
- Watch for serendipity: accidents and unexpected connections are worth following.
- Do not bring a favourite style to every problem.
- **Take the next indicated action**: break work into small steps and review one dimension per pass (one for letter spacing, another for colour). Do not multitask attention across many aspects at once.
- Never arrive at a pitch with nothing to show and hope an idea will come in the room.
- Search engines and image libraries can act as a creative partner when used iteratively (loop in `art-director` ch08).

## Protect Thinking Capacity
- Fires-first days block innovation; keep bandwidth free for thinking.
- Schedule protected unplugged time; idle time lets new connections form.
- Plan in short cycles: twelve one-week projects rather than one twelve-week plan, adjusting as you go.
- Study organisations that failed to reinvent themselves and ask what they did for their audience, not what they sold; do not use the past as the compass.
- Try to disprove your own idea as early as possible. Master one thing before expanding; ambition should follow mastery. Judging "bad versus great" is easier than "good versus great".

## Direction Over Speed
A goal is a dream with a plan; direction matters more than hustle, because effort in the wrong direction only goes further from the target (a small angular error over a long distance becomes a large miss).
- **Vision casting**: picture the future state in vivid detail, then put it into words; check whether you believe it (hesitation in how you state it shows doubt).
- Plan down the time scale: years, quarters, months, weeks, days.
- Include at least one stretch that changes how you work, not only what you produce.
- State goals publicly and report on a fixed rhythm; people often keep promises to others better than to themselves.
- Do not credit planning for results that came from luck; do not leave big dreams without dated actions.

## Strategy Ladder and Written Principles (Design Leadership)
- **Strategy ladder**: mission, then company-level strategy, then criteria of success, then proposals judged against those criteria. A proposal is accepted or rejected by the criteria, not by taste or seniority.
- **Write principles down** so "amazing" means the same thing to everyone.
- Give designers full ownership across the whole product, not a narrow slice; a team needs range (visual, interaction, research, concept), generalists and specialists alike.
- A design leader's time: about half on organisational health (hiring, one-on-ones, growth), half on product direction and critique.
- Intuition fails as the audience grows or differs from you: test with the people actually using the work and keep unlearning.
- To build confidence in sharing opinions, publish regularly.
- Do not assume that people who like early-stage work cannot do detailed execution. New managers need time to feel confident.

## Review Culture
- Hold a regular review where open questions are aired face to face so nothing stays unresolved.
- Leave ego at the door; disagree without being disagreeable.
- Reason first about usability (can users find the main action?), then business goals; test with real users and compare metrics (conversion, bounce) with the old version.
- Patterns must stay consistent (headings, body, sizes); expressive layouts that break patterns hurt function. Check type hierarchy: if a level is missing, add a weight or contrast step rather than relying on scale alone.
- Colour pairings with poor legibility (white on red, white on blue, red-yellow-black combinations) fail even when strong.
- Define quality as shared criteria: a short list of bad-to-good parameters, or about five checks a piece must pass (see `graphic-designer` ch07).
- A designer may do the part they like (the interface) even when they dislike the overall look; assignment is not a verdict.
- Ask for grading criteria and constraints up front.

## Checklist
- [ ] Personal formula written and tested
- [ ] Next indicated action defined; one dimension per pass
- [ ] Protected unplugged time scheduled
- [ ] Direction stated before speed; dated plan
- [ ] Success criteria written before proposals are judged
- [ ] Review rituals with defined criteria, not taste
