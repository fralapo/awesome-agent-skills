# Concept Generation Methods

Practitioner notes on turning a framed problem into concepts with named, repeatable methods, and on the habits that keep ideas coming. Extends "Generate Ideas" in `concept-and-insight-in-design-practice.md` and the formula library in `conceptual-design-patterns.md`; the framing step comes first (`problem-discovery-and-framing.md`). The visual direction that follows a chosen concept belongs to `art-director`; construction to `graphic-designer`.

## Choose the Method by Stage
| Situation | Method | Level of effort |
|---|---|---|
| Existing design or brief with visible faults | Problem sprint | low to mid |
| A strong competitor owns the category | Reverse engineering a competitor | mid |
| Brief is a few words and nothing comes | Word association or keyword boxes | low |
| The idea must fit how people actually meet the piece | User journey mapping | mid to high |
| The problem is clear but solutions are obvious and safe | Reverse brainstorming, metaphor, pattern collision | mid |
| Team stuck or ideas feel borrowed | Constraint exercises, change of context, 3-3-3 feedback | low |
Mind mapping is useful for laying out a topic but is the weakest of the methods listed for generating a concept; use it to organise, not to decide.

## Problem Sprint
1. List every problem in the brief or in the existing design (too detailed, generic, dated, lost at small sizes, unclear message).
2. Write a fix for each problem. Each fix is a candidate concept.
3. Example: a dated logo that was detailed, app-icon-like and generic became black and white, typographic and simple, because the problems were "lost at small size" and "generic".
4. Keep fixes that map to the goals of the brief; discard fixes that only improve taste.

## Reverse Engineering a Competitor
1. **Study** the identity: logo, visual elements, palette, type, principles, position, audience.
2. **Deconstruct**: symbolism, shape psychology, proportions, style, taglines.
3. **Analyse positioning**: strengths, weaknesses, how the audience perceives them (for example premium), and the opportunity that leaves (mid-range, warmer, more playful).
4. **Build a differentiated identity** that uses the gap (against a minimal black-and-white serif brand: a modern sans with an electric accent on black).
Mistake: competing head-on in the leader's own style instead of using its weakness.

## Word Association and Keyword Boxes
- Write words for several topics (the product or franchise, the genre, the launch psychology, key features, the audience). Starting words must relate to the brief and the audience.
- **Combine across topics** into concepts: for a game launch, "immersive, excitement, battle" and "countdown, online, gameplay" suggest different pieces.
- **Keyword merge**: write keywords around client, project and brief, then merge pairs into one concept (a wasp plus the military suggested a geometric drone-company symbol). More relevant keywords written down means better odds of an original concept.
- The same boxes (target market, brand, brief requirements) feed the moodboard in `art-director` ch12.

## User Journey Mapping
1. List the stages the person goes through with the piece (discovery, interest or engagement, decision or action).
2. For each stage write the action and the emotional state (notices a cover among others, curious; reads headlines and images, captivated; weighs value, eager).
3. Tailor the concept to each stage (a striking subject for discovery, a bold headline for interest, vibrant colour and a clear next step for decision).
4. Seeing the work from the interacting person's position produces concepts tied to real use.

## Reverse Brainstorming and Tensions
- Brainstorm ways to cause the problem (confuse, frustrate, overwhelm the user), then design the opposite. It makes weaknesses visible and yields user-friendly solutions.
- State the problem as a **tension** ("modern yet traditional", "for young and old", "order versus chaos"); a tension suggests form directly, and in a brand it can serve as its DNA.
- Every idea still has to address the goals set at the start; discard ideas that map to none.

## Metaphor, Quote and Cross-Sensory Concepts
- **Visual metaphor** connects two unrelated ideas into one image (soap bars forming a brain) and works across languages. Use it only if the brief benefits.
- **Quote to image**: choose a quotation that carries the message and translate it into a metaphor with shock and memorability (a human brain for "safety happens between your ears"). A strong focal point with plenty of negative space carries a safety message.
- **Read brief keywords for opportunities**: "local" suggests local artists; a hot sauce can show exaggeration as the narrative.
- **Cross-sensory cues**: colour for taste, type for aroma, sound in motion. Gather textures, sounds and references as emotional cues, not literal elements; find the patterns and tensions, then build.
- **Niche-language concept**: for a specialist audience use their own term or joke (a "bull market" cover that literally shows a bull in a field works only for people who know the phrase). A concept that speaks to everyone often speaks to no one.

## Visual Storytelling Concepts
- Three principles: clarity and simplicity, consistency (colour, fonts, imagery), emotion (mood through colour, imagery, type).
- Three-phase structure: **setup**, **confrontation** (the problem), **resolution** (the product or mark appears as the answer, often at the end or bottom). Humour variants work as well.
- Understand the narrative before choosing the typeface; type has a voice, and a traditional serif beside a bold modern image can express "sophisticated meets low-brow".
- Not every brief needs a narrative; ask whether it helps the goal.

## Pattern Collision and Constraints
- Creativity comes from improbable combinations; collide two unrelated worlds (architecture, film, fashion, packaging, signage, raw human emotion) with the brief.
- Broaden inputs beyond design sites and trend feeds, about 15 to 20 minutes a day, otherwise the work looks like everyone else's.
- **Constraint exercises**: one typeface only; black and white only; one shape language; no gradients.
- **Idea archive**: save every composition that makes you feel something, with a note on why.
- **Inspiration trap**: one favourite solution (a single typeface, one look) used on every job regardless of client or audience. Rotate the method, not only the surface.

## Habits That Feed the Method
- Work where you are comfortable; when stuck change location, move, or go to pen and paper. Keep a notepad within reach (bedside, shower) and always turn captured ideas into something.
- Energy produces ideas: sleep, exercise and diet affect output; schedule downtime and stop late-night forcing. Thirty to sixty minutes of descriptive fiction can warm up imaginative thinking.
- Set a personal deadline earlier than the real one; rushing rarely produces the best work, and fear of failing blocks output. Treat failures as stepping stones.
- **3-3-3 feedback**: ask someone for three things they like, three they dislike and three things it reminds them of; the third list reveals unintended associations.
- Generated imagery is useful in ideation and exploration but drifts in proportion and style and invents odd logic; direction, selection and refinement stay human, and the vision must exist before generating.

## Common Mistakes
- Using mind mapping as the only method and stopping at the first cluster.
- Starting words unrelated to the brief or audience.
- Skipping the goal check, so charming ideas survive that solve nothing.
- Competing in the leader's style.
- Reusing last project's solution.
- Concept and execution produced at the same instant with no sketching (see `concept-and-insight-in-design-practice.md`).

## Hand-off
Give the art director the chosen concept in one sentence, the audience, the single feeling it should produce, the tension it resolves and the strategic filters (`art-director` ch05, ch12). Ask for the direction, not for fonts and colours.
