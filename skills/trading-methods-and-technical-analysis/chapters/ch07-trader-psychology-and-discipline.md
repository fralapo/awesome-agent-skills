# Chapter 07: Trader Psychology and Discipline

> Educational concepts, not investment, trading or psychological advice.

## Core Idea
In this model, most behavioural failures in trading are **predictable biases acting on a poorly sized position**. The trader who panics was usually over-exposed; the trader who holds a loser hoping is protecting self-image; the trader who closes winners early is avoiding regret. The remedies offered are structural (written rules, fixed sessions, size small enough to follow them) rather than willpower. The general theory of these biases sits in `investing-and-financial-history` ch08; this chapter covers how they show up in active trading.

## 7.1 Cut losses, let profits run
- **Thesis**: the most repeated rule in trading is the one most often broken.
- **Mechanism**: the typical error is the reverse pattern: many small gains taken quickly and one large loss held (the disposition effect, general knowledge). It produces a high hit rate with negative expectancy (6.1). Markets, in a recurring saying, transfer money from the impatient to the patient. Avoided losses are part of returns: a loss on a sensible trade is not a mistake, it is the cost of the method, and losses are described as the best teacher.
- **Trade-off**: letting profits run means giving back open gains on reversals.
- **Does not hold when**: the method is explicitly mean-reverting with fixed targets (ch05, 5.3).

## 7.2 Panic as a sizing signal
- **Thesis**: if a position causes panic, the exposure was too large: a money-management error, not an analysis error.
- **Mechanism**: stops too large for the account damage decision-making; on very small accounts a normal drawdown leads to abandoning a system at its worst moment; high leverage on one idea produces the overconfidence that skips the stop. A practitioner's own reported error: holding a short with a small remaining potential gain while risking several times that amount, against the rule.
- **Does not hold when**: fear reflects new information that invalidates the trade; then the exit rule, not the emotion, applies.

## 7.3 A rigid plan
- **Thesis**: fixed rules reduce the number of decisions taken under stress.
- **Mechanism**: fixed trading hours; a maximum daily loss after which the session ends; a written entry, stop, target and size before every order; no new positions before major events. Practices proposed by experienced traders: treat a demo account as if it were real; start with a sum whose loss creates no problem; do not increase size just because things are going well; do not hold a losing position hoping; do not leave a job too early. High leverage is compared with taking a roundabout with the handbrake on.
- **Trade-off**: rigidity misses discretionary opportunities.
- **Does not hold when**: the plan itself is untested (ch08); discipline then executes an error consistently.

## 7.4 Honesty before the fact (hindsight)
- **Thesis**: a signal that looks clear afterwards may not have been clear in real time.
- **Mechanism**: charts reviewed after the move show the turning point; at the time, the right edge of the chart is always ambiguous (hindsight bias, general knowledge). Practitioners describe overconfidence and missing stops after costly errors. One exercise: look at a chart without knowing the instrument or the date, to separate reading from story. Another: write the scenario and its invalidation before the open.
- **Does not hold when**: the review records only the trades that were taken, not the ones that were considered and skipped.

## 7.5 Judging decisions, not outcomes
- **Thesis**: a good trade can lose and a bad trade can win.
- **Mechanism**: errors are analysed independently of the result; a fixed break-even rule is tested on 50-200 trades before being judged; a "sum of conditions" rule enters only when several factors agree and exits at the first contrary sign. Trading is described as "the only activity where you can bet on a horse after the race has started", which tempts late entries after moves are visible.
- **Does not hold when**: sample sizes are too small to separate skill from luck (ch08, 8.2).

## 7.6 Social proof, crowds and promises
- **Thesis**: confidence borrowed from others is not evidence.
- **Mechanism**: claims of huge returns from automated systems or signal sellers, copying other traders, and repeating "I am a winning trader" as self-suggestion are all mentioned in the material. In this model: distrust anyone who promises gains or collects other people's money; study trading out of interest, not to replace an income quickly. Social and copy trading are dismissed by some practitioners outright.
- **Does not hold when**: a track record is long, audited and net of costs (rare; `investing-and-financial-history` ch02, 2.7).

## 7.7 Short-term trading and stress
- **Thesis**: shorter horizons concentrate psychological load.
- **Mechanism**: scalping is described as mostly psychology; short timeframes multiply decisions, false signals and costs. Some limit intraday trading to a small share of time and capital; others keep it only for fun. Position trading over days to weeks is described as where "the real money is made", a position, not a measured fact.
- **Does not hold when**: the trader's schedule or temperament fits short horizons and costs are low.

## 7.8 Practical routine (educational)
1. Before the session: higher-timeframe context, scheduled events, maximum daily loss.
2. Before each order: invalidation level, target, reward-to-risk, size from risk.
3. During: no stop moved away from price; no averaging down.
4. After: journal the decision and the reason, not only the outcome; review in batches.
5. When rules are broken twice in a row: stop for the day.

## Common mistakes
- Blaming the analysis for a sizing problem.
- Increasing size after a winning streak.
- Reviewing only finished trades, with hindsight.
- Using discipline to execute an untested rule.
