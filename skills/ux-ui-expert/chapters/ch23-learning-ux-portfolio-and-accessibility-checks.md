# Chapter 23: Learning UX, Portfolio Case Studies and First Accessibility Checks

## Core Idea
The UX/UI designer role owns screens, flows, interaction and usability evidence. This chapter covers how to build competence and proof of it (self-initiated projects, case studies), plus quick accessibility checks that precede formal testing. Concept belongs to `creative-director`, visual direction to `art-director`, and print/poster/logo craft to `graphic-designer`; this role adapts their direction to interfaces and reports back when it fails on screen.

## Learning by Doing
- **Informational conversations**: talk to practising designers to learn required skills and the hiring process, not to ask for a job. A good outreach message is brief, personal, mentions a shared connection, shows genuine interest in the person's path and asks for 15 to 30 minutes. Relationships compound over a career.
- **Reverse-engineer the goal**: study strong UX portfolios before building your own so you know the outcome you are working toward.
- **Self-initiated project**: start from a real frustration with an existing product, validate it by interviewing users or reading forums, then redesign and prototype it. Test the prototype with users and iterate; a project with a feedback loop convinces more than a one-shot redesign.
- **Time plan**: one to three months with milestones (research in month one, design and prototype in month two, testing and iteration in month three). Quality over quantity.
- **Tools matter less than design thinking**: pick one and move on; you will learn several anyway.
- **Personal library**: platform design guidelines for mobile, collections of real product flows for competitive analysis, template libraries to speed up interface work.
- **Publish consistently**: work and process in public create feedback and opportunities; a first role may take six months to a year.

## A Strong Case Study Contains
User research · a validated problem statement · a clear statement of your own contribution · the reasoning behind design decisions · limitations and challenges · what went wrong and what went right · user testing or surveys · business impact.

## First Accessibility Checks
- Simulate impairment (blur, vignette, low contrast) as a first pass, then validate with real users; imagination is not evidence.
- For low-vision readers, desaturate very bright colours and pick out forms with high-contrast outlines; both fully saturated images and full black-and-white conversions performed worse than this middle ground.
- Research instruments for such audiences: large recommended typefaces, high-contrast colours, and answers given by marking a line on a 1-to-5 scale rather than precise circling.
- Numeric thresholds (contrast ratios, sizes) are in ch21 and ch22.

## Working With the Other Design Roles
- Receive the direction as written criteria (feeling, rules, references) and adapt it to components, states and breakpoints.
- Report back when a direction fails on screen (contrast, density, touch targets) with evidence, instead of silently changing the look.
- Do not redefine the concept or the brand identity because a screen is easier that way; raise it.
- Zoom between micro (type, spacing, states) and macro (flows across mobile, desktop and the wider brand message); run a pre-mortem on contrast, small screens and different backgrounds.
- Hand over files a stranger can continue: named layers, grouped by function, a readme with fonts, colour tokens and export instructions.
