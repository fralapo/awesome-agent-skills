# Chapter 30: Prototyping Early and Design Sprints

## Core Idea
Fake it before you make it: prototype and test before building. Building too soon costs scope cuts, rework and a false sense of progress. A time-boxed design sprint is a form of user research: its valuable output is what real users teach you, not the prototype. Extends ch14 (sketch several directions) and ch13 (cost multiplier).

## Symptoms of Building Too Soon
- Product: scope creep, then scope reduction.
- Team: demotivation, an assembly-line feel.
- Users: weak growth, support complaints about confusion.
Failures cited as too feature-heavy with no clear use case. Debating button colours before research is the same error in small.

## Four-Phase Prototyping
1. **Research**.
2. **Synthesize**: journey map, storyboard, user flow (ch16).
3. **Create** a prototype at the right fidelity.
4. **Test**.

**Storyboard**: focus on people and moments, not screens; mark where the product enters; note constraints (sunlight contrast, battery, connectivity). Keep it as the reference in feature debates: a feature that serves no storyboard step is cut.

**Fidelity matches audience**: usability testing needs real text and imagery; an investor pitch needs polish; an internal team needs low fidelity. Sketches are communication tools, not art, and can be tested. Avoid heavy animation early; it is hard to implement. Keep a fixed feature list and time-box the prototype.

**Fake the back end** with existing services (page builder, forms, automation, payments, e-signature) before writing software. Do not write software to validate demand. Involve developers, content and marketing in parallel.

## Cost Ladder (two framings)
One practitioner estimate: a change costs about 2 hours at wireframe, about 10x at design, about 20x at development. ch13 cites about 100x after development. Both agree on direction; treat the numbers as illustrations and use your own team's data for a business case. Build when two answers are yes: will the audience sign up, and will they pay.

## Design Sprint as a Research Vehicle
- Prototype tested with real users by the end of the week makes research happen even in a team that doubts it ("Trojan horse").
- Start from assumptions and let tests challenge them; do not impose one golden process.
- Tests are qualitative, about five participants per round, to pick a direction rather than prove a fact.
- Get the prototype close to a real product so answers are realistic.

**Pre-sprint intake (each client participant)**: top two goals (validate an idea, redesign, raise engagement or activation, business model, shared vision); what happens after the sprint (funding, handoff, development); a long-term sentence ("in two years our product will ..."); target customer (B2B or B2C, age, mobile or desktop); main competitors; one to three inspiring products; directions to explore; prior documents. Then a decision-maker call to go deeper, and "how might we" questions for the expert-interview section.

**Test day**: primer questions on current behaviour; a script; positive and negative notes in two colours; closing questions (liked, disliked, what to change, how disappointed without it); a rating; then yes/no per participant against the sprint's key questions.

## Research Before a Sprint
Prior research helps only if a skilled researcher gathers it, presents it credibly and the team believes it. Deliver it as a short executive summary (customer journey, empathy map, sprint questions), not a long PDF or wall of slides, which loses credibility. Existing products are free prototypes to study before building. Multi-month research phases that paralyse a team, and research used as a checkbox before going with hunches anyway, are both failure modes; a prototype tests hunches faster. Be helpful rather than prescriptive: do not make colleagues feel guilty for skipping a method, and take the team's enthusiasm for its solution as the start of a faster way to learn.

## Recruiting for Tests
Recruit outside your network (ch27). Record sessions as evidence.

## Mistakes
- Treating a sprint as skipping research.
- Asking about concepts users have not seen.
- Over-detailed prototypes for internal communication.
- Claiming pre-launch tests give final answers.

## Checklist
- [ ] Storyboard exists and gates feature debates.
- [ ] Fidelity matches the audience.
- [ ] Back end faked with existing services.
- [ ] Key questions written before test day.
- [ ] Five participants outside the team's network.
