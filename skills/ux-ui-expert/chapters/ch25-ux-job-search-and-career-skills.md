# Chapter 25: The UX Job Search and the Skills That Decide Careers

## Core Idea
Run the job search like a UX project: research yourself, set explicit criteria, source proactively, target each application and track everything in one place. Beyond craft, advancement depends on communication, typography literacy, facilitation, dependability and improvisation, which are routinely neglected. Evidence limits: the source material covers search strategy, role definitions and soft skills; it gives no guidance on resume formatting, applicant-tracking filters, interview answers or hiring-side scoring, so none is offered here beyond mirroring the job description and preparing questions.

## Role Landscape
- Define UX by what employers actually ask for, not by academic definitions, which are too broad to guide learning or hiring.
- Large organisations split the field into specialists: UX engineer, visual designer, motion or interaction designer, UX researcher, content strategist, UX writer.
- The generalist role is increasingly titled **product designer**: owns flows and app design, prototypes, tests and iterates with engineers and data. Research is becoming a separate role that product designers use rather than run alone.
- A single post that combines UX, UI, research and SEO signals a less mature design practice; read it as a scope risk, not an opportunity to do everything.
- Map your strengths honestly; nobody does every specialism well. UX is useful, usable and desirable (the three-criteria check), and daily work spans wireframing, field research, translating between users and developers, testing, documenting and facilitating.

## The 5-Step Search
1. **Understand needs**: picture the role about two years ahead; write a "like and loathe" list from past jobs.
2. **Criteria**: a written filter covering company size, team size, industry, culture, location and remote work, travel, growth, salary.
3. **Work the sources**: company sites, referrals from your network, groups where roles surface before posting; start real conversations with people at target companies (ch23 informational interviews).
4. **Study job descriptions**: one document per role, copy the standout bullets, mirror them in resume and portfolio (ch24), and derive sharper interview questions.
5. **Project-manage**: one tracker (any tool) with applications, last contact and follow-up dates.

## Principles
- Be proactive; an "open to work" flag alone waits for nothing.
- Apply only where criteria match; fewer decisions, more energy per application.
- Interviews are two-way: arrive with questions drawn from your criteria.
- Build the relationship before asking for a hire; cold outreach that opens with a request fails.

## Skills Most Juniors Neglect
| Skill | Practice |
|---|---|
| Client language | Drop design jargon; state the business value of each decision. Never blame the client for not understanding design. |
| Typography | It covers most of a UI screen; learn its rules (ch21) before polishing shadows or palettes. |
| Facilitation | Run a short structured workshop, repeat it, and ask a colleague for feedback after each run (ch29). |
| Dependability | Take notes, confirm commitments. Reliable peers get the interesting work over late, unchecked talent. |
| Improvisation | When asked something you cannot answer, connect what you know to the client's need instead of stopping at "I don't know". |

## Common Mistakes
- Mass-applying to any title containing "UX" or "design".
- Notes scattered across phone and documents, so follow-ups are missed.
- Assuming the job means everything.
- Relying on memory for commitments.

## Checklist
- [ ] Like/loathe list and written criteria exist.
- [ ] At least three channels besides job boards are in use.
- [ ] One tracker with next-action dates.
- [ ] Each application mirrors that role's description.
- [ ] Two to three interview questions per target, drawn from criteria.
