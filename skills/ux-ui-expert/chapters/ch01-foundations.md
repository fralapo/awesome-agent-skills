# Chapter 1: Foundations — What UX Research Is, Why It Matters, Who Does It

## Core Idea
UX research is not a job title or a set of methods (usability testing, A/B testing) — it's the systematic study of people's behaviors, needs, and motivations to guide design and innovation, following the scientific method but aimed at business outcomes, not pure knowledge.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Scientific Method → UX Research Process mapping**: research question → background research (lit review) → hypothesis → objective → procedure/methodology → data collection → analysis → results. UX research is the same process, but oriented to business goals and actionable recommendations, not just knowledge.
  - When to use: framing UX research's legitimacy to skeptical stakeholders or new researchers.
- **Problem Spotters vs. Problem Solvers**: usability/evaluative research fixes existing ideas; strategic/generative research discovers new problems and opportunity spaces ("faster horses" — the classic Henry Ford anecdote).
  - When to use: justify foundational/generative research budget, not just fixit testing.

## Key Concepts
- **UX Research**: study of people's behaviors, needs, and motivations through systematic methods to guide design/innovation.
- **Research is the Keystone**: interlocks psychology, business strategy, leadership, statistics, ethics, and design across the product lifecycle.
- **Stakeholder**: anyone with a vested interest in a product/initiative/project.
- **Tolerance for ambiguity**: the single most important trait of a UX researcher — comfort not knowing an answer yet, because research will find out.

## Mental Models
- Think of UX research as "holding the flashlight" — illuminating direction for the team, not dictating the path.
- Use "we test our products with users" framing, never "we test users" — how you phrase research to stakeholders shapes their buy-in.
- Treat title/terminology debates (UX research vs. CX vs. human factors) as irrelevant — the substance (understanding people to unlock opportunity) is what matters.

## Anti-patterns
- **Defining UX research by its methods**: reduces it to "wireframes," "A/B testing," or "asking people what they want" — misses that it's a way of thinking and problem-solving.
- **Only doing evaluative research**: teams that only fix existing designs never discover new opportunity spaces (the classic Juicero failure — good usability, no real problem solved).
- **Ignoring context (the MRI-machine example)**: band-aid fixes (bribing scared children with candy) vs. real research-driven redesign (turning the machine into a themed "adventure") — surface fixes miss root causes discoverable only through empathy-driven research.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define UX research by outcome (understanding people to unlock product/business opportunity), not by method.
2. Balance evaluative/tactical research with foundational/strategic research — usability wins mean nothing if the underlying problem is wrong (Juicero).
3. Any professional background can become a UX researcher; the qualities that matter are curiosity, humility, tolerance for ambiguity, and storytelling/communication skill.
4. Frame research narratively to stakeholders as "we test our product with users," never "we test the users."
