# Chapter 28: Personas, Archetypes and Mental Modeling

## Core Idea
Before designing, answer who the design is for and how they expect the system to work. Personas and archetypes are decision tools only when built from research and framed around behaviour and motivation. Mental modeling is a discovery practice that turns expectations into tasks, gaps and opportunities before any solution exists.

## Personas
- **Content, in order**: behaviours, attitudes, goals, needs, habits, other tools used, and a holistic view of the person's life. Add a photo and a name plus a memorable descriptor so the team can recall it ("busy executive").
- **Rules**: never build from assumptions; check against what real people said. Demographics alone (age, income, family size) do not guide design decisions; marketing-style personas fail here.
- **Use as a gut check**: does this feature map to this person's needs and goals? If not, do not build it.
- **Count**: 3 to 5 archetypes; with 8 to 10, cluster them. Include a first-time visitor, not only returning customers.
- **A persona nobody embodies is wasted**: the team must use it in meetings.
- A quick signal: within minutes of talking to a client you can tell whether they know their customers; if not, research is the first deliverable.

## First Questions for Any Project
1. What should make users feel good? (a guess, refined by discovery)
2. Who are we designing for? Build an archetype first; the same puzzle differs entirely for a child and an adult.
3. What is the user's goal and why?
4. What tools does the user need (special interfaces, audio, accessibility)?
5. What is at stake if they fail? Higher stakes mean deeper testing.
6. What variables can we control?
**Output/input model**: you control the output across visuals, sound, smell, taste, touch and overall message; you do not control the input, because the user filters it through bias, ability and comprehension. The result is the message received plus an emotional response. Never assume the intended message is the perceived one.

## Mental Modeling
Position: between research and definition in the Double Diamond (ch14). Progression: mental models, conceptual model, interaction models, wireframes.

**Six components to check**: affordances (how it appears to work), simplicity (grasped at a glance), familiarity (seen before), availability (cognitive load to understand and complete), flexibility (more than one way, with a way back), feedback (confirmation an action happened).

**Workshop (about 1.5-2 hours, sticky notes, about five minutes per column)**:
1. **Tasks**: a day-in-the-life timeline (morning, afternoon, evening, miscellaneous).
2. **Gaps**: challenges and pain points on the same timeline.
3. **Opportunities**: possible solutions; the only stage where solutions are invited.
4. **Affinity mapping**: group similar notes and look for patterns.
5. **Clusters to features** (the umbrella), then use cases (specific instances).
Prioritise features with a short/medium/long-term matrix (ch29).

## Rules
- The exercise supplements user interviews; it does not replace them.
- Map to the client's goal, not one-to-one to existing screens.
- Hold back solutions until the opportunities column.
- Too many options raise error rates and choice overload (ch20).

## Mistakes
- Jumping to solutions in the task or gap stage.
- Personas made without research, or kept in a PDF.
- Skipping the archetype and designing from one's own habits.

## Checklist
- [ ] Each persona traces to interview or data evidence.
- [ ] Behaviour and goals outweigh demographics.
- [ ] Persona count is 5 or fewer and each has a short label.
- [ ] Six mental-model components reviewed.
- [ ] Workshop outputs checked against real user input.
