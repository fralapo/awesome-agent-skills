# Chapter 24: The UX Portfolio and Case-Study Narrative

## Core Idea
A portfolio is a product: it has a strategy (who reads it, what they decide), research (what the job asks for), writing (the story of each project), design and distribution. The reader is deciding whether you can think, not whether you can decorate. A clear story of the work beats format and visual polish. This extends ch23, which lists what a case study contains; this chapter covers how to assemble, order and test it.

## Principles
- **Narrative before layout**: write each case study as text first (problem, process, decisions, results), then design the slides or pages around it.
- **Show the thinking, not only the deliverables**: screenshots alone are not evidence of reasoning. Pair each artifact with the decision it served.
- **Depth beats volume**: one well-told project can outperform five deliverable-heavy ones.
- **Claim, then proof**: state the outcome or value first, then show the work. Work shown before the message undercuts it, and leading with output instead of value hides the skill.
- **Map to the job**: the reader wants to see the skills the role requests; every claim in the portfolio should be backed by a case.
- **Timeless over trendy**: clarity, context, process and relevance last; template tricks and tools that promise results in days do not.
- **One artifact, two uses**: the same portfolio supports applications and the interview walk-through.

## Format Choice
| Option | Fits when | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| Presentation deck shared by link | Time is short; a link is requested; you will present it live | Needs a self-explanatory narrative when read without you |
| Website | Time allows, or the role is web-facing | A requirement for a "link" does not mean a website is mandatory |
| Printed binder / screenshot dump | Rarely | No narrative, no process |

## Case-Study Skeleton
1. **Intro block**: client or context, role, services or categories, timeframe.
2. **Challenge**: the validated problem and why it mattered (ch23: research-backed).
3. **Process and decisions**: options considered, why one was chosen, what the user evidence said.
4. **Solution**: the result, shown at the level of detail the reader needs.
5. **Outcome**: measured impact where it exists (ch10, ch13), plus limitations, what went wrong and what you would change.
6. **Your contribution**: what you did versus the team.

Break long copy into headline, subhead, callouts and short body blocks placed beside the image they describe. Give more than one route to the same content (a module link and a navigation link). Use only real clients and real logos as proof; invented proof is exposed in due diligence.

## Refinements
- **Fewer, polished projects**: remove any project you are not sure is excellent; keep improving until it is.
- **Relevance filter per application**: show only work that matches the target role and leave out unrelated side work (a local event logo in an industrial-design portfolio). Include personal projects when they show initiative.
- **Screen first**: design for the display and use its full area rather than a print-magazine layout.
- **Page one states who you are and what you want**, with some personality (interests, hobbies); teams value collaborators. Then projects, then contact and social links. A standard CV template says nothing about design.
- **No self-rated skill scores** (star ratings for software, "3/5"): subjective and meaningless. A degree grade is not a signal of design quality either.
- **Tailor the cover letter and portfolio** to the company's actual work and the role; proofread spelling, grammar and capitalisation before sending.

## Procedure
1. Collect three to five job descriptions for the role you want; list the skills they repeat.
2. Pick the projects that demonstrate those skills with the strongest evidence of process.
3. Write each narrative in plain text and cut anything that does not change a decision.
4. Build the deck or site; keep a simple grid and no overlap of type and images.
5. Step away for a period, then review with fresh eyes for errors and unsupported claims.
6. Walk someone through it aloud; if the story needs you in the room, rewrite.

## Common Mistakes
- Decoration or personality in place of evidence of skill.
- A binder or gallery with no problem statement.
- Hover-only information with no touch equivalent on mobile.
- Big unedited blocks of copy.
- Treating the format requirement as the goal and neglecting the story.

## Checklist
- [ ] Each case opens with the problem and your role.
- [ ] Every skill the target job names is backed by a case.
- [ ] Decisions are explained, not only shown.
- [ ] Outcome and limitations are stated.
- [ ] Reviewed after a break; reads without the author present.
