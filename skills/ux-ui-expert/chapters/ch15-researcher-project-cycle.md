# Chapter 15: The UX Researcher's Project Cycle and Evaluation

## Core Idea
UX research exists to replace subjective, biased assumptions about users (typically held by whoever built a feature) with objective, evidence-based understanding, and it follows a repeatable six-step project cycle from vague curiosity to actioned findings.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Six-Step Research Project Cycle**:
  1. **Surface the knowledge gap** — through ongoing cross-functional conversation, identify a topic the team is curious about or unsure of (e.g., "do users like this existing feature?").
  2. **Define the question** — identify concrete goals/objectives the research should achieve; clarify what's already known, what isn't, timeline constraints, and which user segments to target.
  3. **Define the research plan** — a document covering goals/objectives, timeline, participants to recruit, and the questions to be answered.
  4. **Recruitment and guided script** — recruit participants and write a discussion guide with specific, well-thought-out questions plus probing/follow-up questions; in smaller teams this also means scheduling, incentives, and consent paperwork without dedicated recruiting support.
  5. **Facilitation** — execute the plan with participants (interview, diary study, usability test, workshop, etc.), typically recorded, often with stakeholders (PMs, designers, engineers) listening in live.
  6. **Synthesis** — review recordings/notes, extract insights that answer the research questions, then package findings into a share-out deck or document and present to stakeholders, followed by a working session on next steps/solutioning.
  - When to use: as the default shape for any research engagement, scaled up or down depending on team size and resourcing.
- **Bias-Replacement Rationale for Research**: before dedicated research exists, assumptions about users are made by whoever is closest to the feature (PM, engineer, designer) — people who have psychological ownership over what they built and are structurally unable to be objective about it. Research's core value is being the neutral party that can tell those three roles what users actually want.
  - When to use: to justify why research needs to sit outside the group that built the feature being evaluated.
- **Qualitative Evaluation of Research Impact**: because research value is hard to reduce to a single KPI, evaluate a researcher (or your own research practice) by process quality: how well findings were represented to the team in conversation, how much cross-functional rapport/trust was built, how effectively workshops brought the team together around users, and what insights actually caused a product-decision pivot — not just a checklist of studies completed.

## Key Concepts
- **Guided script**: the interview protocol — specific questions plus prepared probes/follow-ups — used during facilitation.
- **Psychological ownership**: the bias a feature's builder develops toward it, which makes self-assessment of that feature's success unreliable.
- **Cross-functional listening-in**: allowing stakeholders (PMs, designers, engineers) to observe live sessions so findings land with more shared understanding, not just a secondhand report.

## Mental Models
- Treat the research cycle as looping back into itself: synthesis and stakeholder presentation often generate the next cycle's "knowledge gap" (step 1) rather than closing the topic permanently.
- When evaluating research quality, look for evidence of influence (a decision that changed because of the research) rather than volume (number of studies run) — this mirrors the influence-vs-persuasion distinction found elsewhere in this domain.
- Use facilitation time as the place to build the rapport that pays off in synthesis and presentation — the relationship built with stakeholders who observe sessions shapes how receptive they are to the findings later.

## Anti-patterns
- **Skipping step 2 (defining the question) and going straight to a research plan**: without clarity on what's already known, what isn't, and which segments matter, the resulting study risks answering the wrong question.
- **Evaluating research purely on output volume** (number of studies, number of KPIs hit) instead of on whether it changed a decision — this misses what actually makes research valuable.
- **Letting the feature's builder be the sole judge of whether it's working**: psychological ownership makes an unbiased self-assessment structurally unlikely.

## Key Takeaways
1. Run every research engagement through the same six-step shape: surface gap → define question → define plan → recruit + script → facilitate → synthesize and present.
2. Justify research's organizational role by naming the specific bias it corrects: psychological ownership among builders.
3. Judge research impact by influence on decisions and cross-functional trust built, not by a simple KPI count.
4. Invite stakeholders to observe live sessions (facilitation) to build shared understanding that makes synthesis land harder later.
