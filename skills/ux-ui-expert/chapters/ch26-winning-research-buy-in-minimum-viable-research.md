# Chapter 26: Winning Research Buy-In and Minimum Viable Research

## Core Idea
Resistance to research is itself a UX problem: find out why people say no, then propose a study small enough to pass and tied to a metric they already care about. A narrow first study that produces a visible insight earns the trust and budget for larger research. This chapter supplies tactics; the argument from cost is in ch13 and the influence model in ch11.

## Why Skipping Research Costs More
Research pays off in five ways that work as stakeholder talking points:
1. **Early adopters**: people who share the core problem give focused feedback and become advocates.
2. **Hypothesis validation**: write down how success will be measured before building, then let research test it.
3. **Underserved needs**: observe real use; features can emerge from unplanned use.
4. **Workarounds**: homemade solutions (a paper log, a spreadsheet) reveal unmet needs. Interview in people's environment and on their devices.
5. **Competitor knowledge**: participants name and show the tools they use; live follow-ups beat surveys.
Side effects of not researching: launches nobody uses, expensive rework, falling morale.

## The "We Are the User" Fallacy
Insiders are too close to be typical users, and a founder's or relative's habits are not market evidence. People describe their problems well and predict future features poorly, so spend most effort on the problem and little on the solution.

## Minimum Viable Research (MVR) Sequence
1. **Know your audience**: interview the sceptic and people close to them. Ask for stories about past research (over-budget studies, unusable insights, poor recruitment), not "why don't you want research?", which yields stock answers.
2. **Know your product**: find the metric that matters now (sign-ups for a new product, subscriptions for an established one, cart completion for commerce). Mine existing sources for a few hours: analytics, customer-service logs, app reviews, social mentions. Locate the step with the biggest drop-off and cross-check with service feedback and team intuition.
3. **Run one narrow study** on that step: a single question, combining quantitative (A/B test of one variable) and qualitative (moderated sessions, think-aloud). Test one element so the study takes days, not weeks.
4. **Present the insight first, then the evidence**: numbers show what happens; session video (face, mouse path) shows why and persuades more. Heatmaps and click maps are baseline instrumentation.
5. **Reinvest**: use the win to ask for larger or multi-location work. A shared picture of the user across departments is itself a deliverable; an outside researcher can reduce bias.

## Data Gives the What, People Give the Why
Use analytics as a wide spotlight to find where to investigate (for example checkout abandonment), then run one-to-one interviews or usability tests on that area. Never infer the cause of a drop-off from numbers, and do not skip interviews because the dashboard looks clear. (ch05: analytics answers "how many".)

## First Usability Test: Keep It Small
Pick the area where analytics show a problem or the team disagrees (one onboarding step with drop-off). Recruit from the real target audience, matched on interest and technical proficiency. Run about five sessions, in person if possible for body language, remote if not. Themes usually repeat after two or three participants; test at least two so there is something to compare. Teams are too close to their product, so new users give fresh eyes. Do not test a whole checkout flow at once; logistics and data become unmanageable.

## Planning Research Around the Audience
- Decide who the findings are for first. Product teams accept raw recordings; executives or investors need polished evidence (better capture, a professional facility).
- Research needs no dedicated lab: remote tools that recruit and record screen, face and audio replace rented facilities.
- Test early with sketches, paper or clickable wireframes.
- **Bookend** the development cycle: before building, before launch, after launch, after each significant change. Test early and often.

## Where Rigor Differs: Insight-Led vs Research-First
Disagreement: some products legitimately start from a technology or business insight and build a bridge to customer fit; established companies research incremental changes heavily. Use research-first when the cost of a wrong launch is high or the audience is unknown; use an insight-led start with a sprint (ch30) when speed matters, treating assumptions as hypotheses that testing may overturn. Do not impose one golden process, and do not claim testing gives final answers before launch.

## Research-Led Launch Loop (single anecdote, treat as illustrative)
Ask about goals, outcomes and obstacles, not wish lists; recruit where the audience gathers (ch27); turn answers into goals, problems and obstacles with exact quotes beside each; build the smallest thing that moves people from obstacle to outcome; launch, learn, repeat. Reuse customers' own words in sales copy. One reported case: a six-question open survey (about 400 answers) followed by a paid pre-sale converted about 21 percent of respondents against a cited benchmark of about 2 percent; one case, not a rule.

## Mistakes
- Proposing a broad study before any evidence exists, or testing the whole flow when one step drives the metric.
- Presenting facts without video.
- Framing research as a large-budget project.

## Checklist
- [ ] Sceptic interviewed; objection traced to a past experience.
- [ ] Target metric named; existing data mined.
- [ ] One question, one step, days not weeks.
- [ ] Insight first, evidence second, video included.
- [ ] Next, larger study proposed using the result.
