# Chapter 42: What Makes a Site Look Professional, and How a Page Converts

## Core Idea
A site reads as professional when many small decisions agree with each other and with one stated goal. Colour, structure and content are chosen for that goal, not for decoration. This chapter collects the page-level signals; the underlying rules live in ch17 (mistakes), ch22 (colour), ch33 (system) and ch37 (premium feel).

## Decide the Goal First
- Storytelling (make people want it) and selling (make people buy) are different jobs. A strong pattern is two surfaces: a narrative landing page and a lean store page, not one page doing both.
- Pick the one action each surface exists for; every section either serves it or goes.

## Signals of a Professional Look
- **Colour discipline**: a dominant neutral, a supporting colour, and one accent reserved for the action that matters (60-30-10, ch22). Reapply per section as a micro-palette with its own accent; a stray accent on the dominant area makes the CTA almost invisible.
- **Colour as a system**: palette stored as semantic tokens (ch33); section palette changes (for instance tied to scroll) are variable swaps, not restyling.
- **Accessible by default**: text contrast at least 4.5:1, status never carried by colour alone (ch18). Treat the legal context as version-dependent / verify before quoting scope or penalties (ch22).
- **Real content, not placeholder**: believable names, images and numbers; lorem ipsum and stock filler read as unfinished (ch33).
- **Restraint**: few fonts, few effects, one motion vocabulary; micro-animations support meaning rather than decorate (ch17, ch37 "know when not to animate").
- **Consistency of components**: the same button looks and behaves the same everywhere (ch33).
- **Colour rules are guidelines**: more colours can serve a story if contrast and accessibility hold; colour is a brand communication tool, not ornament.

## Above the Fold
- The first screen decides within seconds whether people stay: say what the product is, show it, give the key fact (date, platform, price band) and the primary CTA.
- The main CTA is reachable without scrolling on desktop and phone.
- A loader or opening animation earns its place only if it communicates the product.
- Make the CTA follow the reader (sticky or re-appearing in a fixed corner) on long pages, on desktop and mobile.

## Narrative Content
- Show what the user gets as cards with a preview of the real content, instead of a flat feature list.
- Put the most important content at the top and make it most prominent (ch18 hierarchy).

## Lean Selling Page
- Fewer animations, essential layout.
- An in-page anchor bar to jump between sections without reload.
- Trailer or screenshots plus an FAQ to remove doubts before the purchase decision.
- A primary button that scrolls straight to the comparison of options.

## Comparing Options and the Decoy Effect
- Two or three options; the preferred one is in front and coloured, the others are muted with longer lists of what they lack.
- The same pattern appears in free/premium and basic/pro/enterprise plan tables.
- Ethics: when the layout steers people to the costliest option while hiding a better-fit cheaper one, it crosses from clear presentation into manipulation (ch20 nudges, ch39). Show the real differences honestly and let a lower option be a good fit.

## Checklist
- [ ] One goal per surface; sections that do not serve it removed.
- [ ] Primary CTA visible above the fold and sticky on long pages.
- [ ] Accent colour reserved for the action; contrast checked.
- [ ] Real content in place; nothing placeholder.
- [ ] Plan or edition comparison honest about differences.
