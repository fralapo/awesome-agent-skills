# Chapter 10: Measuring and Tracking UX Research Impact

## Core Idea
Impact isn't only hard metrics — it spans product, organizational, team, and personal dimensions — and measuring it always requires a baseline/benchmark, because "everything is relative" without a point of comparison.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Kinds of UX Research Impact**: (1) Product/Strategic Impact — hard KPIs (revenue, churn, conversion, satisfaction) tied to research-driven changes, UI/strategic changes shipped, initiatives prioritized/deprioritized, "Evergreen insights" that stay referenceable for years; (2) Organizational/Team-Culture Impact — shifting from solution-first to user-first thinking, moving stakeholders from reactive to proactive research requests, becoming the "go-to expert," research insights being disseminated and championed by non-researchers; (3) Research-Team/Operational Impact — complementary skill mix (quant+qual), collaboration with other research functions (data science, market research), building research operations (recruiting, incentives, participant tracking), creating reusable processes/templates, scaling research capability across teams; (4) Personal Impact — recognition, shout-outs, screenshotted kudos, number of times your insights/decks are referenced, session/presentation attendance — tracked for performance reviews.
  - When to use: building an impact case study or performance review narrative that goes beyond hard metrics.
- **5 Steps to Measuring UX Success**: (1) Decide what to measure — tie it to actual objectives/outcomes, don't measure for measuring's sake; (2) Plan the study — same process as any study plan; (3) Conduct the baseline/benchmark study — your first measurement is the reference point everything else compares to; (4) Continuously measure and compare against the baseline as you iterate; (5) Visualize the before/after data and tell the story — pair quantitative deltas with qualitative context, since "numbers don't tell the whole story."
  - When to use: any UX metrics program (SUS scores, task success rates, satisfaction tracking) that needs to show progress over time.

## Key Concepts
- **Baseline / Benchmark**: the first measurement, used purely as a comparison point for all future measurements — a raw score (e.g., SUS 65) means nothing without one.
- **Evergreen insights**: findings durable enough to remain useful/referenceable for months or years, distinguishing them from insights that expire as the product changes.

## Mental Models
- "Everything is relative" (Einstein) — track impact by comparing to your own prior state, like a race car driver's lap timer comparing this lap to the last.
- Qualitative signals (a "feel" for how a design change lands, verbatim feedback) matter alongside quantitative deltas — don't let numbers-only thinking crowd out story.
- Screenshot every piece of positive feedback or recognition as you go — personal impact evidence is easy to lose track of otherwise.

## Anti-patterns
- **Measuring everything "just in case"**: dilutes focus and produces data nobody interprets — measure what's tied to a real objective.
- **Reporting only hard-number impact**: misses organizational, team, and personal impact categories that are equally valid and often easier to demonstrate early in a research practice's maturity.
- **Skipping the baseline study**: makes any later "improvement" claim unverifiable — you need your own first study as the comparison point.

## Key Takeaways
1. Track impact across all four dimensions (product, organizational/culture, research-team/operational, personal) — not just hard metrics.
2. Always establish a baseline/benchmark before claiming improvement.
3. Tie every measurement decision back to a real objective; don't measure for its own sake.
4. Pair quantitative deltas with qualitative narrative when reporting progress — numbers alone under-tell the story.
5. Proactively document personal impact (screenshots of kudos, reference counts) — it won't be tracked for you.
