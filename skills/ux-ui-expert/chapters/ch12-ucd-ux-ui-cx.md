# Chapter 12: User-Centred Design and the UX / UI / CX Distinction

## Core Idea
User-Centred Design (UCD) is the practice of designing digital products and services around real user behavior and context rather than internal convenience; UX, UI, and CX are three overlapping but distinct lenses on that same practice, each covering a different scope of the user's interaction with a product, interface, and organization.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **UX / UI / CX Scope Model**: three nested-but-distinct terms.
  - **UX (User Experience)**: "a person's perceptions and responses that result from the use or anticipated use of a product, system, or service" — covers look and feel, responsiveness, and how the product fits into someone's daily life. Broader than the interface itself.
  - **UI (User Interface)**: the physical, visible layer — visual design, color, layout, branding — what the user actually sees and touches.
  - **CX (Customer Experience)**: every step of the journey from comparing products, through purchase, through calling customer service if needs aren't met — encompasses UX and UI plus sales, delivery, and support touchpoints across all channels.
  - When to use: to clarify scope disagreements in cross-functional teams — a UI fix (visual polish) won't resolve a UX problem (task friction), and neither will resolve a CX problem (a broken post-purchase support journey).
  - How: UX and UI overlap heavily in wireframing, prototyping, usability, and accessibility work; UI is the visible subset of UX; CX is the superset that wraps UX/UI inside the full customer journey.
- **The UX Iceberg**: visual design is the visible tip (~10% of the process). Beneath the surface: interface design (structure, navigation, information architecture), then functional specifications and content requirements, then — at the base, earliest in the process — strategy (who the users are, what the objectives are). Priorities should be set bottom-up even though the deliverable is built top-down in visibility.
  - When to use: to push back on stakeholders who want to jump straight to visual design before strategy and information architecture are defined.
- **Five UCD Design Principles**: (1) design for your target audience, (2) account for the organization's goals — the designer's preference doesn't override the business objective, (3) make it easy to learn and use — intuitiveness is non-negotiable, (4) make it satisfying and pleasant to use, (5) prioritize the human experience above all — when principles conflict, the end user's experience wins.

## Key Concepts
- **User-Centred Design (UCD)**: a design process that centers user behavior, needs, and context at every decision point, rather than designer or engineering convenience.
- **Anticipated use**: UX includes not just actual use but a person's expectations before they've even used the product — first impressions and assumptions matter as much as the interaction itself.
- **Information architecture**: the structural organization of content and navigation, addressed in the interface-design layer of the UX iceberg, beneath visual design.

## Mental Models
- Use the footpath/desire-path example as a diagnostic: if users consistently "cut the corner" around a designed process (skip a step, misuse a feature, build a workaround), the system was designed around a builder's convenience, not the user's actual path — redesign around the path people actually take.
- Think of a UI redesign that changes physical interaction with a product (e.g., inverting a container so gravity works with the user instead of against them) as the model for good UX: the best UX shifts often come from re-examining a physical/interaction assumption nobody had questioned, not from adding features.
- Treat "focus on the user and all else will follow" as a design-prioritization heuristic: when a decision is ambiguous, default to what most benefits the end user's experience, and trust that business results follow from that trust-building.

## Anti-patterns
- **Designing for the builder/system, not the user**: convenience decisions made from an internal or engineering perspective (the "cut corner" footpath) that ignore how people will actually move through the process.
- **Treating UI as the whole of UX**: polishing visual design (the iceberg's tip) without addressing the underlying structure, information architecture, or strategy beneath it fixes nothing structural.
- **Letting designer preference override organizational goals**: UCD principle #2 exists precisely because designer intuition and business objectives can conflict — the design still has to serve what the organization is paying for.

## Key Takeaways
1. Distinguish UX (experience with a product/service), UI (the visible interface layer), and CX (the full customer journey across every touchpoint) — a fix at one layer doesn't fix problems at another.
2. Treat visual design as only ~10% of the UX process; the bulk of the work — strategy, information architecture, functional specs — happens beneath what stakeholders see.
3. Apply the five UCD principles as a checklist: target audience, organizational goals, learnability, satisfaction, human-experience priority.
4. Look for "desire path" signals (users routing around your design) as evidence the system was built around the wrong point of reference.
