# Chapter 2: Stakeholders, Daily Life, and Team Structure

## Core Idea
UX research doesn't happen in isolation — success depends on knowing your four core stakeholders' needs, accepting that most of your week is meetings and planning (not sessions), and understanding which team structure (embedded, consulting, or hybrid) shapes how you build impact.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Core Stakeholders**: Product Manager, Designer, Developer/Engineer, Data Scientist/Analyst — each with distinct needs and collaboration patterns.
  - When to use: onboarding onto a new team; scoping who to involve in a study.
  - How: PM needs confidence/validation for product strategy → sync early on goals/scope, involve throughout, give clear next-action recommendations. Designer needs data-informed design direction → sync like a PM, may combine roles at startups. Developer needs exact requirements and feasibility input → tell you what's buildable now vs. later. Data scientist needs to know "why" behind the "what" they see in aggregate data → sync and cross-reference qual/quant samples together.
- **Team Structure Models**: Embedded (dedicated to one team — builds deep expertise and traceable impact, but loses cross-functional context), Consulting/Centralized/Studio (serves many teams — broad exposure, but impact is harder to track since you don't stick around), Hybrid (embedded on a larger team while consulting to its sub-teams).
  - When to use: negotiating role scope or explaining to leadership why research capacity is structured a certain way.

## Key Concepts
- **Stakeholder**: someone with a vested interest in a product, initiative, or project team.
- **Embedded model**: researcher permanently attached to one team/product.
- **Consulting/centralized model**: researcher supports multiple teams as an internal consultant.
- **Bird's-eye view**: cross-functional context an embedded researcher lacks and must actively seek by collaborating across teams.

## Mental Models
- Users experience your product holistically — they don't care which internal team owns which part, so an embedded researcher must deliberately collaborate cross-functionally to avoid tunnel vision.
- Expect your actual week to be mostly meetings, planning, scheduling, and analysis — not "60-70% talking to users." Budget your calendar accordingly, don't feel behind when session time is a minority of your week.
- Treat a research project timeline as inherently parallel, not sequential: you'll typically be running one study, wrapping a second, and planning a third simultaneously.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating stakeholder titles as fixed or important**: PM vs. product owner distinctions rarely matter in practice — involve whoever owns the decision, regardless of title.
- **Not involving developers in the "why"**: developers under deadline pressure build without exposure to user context; researchers should build empathy bridges, not just hand off requirements.
- **Assuming embedded = full context**: embedded researchers who never talk to sibling teams miss how their slice fits the user's holistic journey — a narrow product area can span a much larger end-to-end experience than the one team owns.

## Key Takeaways
1. Map your four core stakeholders' needs explicitly (PM: confidence/validation; designer: data-informed direction; developer: feasibility and empathy; data scientist: the "why" behind their "what") and sync early and often.
2. Involve stakeholders throughout research (not just at readout) — nothing should be a surprise by the end.
3. Expect a hectic, non-linear schedule with multiple simultaneous projects — prioritize communication and buffer time.
4. No team structure is inherently better; embedded trades depth for narrow context, consulting trades breadth for weak impact tracking — pick (or explain) based on team needs.
