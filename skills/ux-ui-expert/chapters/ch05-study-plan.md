# Chapter 5: Crafting the Study Plan

## Core Idea
A study plan is a living, shared document — not just a methodology recipe — that aligns stakeholders on objectives, records how research was conducted, and should take no more than a couple of hours to draft as a first version, because it's meant to evolve through stakeholder feedback, not arrive perfect.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Study Plan Structure**: Background/Problem (why now, links to PRD/roadmap) → Objectives (+ Non-Objectives to explicitly scope out) → Research Questions (+ success metrics, if applicable) → Timeline (+ DACI — who's working on what and when) → Methodology (methods, participants, sample size, materials) → Procedure/Discussion Guide (aka script/protocol — a "cooking recipe" others could replicate).
  - When to use: every research study, as the central artifact for alignment and record-keeping.
- **Research Question Framework (Who / Question / Why)**: a 3-column table — Who you're learning from, the Research Question, and Why you're asking (what you'll do with the answer). Forces you to check whether qualitative research is even the right tool (qualitative can't answer "how many people click X" — that's analytics).
  - When to use: whenever drafting research questions, to avoid asking questions "for the hell of it."
- **Method/Sample Organization Table**: columns for Method, What You're Trying to Learn, and Participant Segment — clarifies which method serves which audience and question.
- **Screener Organization Table**: columns for Track (which answers route to which follow-up), Question, and Why (the purpose of asking) — distinguishes true screening (excluding people) from quota/profiling (seeking a mix of answers).

## Key Concepts
- **DACI framework**: who's driving/working on what and when — prevents team finger-pointing over unclear ownership.
- **Non-objectives**: explicitly scoped-out questions, parked for a future study to keep the current one focused.
- **Pilot study**: a dry run of the study plan/discussion guide before real sessions — the fallback way to catch problems when stakeholders don't give feedback in time.
- **Domain knowledge baseline**: enough industry jargon/context to converse credibly and probe meaningfully with participants — not full domain expertise (which can even hinder fresh-eyed research).

## Mental Models
- Treat your first study plan draft as literally a draft — announce it as such when sharing, to defuse perfectionism/imposter syndrome.
- Tag specific stakeholders on specific sections when requesting feedback (rather than "here's the whole doc") — people engage more when the ask feels small, and they'll naturally skim the rest.
- If you can't convince yourself a study is needed, you won't convince anyone else — resolve internal doubt about scope/necessity before greenlighting a study.

## Anti-patterns
- **Perfecting the study plan before sharing**: burns hours that could go to discussion-guide writing after alignment is confirmed.
- **Skipping the non-objectives section**: leads to scope creep as every stakeholder adds "just one more question."
- **Asking behavioral-frequency questions in qualitative studies**: "how many people click X" needs analytics data, not 5-8 interviews — a common qual-research misuse.
- **Not baselining domain jargon before fielding**: wastes session time, misses what to probe on, and undermines participant trust — a specialized industry (e.g. logistics, healthcare, finance) has its own vocabulary that a researcher must learn enough of to be taken seriously.

## Key Takeaways
1. A study plan's core sections are Background, Objectives (+ Non-Objectives), Research Questions, Timeline (+ DACI), Methodology, and Procedure/Discussion Guide.
2. First drafts should take minutes, not hours — front-load a real kickoff meeting, then just transcribe notes into the template.
3. Use the Who/Question/Why table to test whether each research question is qualitative-research-appropriate and purposeful.
4. Get feedback before running the study whenever possible; a pilot study is the fallback, not a substitute, for stakeholder alignment.
5. Baseline enough domain jargon to be taken seriously and know what to probe — but don't over-invest in becoming a domain expert; outsider perspective is an asset.
