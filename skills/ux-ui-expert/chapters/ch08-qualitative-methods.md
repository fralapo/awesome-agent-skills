# Chapter 8: Core Qualitative Methods

## Core Idea
Four core qualitative methods — usability testing, card sorting, contextual inquiry/field research, and diary studies — each have well-established sample-size and procedure conventions that most researchers reinvent from scratch; knowing the defaults saves time and builds credibility.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Magic Number Five**: 5 participants in a usability test typically uncover ~80-85% of usability problems (Nielsen, Virzi, Lewis research); more participants yield diminishing returns. Practical recruit target: 6-8 (buffer for no-shows). This holds per design/prototype being tested, regardless of how many participant sub-segments (countries, personas) you're sampling from — unless you have a strong hypothesis that segments differ enough to warrant separate localized studies.
  - When to use: any single-design usability test; recruit more (and consider between-subjects splits) only if testing multiple distinct prototypes.
- **Card Sorting — Open vs. Closed**: Open card sort (no predefined categories, used to develop a new information architecture) vs. Closed card sort (predefined categories, used to validate an existing IA — "where would you place this?").
  - Sample size: moderated card sorts, 10-15 participants (deeper probing compensates for fewer people); unmoderated, 20-30+ participants for adequate statistical power.
  - **5-Step Card Sort Procedure**: (1) prepare labeled cards + blank cards for "missing" items, (2) have participants think aloud while sorting, probe on hesitation, (3) review resulting groups and ask if any should merge/split, (4) have participants label each group, (5) photograph/screenshot the final layout. Physical or online, in-person 30-60 cards, online 30-80 cards (fatigue increases past that).
- **Contextual Inquiry**: combines observation with interviewing to study behavior in the participant's natural environment (home, office, worksite) — explicitly NOT true ethnography (which studies culture/customs over extended time); scope is hours-to-days, focused on specific goals/tasks, not open-ended culture study. Use when a team can't find root causes for a problem, needs to understand user context for a task, or wants field validation before brainstorming solutions.
- **Diary Study**: longitudinal method (days to years) where participants self-log behaviors/attitudes over time to reveal how small actions add up to bigger decisions. Bookend with an initial interview and a final exit interview. Strong at avoiding the Hawthorne Effect (behavior change from being observed) since you're not physically present.

## Key Concepts
- **Information Architecture (IA)**: how content/features are organized and labeled for navigation — what card sorting is used to design or validate.
- **Mystery shopper / service safari**: undercover field method where the researcher poses as a customer to study service interactions.
- **Hawthorne Effect**: behavior changing because participants know they're being observed — a known risk of live field observation, mitigated by diary studies.
- **Attrition/drop-off**: the main risk of long diary studies — mitigate with shorter durations or milestone incentives.

## Mental Models
- Don't recruit people "too close" to the product (teammates, yourself, non-target-audience family/friends) — you'll learn nothing representative.
- Usability testing is "the pull-up of UX research" — a foundational skill, not the whole job, but one every serious researcher should master.
- Card sorting can sort more than words — images, icons, even wireframe sections — get creative with the stimulus.

## Anti-patterns
- **Recruiting only for saturation-by-segment**: over-recruiting per demographic subgroup when 5 total target users would answer the usability question just as well.
- **Giving 100+ cards in a single card sort**: causes fatigue and unreliable sorting; trim the set or split into multiple studies first.
- **Calling contextual inquiry "ethnography"**: overclaims rigor/scope the method doesn't have.
- **Running a one-day diary study**: too short to reveal any behavioral trend — defeats the method's purpose.

## Key Takeaways
1. Five participants per design is usually enough for usability testing; recruit 6-8 for buffer.
2. Use open card sorts to build new IA, closed card sorts to validate existing IA; moderate with 10-15 participants, or go unmoderated with 20-30+.
3. Contextual inquiry = observation + interview in situ, scoped to hours/days — don't call it ethnography.
4. Diary studies excel at revealing behavior-over-time and avoid the Hawthorne Effect, but cost time and risk attrition — bookend with interviews and plan duration deliberately.
