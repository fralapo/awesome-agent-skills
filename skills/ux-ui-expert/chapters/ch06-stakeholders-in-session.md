# Chapter 6: Managing Live Sessions and Writing Recommendations

## Core Idea
Moderating a session well means diplomatically managing observer behavior in real time, and the research only creates impact if recommendations are data-driven, appropriately scoped, and actively tracked to completion afterward.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three Stakeholder-Gone-Wild Scenarios and Fixes**: (1) Observer forgets to mute/hide camera → don't call them out on-call; politely message them and use it as a chance to introduce them to the participant. (2) Observer pings you many questions mid-session → acknowledge without ignoring ("got it," "sounds good," "I'll cover it later"), so they don't feel unheard. (3) Observer unmutes and asks the participant directly (possibly a leading question) → let them finish, reframe/re-ask the question neutrally if needed, then privately ask them to relay future questions through you.
  - When to use: set ground rules before the call, but have these responses ready for when rules break down anyway.
- **Good Recommendation Criteria**: data-driven (backed by evidence, tied to user needs/pain points), addresses the bigger opportunity when possible (not just one narrow fix), feasible within technical/resource constraints, appropriately prioritized (impact, severity, prevalence, frequency), never blames the user, not too specific nor too broad, and concise (recommendation stands alone, context lives elsewhere).
  - How: give a recommended priority from the research, but let the cross-functional team make final prioritization calls with other data sources.
- **Poor Recommendation Red Flags**: too solution/feature-focused (pigeonholes the team to one fix instead of opening a brainstorm), not data-driven (based on assumptions or one attractive participant suggestion — reframe as "a participant suggested X, let's explore it" rather than adopting it outright), "more research is needed" as a cop-out (fine for surfacing new themes, not fine when it dodges the original research question), and blaming the user.

## Key Concepts
- **Findings vs. Insights vs. Recommendations**: findings are the "what," insights are the "so what," recommendations are the "now what."
- **Momentum after a study**: proactively tracking whether recommendations get actioned (tickets in a project-management tool or a simple tracking spreadsheet), not just publishing a report and hoping.

## Mental Models
- You are the research expert and moderator — stay in control of the session diplomatically; redirecting an eager stakeholder isn't rude, it protects data quality.
- Treat a participant's off-the-cuff suggestion as a discussion prompt for the team ("how might we..."), not as an adopted recommendation.
- If nobody acts on your recommendations, the research effectively "didn't happen" — proactive tracking is part of the researcher's job, not just handoff.

## Anti-patterns
- **Ignoring a chatty observer**: makes them feel dismissed and more likely to interrupt directly later.
- **Presenting one-solution recommendations**: limits the team's creative range; prefer opening a brainstorm framed by the insight.
- **Over-explaining a recommendation**: burying the actual "now what" in context makes it hard for stakeholders to act — separate context from the recommendation itself, visually and verbally.
- **Passive reporting**: publishing findings and expecting stakeholders to self-organize action, instead of creating tickets and running 1:1 follow-ups.

## Key Takeaways
1. Prepare (mentally or literally) for the three common stakeholder-gone-wild scenarios before every observed session.
2. A good recommendation is data-driven, feasible, prioritized, concise, and never blames the user.
3. Avoid single-solution, non-data-driven, or "needs more research" recommendations unless genuinely exploratory.
4. Momentum requires actively tracking implementation (tickets, 1:1s) — don't stop at the readout.
