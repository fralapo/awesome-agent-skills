# Chapter 3: Types of Research and Business Acumen

## Core Idea
The dozens of confusing research-type terms (generative, foundational, exploratory, evaluative, formative, summative...) collapse into one clean map along the product development process — and knowing this map matters less than understanding that research is the keystone of business itself, because businesses exist only because they solve customer problems.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Strategic vs. Tactical Research Map**: Strategic research (= foundational = generative = exploratory = discovery — all the same thing) focuses on big-picture, long-term, adaptive understanding of users, markets, and opportunities; it's the foundation everything else builds on. Tactical research (= evaluative research) is nitty-gritty implementation-focused work — testing specific designs/prototypes.
  - When to use: pick ONE set of terms per team/audience and stick with it — use "strategic/tactical" with leadership (sounds businesslike), "generative/evaluative" with product teams.
- **Evaluative Research Sub-types**: Formative (early-stage evaluative work — usability testing, heuristic evaluation, improving an existing idea) vs. Summative (post-launch, usually quantitative — tracking metrics, sentiment, NPS at scale).
  - When to use: formative before launch to improve; summative after launch to measure real-world performance.
- **Concept Testing**: not a method itself, but a goal — validate or invalidate whether an idea resonates, using any method (survey, interview, focus group, usability test). Goal is idea-level value, not usability.
  - How: ask "do users care about this idea," not "can they use it." Don't usability-test at the concept stage.

## Key Concepts
- **Foundational/generative research**: building the knowledge base of who users are and what problems exist — "users don't tell us the problems; it's the researcher's job to synthesize and spot them."
- **Evaluative/tactical research**: testing an existing idea/concept/prototype to improve it.
- **Product development process**: problem/opportunity → research → concepts → solutioning → evaluation → decision → shipping → measure → iterate; research touches every step.
- **Double (or Triple, Quadruple...) Diamond**: the design-thinking pattern of diverge-then-converge, repeated as many times as the product/timeline requires — not fixed at two diamonds.

## Mental Models
- Use whatever research-type terminology your team already uses — the point is shared understanding, not vocabulary purity.
- Think of "the most important person in any business" as the customer — any business framework or KPI conversation that forgets this is missing the point, since businesses only exist because customers believe in the solution.
- Product managers are "mini-CEOs" of their product; know how they think (business acumen) so research and business goals stay tied together.

## Anti-patterns
- **The Tactical Research Death Spiral**: only ever doing evaluative/usability testing because it's easy to schedule, starving foundational research and losing the "why" behind user behavior.
- **Conflating validation with research**: when a team asks you to "validate" a concept, that's a biased framing — the job is to validate AND invalidate, finding both what works and what doesn't.
- **Metrics-only thinking (Campbell's Law)**: chasing incremental metric gains (1.2% → 1.3% conversion) can pull a team off-track from real user needs; measurements are inputs to minimize risk, not the goal itself. "Not everything that counts can be counted, and not everything that can be counted counts" (Einstein).

## Key Takeaways
1. Reduce the research-type jargon jungle to one map: strategic/foundational/generative (big-picture) vs. tactical/evaluative (implementation), with evaluative splitting into formative (pre-launch) and summative (post-launch).
2. Concept testing is a goal (validate/invalidate an idea), not a method — pick whatever method fits, and never usability-test at the concept stage.
3. Pick one terminology set per audience and stay consistent to avoid confusing your team.
4. Remember research is the keystone connecting business survival to customer understanding — reground any prioritization debate in "who is the most important person here."
