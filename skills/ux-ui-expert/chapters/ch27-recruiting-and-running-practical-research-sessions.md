# Chapter 27: Recruiting and Running Practical Research Sessions

## Core Idea
Good findings depend on who you talk to and how the session is run. Recruit where target users already gather, screen everyone, ask for stories about past behaviour, and benchmark top tasks before and after a redesign. This extends ch05 (screener), ch08 (methods, n=5) and ch15 (cycle) with field-level tactics.

## Who Not to Recruit
- **Friends and family**: too kind to give negative feedback and usually not the target profile. Include them only if they fit the profile, and always add qualified strangers.
- **Coffee-shop or street ("guerrilla") tests**: strangers are unscreened, rushed, give short answers with no room for "why?" probes, and printed mockups do not reproduce screen colour, readability or interaction. If unavoidable: 1-2 minutes of screening first and end early if criteria fail; treat answers as broad opinion; confirm with properly recruited participants before changing the design. Never change a design on this feedback alone.
- **Only happy customers**: add power users and people who dropped off after a trial.

## Where to Find Participants (in order)
1. Communities where users already talk (groups, forums, niche blogs): take part and add value before asking.
2. Niche influencers who can share the call.
3. Competitor reviews (app stores, blogs): reviewers describe the problem in their own words and many can be contacted.
4. Social search with several phrasings of the activity to see how people talk about the problem; treat opinions with scepticism.
5. Classified-style posts with a screener link: state time slots, duration and incentive; the post is the first filter.
6. Your own users: successful ones and those who left.
Recruiting firms are expensive per head and slow for niche audiences. Never spam groups without participating.

## Session Logistics
- Schedule usability sessions for one hour; 30 minutes leaves no buffer.
- Compensate participants for their time; one view holds it does not skew answers once people describe their own experience.
- Record sessions as evidence for the team (screen, face, audio).

## Interview Discussion Guide
1. **Intro and icebreakers**: day in the life, role, first technology used today.
2. **Topic behaviour**: current routines and habits.
3. **Story prompts**: "Tell me about the last time you did X"; then "what was hardest?", "did you invent any workarounds?", "what other tools did you use and how did you find them?"
4. **Product opportunity**: show a prototype or homepage; ask them to narrate what it is, who it is for, what task it supports, why they would or would not trust it.
5. **Reaction**: most appealing, hardest, surprising or missing, would they keep using it. Use the same questions for a competitor when no prototype exists.
Rules: ask about past events, not hypotheticals; follow up with "why?" and "what happened next?"; a 30-question guide gets through about half, so prioritise; workarounds signal an unmet need.

## Top-Tasks Benchmark (before/after)
1. Define 10-12 representative tasks for the key users (banking example: send money, withdraw, add funds).
2. Recruit about 15-18 people from the target segment; split by user group and test each group's tasks.
3. Run about one hour per person, all tasks, moderated (video call is fine).
4. Measure average time per task and success rate; target at least 80 percent success.
5. Debrief and take notes.
6. Repeat with the same segment after the redesign and compare.
For a freshly shipped feature, write 10-12 tasks covering its variants (with/without filters, with/without sorting). For an end-to-end test cover landing, onboarding, core task, upgrade. Quick check: talk with five users about problems, no formal test.

## Surveys for Problem Trends and Customer Language
Use a survey when you need the shape of a problem and the words people use, at low cost; follow with interviews for the why (ch26).
1. **Agree three zones** with the team or client first and map every question to one: current reality (what happens now, real recent events), obstacles (what blocks them, consequences, concerns), desired future (goals and motivations behind the wish).
2. **Write**: a screener first (those who answer no skip the rest), then specific questions. Use first-person, specific prompts that produce story-length answers ("The last time I felt X was when...", "My number one problem with X is...", "I'm interested in ... because..."). Ask for three to five words describing the problem to capture vocabulary. Always include an obstacle question and an open "anything else" field.
3. **Analyse one question at a time**: group answers into categories (an answer can fall in more than one), count the share per category. In one example about 200 responses put 41 percent in a single category. Apply the small-sample rules of ch07 below n of about 30.
4. **Reuse verbatim answers** as raw material for copy and for interview probes.
Avoid abstract "why" questions, hypothetical framing and skipping the obstacle question.
Staged demand tests (a sign-up form, then a small paid offer) work as a ladder: define what "passing" means before each step and advance only if it is met; paid commitments are stronger signals than free sign-ups (one reported case, ch26).

## Where Sources Disagree
- **Sample size**: five users surface major issues (ch08); about 15-18 are suggested when you need usable timing and success numbers. Choose five for finding problems, 15-18 for benchmarks.
- **Think-aloud**: ch08 uses it; one practitioner view says it slows people and distorts timing, so act naturally and debrief afterwards. Use think-aloud for diagnosis, silent tasks plus debrief for timed benchmarks.
- Test the product, not the participant: do not help or correct; let people fail, since failures are the most valuable data.

## Mistakes
- Treating a parent's or child's reaction as market evidence.
- Asking only about concepts users have not seen.
- Applying a generic template without adapting it.

## Checklist
- [ ] Screener written; every candidate filtered.
- [ ] At least two non-network sources used.
- [ ] One-hour slots, incentive stated, recording consent.
- [ ] Guide prioritised for half coverage.
- [ ] Benchmark tasks and success threshold set before designing.
