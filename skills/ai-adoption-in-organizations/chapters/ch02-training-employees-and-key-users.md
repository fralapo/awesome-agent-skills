# Chapter 2: Training Employees and Key Users

## Core Idea
Training is the most repeated recommendation in the adoption practice this skill draws on. The reasoning: a generic tool in trained hands solves many small problems at once, while a custom system for untrained people solves one problem and is often not used. A good programme is hands-on (most of the time spent at a computer on the learner's own tasks), leaves behind a reusable template, and continues after the workshop through named champions.

## 2.1 The four phases
- **Principle**: Assess, teach, sustain, measure.
- **How it works / sequence**:
  1. **Assessment**: a short questionnaire on current level, resistance, software in use and repetitive tasks.
  2. **Workshop**: usually two days in person; day one installs the working environment, day two builds a few skills per person for their own repetitive work.
  3. **Continuous training with champions**: a few people per department who keep teaching and collecting requests. Practitioners call this the phase most often done badly.
  4. **KPIs**: repeat the assessment after a few weeks and compare.
- **Moves**: Schedule the follow-up before the workshop ends; give champions protected time.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner.
- **Trade-off**: Two days out of the office is a real cost; a single lecture is cheaper and rarely changes habits.
- **Does not hold when**: The group is tiny and already skilled; then pairing is enough.

## 2.2 What the first day installs
- **Principle**: Give each person a "second brain": a folder that holds who they are, their role and goals, their writing style, current projects, processes, skills and outputs. The assistant reads it at the start of each session, so context does not have to be retyped.
- **How it works / sequence**: Start from a template with sections for role, daily notes, projects, processes, skills and output. Fill it by dictating or being interviewed by the assistant rather than typing.
- **Moves**: Keep it as plain text files the person owns; connect to company systems (customer database, accounting) in a second phase, with the data rules of [ch03](ch03-company-knowledge-and-sensitive-data.md).
- **Evidence**: Practitioner.
- **Trade-off**: A personal folder duplicates knowledge that the organization may later want shared.
- **Does not hold when**: Work is mostly in a controlled system that forbids local files.

## 2.3 Three habits worth teaching first
1. **Speak instead of typing**: dictation gives longer, richer context than short typed prompts.
2. **Let the assistant interview you** before it works, so missing context surfaces.
3. **Do not trust, ask for the source** of every number, and check it against the document.
- Teach the core mechanism in plain terms: the model produces probable text, it can be wrong with confidence, and it only knows what is in the current conversation, so a new task usually deserves a new conversation (see `ai-ml-concepts`, `coding-with-agents` ch06).
- **Evidence**: Practitioner.

## 2.4 Skills per employee
- **Principle**: A skill is a written procedure the assistant can load when relevant. For a team it works as a living standard operating procedure: updated when the process changes, shareable between colleagues.
- **Moves**: Each person leaves with two or three skills on tasks they repeat weekly (a report format, a reply style, a checking routine). Review them after real use and remove outdated prohibitions.
- **Trade-off**: Skills drift out of date silently; assign an owner.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner.

## 2.5 Who the key users are
- **Principle**: A key user is a colleague who knows the work, is curious and is trusted by peers. Pick for credibility, not for seniority or technical title.
- **Moves**: Start with a small group; give them a channel to share working examples; let them present results to the rest.
- **Evidence**: Position that trained key users can beat more powerful software; plausible and widely repeated, not measured here.

## 2.6 Resistance and fear
- Some people fear the environment ("this is for programmers") or fear for their job. Address both openly: show a task that disappears and the work that replaces it; start in a familiar interface; avoid promising that whole departments will vanish.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner.

## 2.7 Funding and obligations
- Public and sector training funds, chamber-of-commerce vouchers and regional programmes often exist to cover part of the cost of staff training. Rules, amounts and eligibility change and differ by country: **confirm with an adviser or the issuing body**. This skill gives no figures.
- Some regions place duties on organizations that use AI to train their staff and keep records. **This is not legal advice**: check the current text and ask a professional what applies.

## 2.8 Privacy basics for learners
- Free and low-cost consumer plans may use conversations to train models; business plans and API use usually do not, but read the current terms. Teach people which categories of data never enter a public service (see [ch03](ch03-company-knowledge-and-sensitive-data.md)).

## Common Mistakes
- A one-off lecture with no follow-up.
- Training only managers, or only the technical team.
- A template so large nobody fills it.
- No champions, no owner for the skills.
- Teaching tools instead of tasks.

## Checklist
- [ ] Assessment done; baseline recorded
- [ ] Workshop is mostly hands-on, on real tasks
- [ ] Each person leaves with a filled template and 2-3 skills
- [ ] Champions named with time allocated
- [ ] Second assessment scheduled
- [ ] Data rules taught on day one
