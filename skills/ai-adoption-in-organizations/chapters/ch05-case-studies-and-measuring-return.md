# Chapter 5: Case Studies and Measuring Return

## Core Idea
Most public AI-in-business case material is written by the people who sold or built the system. It is useful as a catalogue of **what was tried** and weak as evidence of **what it was worth**. This chapter gives a way to read cases, shapes seen repeatedly, and a minimal method for measuring return on your own pilots. Figures from cases are deliberately not reproduced here: they are dated, self-reported and often internally inconsistent.

## 5.1 How to read a case
- **Principle**: Separate the claim, the baseline, the measurement and the incentive.
- **How it works / sequence**: Ask five questions of any case. What was the situation before, in numbers? Who measured the change, and how? Over what period? What did it cost in money, staff time and upkeep? Who benefits from the story?
- **Moves**: Discount screenshots and testimonials; look for the unit (per quote, per call, per employee-week). If the arithmetic in the story does not reconcile (people times hours does not equal the total), treat the headline as unreliable.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner.
- **Trade-off**: Strict reading leaves little to learn from; use cases for ideas, not for forecasts.

## 5.2 Recurring case shapes
- **Manager upskilling in a large group**: a short workshop followed by dozens of small internal tools built by the managers themselves (purchasing checks, accounting helpers, a driver web app, a sales dashboard, supplier control, a mail assistant, a management dashboard). A minority are used every day; some are passed to colleagues; some avoid buying another software product. The lesson is the **funnel**: many built, few kept, fewer shared.
- **Rebuilding a CRM with an agent**: a small business replaces a purchased system with one built by an agent after training, adding weekly routines (sales feedback from call recordings, objection analysis to decide content) and a customer app. Lesson: value came from the process knowledge of the owner, and from keeping the human relationship with customers.
- **Reactivation of dormant customers**: see [ch04](ch04-customer-and-sales-assistants-on-messaging-channels.md); results are self-reported.
- **Quote automation**: classify mail, find customer data, draft the quote for approval. Lesson: the saving is per item times volume; check both.
- **Domain research assistant**: a lawyer's research workflow with a search tool, firm-specific skills and anonymised data. Lesson: **a professional was sanctioned in one reported case for citing invented rulings**, so every output is verified against the original source.
- **Fan or customer assistant for an organization**: a prototype built only from public sources to demonstrate value before touching private data. Lesson: a public-data demo is a cheap, safe first step.
- **Evidence**: Observational; all are self-reported.

## 5.3 Training path versus custom build
- **Position**: A training-led path returns value in weeks to a few months; a custom build needs a longer development and test period, and returns later. Practitioners quote different durations; treat any single figure as an illustration, not a benchmark.
- **Decision rule**: Choose custom where the task is high-volume, well-defined, integrates with systems that have interfaces, and an owner will maintain it. Otherwise train first.

## 5.4 Measuring return in a pilot
- **Principle**: Decide the metric before the pilot, measure the baseline, run for a fixed period, and compare.
- **How it works / sequence**:
  1. Pick one unit of work (a quote, a report, a support request).
  2. Time it before: median and spread, with who did it.
  3. Run the assisted version for a set period; record time, rework, errors caught in review, and satisfaction.
  4. Cost it: licences, usage, build time, upkeep, review time, training.
  5. Report net time or money, the error rate and what you chose not to count.
- **Moves**: Use a **comparison group** or a before/after window of the same season. Separate hours saved from hours *redeployed*: a saved hour is only worth money if it is used for something valuable.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner (standard evaluation practice).
- **Trade-off**: Time tracking itself costs effort and can change behaviour.
- **Does not hold when**: The task is rare or strategic; then judge by a qualitative review.

## 5.5 Typical numbers you should not copy
- Hours saved per person per week, "percentage of work automated", revenue "unlocked", valuation of a built system by what a vendor would charge. These are the most quoted and the least comparable figures. **Contested.** Record your own.

## 5.6 Second-order effects
- Software spend can go down while usage cost goes up; tools that nobody maintains decay; staff learn faster than policy changes. Review monthly for the first quarter.

## Common Mistakes
- Using the seller's number as the business case.
- Counting gross time saved and ignoring review and upkeep.
- Measuring after the novelty, or only on volunteers.
- Calling a pilot a success on a few friendly cases.
- No decision rule for stopping.

## Checklist
- [ ] One unit of work and one baseline
- [ ] Fixed period and comparison
- [ ] Costs include review, upkeep and training
- [ ] Errors and rework counted
- [ ] Stop and scale criteria fixed in advance
- [ ] Results written with sample size and dates
