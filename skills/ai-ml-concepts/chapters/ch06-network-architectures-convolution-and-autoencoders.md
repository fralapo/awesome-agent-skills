# Chapter 6: Network Architectures: Dense, Convolutional, Autoencoders and Generators

## Core Idea
An architecture is a set of structural assumptions that makes learning feasible for a kind of data. In this model, choose the architecture by asking what symmetry or structure the data has, then check whether the assumption costs you something elsewhere.

## 6.1 Dense (fully connected) networks
- **Thesis**: the simplest network connects every unit of a layer to every unit of the next; it is a universal building block and the base of the feed-forward part of larger models.
- **Mechanism**: matrix multiplication, bias, nonlinearity, repeated. Parameter count is the product of layer sizes: 784 inputs by 16 hidden units is already 12,544 weights.
- **Trade-off**: no built-in structure, so it needs much data and ignores that nearby pixels or words are related.
- **Does not hold when**: inputs are large images or long sequences; parameters and cost explode.

## 6.2 Convolutional networks
- **Thesis**: reuse one small detector at every position of the input.
- **Mechanism**: a kernel (a few weights) slides across the image; each output is the weighted sum of a local patch (the convolution operation of chapter 2). Early filters detect edges, later ones combine them into parts and objects. Weight sharing slashes parameters and gives translation-equivariance.
- **Trade-off**: strong bias that suits images, audio and local structure; long-range relations need many layers or other mechanisms.
- **Does not hold when**: the signal has no locality (set-like data) or the absolute position matters.

## 6.3 Autoencoders: compress to learn what matters
- **Thesis**: train a network to reproduce its input through a narrow bottleneck; the bottleneck code is a learned compact representation.
- **Mechanism**: same number of input and output units, a much smaller middle layer, loss on reconstruction error. Variants: denoising (reconstruct the clean input from a corrupted one), sparse, and variational (the code is a distribution that can be sampled to generate new examples).
- **Trade-off**: autoencoders were not better than classical codecs for plain compression; their value is the representation and generation.
- **Does not hold when**: the bottleneck is too wide (identity copy, nothing learned) or too narrow (blurry reconstructions).

## 6.4 Sparse autoencoders as an analysis tool
- **Thesis**: the same idea is used on a model's internal activations to look for the features it uses.
- **Mechanism**: reconstruct activations through a wide, sparse code; hope each code unit corresponds to one interpretable feature even when features are superimposed across raw neurons (chapter 11).
- **Trade-off**: gives readable units at some reconstruction error; the features it finds are partly a product of the method.
- **Does not hold when**: you treat found features as complete or causal without intervention tests.

## 6.5 Generative models in outline
- **Thesis**: a generative model learns a distribution over data so that new samples can be drawn; images, audio, video and text are generated by learned processes rather than copied.
- **Mechanism**: families include variational autoencoders, adversarial pairs, autoregressive models (text, next-token) and iterative denoising. A common recipe is to learn to remove noise step by step and run the process from pure noise.
- **Trade-off**: realism improves with scale and data; faithfulness to physics or facts does not follow automatically.
- **Does not hold when**: you need consistency of world state over long outputs (objects reappearing, physics): current generators still fail there, and more data does not guarantee an accurate world model. Families, objectives and trade-offs are in chapter 13.

## 6.6 Learned simulators
- **Thesis**: a network can be trained once on simulations to predict the next state, then run in milliseconds where the simulator needed minutes.
- **Mechanism**: show many fluid or smoke simulations; the network learns the dynamics well enough to roll forward. Training is an expensive pre-processing step; inference is cheap, like long exam preparation followed by fast answers.
- **Trade-off**: speed against guarantees: failure modes of hand-written physics can be read from the equations, those of a network are harder to predict; early results may not be peer-reviewed.
- **Does not hold when**: you need certified accuracy or the situation is far from the training simulations.

## 6.7 Choosing an architecture
- **Thesis**: match structure to data and constraints, then test.
- **Mechanism**: tables go to trees or boosting first; images to convolutions or attention; sequences and language to transformers (chapter 7); signals with periodic structure may benefit from frequency features; small data favours strong priors; deployment limits favour pruned or distilled models.
- **Trade-off**: simpler models are cheaper and easier to audit.
- **Does not hold when**: a general architecture plus enough data and compute beats handcrafted structure (chapter 12).

## 6.8 Depth, the degradation problem and skip connections
- **Thesis**: making a plain network deeper eventually raises its training error, and an identity shortcut around each block fixes it.
- **Mechanism**: past roughly twenty to thirty layers, plain convolutional stacks trained worse, not just generalised worse: a 74-layer plain network was beaten by an 8-layer one in an illustrative experiment, which also showed shattered gradients and a chaotic loss surface in early layers. Because it is the training error that rises, overfitting is not the explanation. A skip connection adds the block's input to its output, so each block learns a residual correction on top of an identity path; a hypothesis-driven check found smaller layer responses in residual nets, and the same illustrative network that stalled below 40 percent accuracy reached about 73 percent once skip connections were added (as reported at the time, one toy setting). Shortcuts that change dimension use zero padding or a 1x1 projection; projecting every shortcut adds parameters and gave little extra.
- **Trade-off**: very deep networks become trainable at no extra parameters in the identity case; the identity path is itself a strong inductive bias (each layer should only compute a difference).
- **Does not hold when**: the task is shallow enough that depth gives nothing, or when shortcuts must cross a shape change and need a projection.

## 6.9 The residual stream: layers refine, they do not form a strict chain
- **Thesis**: with skip connections the network is better described as a running sum that each block edits than as a hierarchy where every layer is indispensable.
- **Mechanism**: redraw the skip connections as one straight line from input to output; each block reads it and adds to it, so the output of a six-layer residual net is the input plus the contributions of each block. Removing or swapping a single layer from a 56-layer residual network barely hurt it and damage grew smoothly as more were removed, while the same surgery on a non-residual network was catastrophic. Transformers inherit this design: the running vector per token is the residual stream, and interpretability work (chapter 11) reads and edits it. Both pictures can be partly true (a separate study found evidence of both).
- **Trade-off**: robustness and easy gradient flow against less clean layer-by-layer stories.
- **Does not hold when**: a block performs an essential, non-redundant step; deleting it does hurt.

## 6.10 Scale, not a new idea, made the first deep convolutional networks work
- **Thesis**: the early-2010s jump in image classification came from data, compute and a few training tricks applied to an old idea.
- **Mechanism**: a large labelled image set (over a million images), graphics processors with about ten thousand times the compute available to a 1990s digit network, rectifier activations, dropout and augmentation. On the benchmark of the time the top-five error was about 17 percent against 25 to 28 percent for earlier methods (as reported at the time); removing a single middle layer cost roughly two points of top-one accuracy, so depth mattered. Parameters grew from tens of thousands to tens of millions, and frontier language models later grew by orders of magnitude more (figures approximate, as reported). The authors also noticed that results improved with every increase in size and speculated about unsupervised pre-training and video.
- **Trade-off**: large gains at the price of engineering (splitting across devices) and opacity.
- **Does not hold when**: data are scarce: then the older, smaller, prior-heavy models can win (5.5).

## 6.11 Embedding spaces: similar concepts sit close together
- **Thesis**: the vector one layer before the output is a learned embedding where distance means similarity of concept, not of pixels.
- **Mechanism**: for each image the next-to-last layer gives a vector of a few thousand numbers, a point in a latent space. The nearest neighbours of an elephant image are other elephants in different poses even though their pixel values differ greatly. Visualisations (atlases, feature visualisation by optimising an input to maximise an activation) show what a unit or direction responds to. Word vectors learned by prediction have the same property (chapter 14).
- **Trade-off**: reusable features for retrieval and classification; the space is high-dimensional and projections to two dimensions distort it.
- **Does not hold when**: you assume a clean, human-named axis for each direction (7.7).

## 6.12 Patches instead of convolutions: the vision transformer
- **Thesis**: an image can be cut into patches treated as tokens; a plain transformer then classifies it, without convolutions.
- **Mechanism**: split the image into 16-by-16 patches, flatten each, multiply by one learned matrix, add a position embedding, prepend a learned classification token, run a standard transformer and classify from that token's output. With very large pre-training datasets this matched or beat convolutional networks at a fraction of the training compute (about 2.5 thousand against about 9.9 thousand accelerator-days in one comparison, as reported at the time; that dataset was proprietary).
- **Trade-off**: fewer built-in assumptions (locality, translation invariance) means more data is needed to learn them; with enough data the freedom wins (position).
- **Does not hold when**: data are limited: convolutional priors then help. A commentator predicted that skip connections might be the next built-in bias to be relaxed (speculation).
