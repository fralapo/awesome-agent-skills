# Chapter 15: Reading Market Commentary and Forecasts Critically

> Educational method for reading opinions, not advice and not a guide to any product or person. A commentary is evidence about the speaker's reasoning and incentives; it is not evidence about the market until it is checked.

## Core Idea
In this model, a piece of market commentary mixes four kinds of content: **observations** (dated data), **mechanisms** (how the speaker thinks prices move), **forecasts** (what the speaker expects) and **recommendations or promotions** (what the audience should do or buy). Each needs a different test. Most errors come from treating the fourth or third category as if it were the first. The protocol below separates them, tests the forecasts against their record, and checks the incentives of the person speaking.

## 15.1 Separate the four kinds of content
- **Observation**: a figure with a date and a source. Checkable against the primary publication.
- **Mechanism**: a causal story ("liquidity drives risk assets", "fund selling amplifies the fall"). Checkable only if it names a measurable variable and says what would falsify it.
- **Forecast**: a level, a date, a direction or a probability. Checkable only after the date has passed, and only if it was recorded before.
- **Recommendation or promotion**: an instruction, a product, a course or a link. Its test is the incentive of the speaker (15.5), not the market.

A useful habit: rewrite each paragraph with a label on each sentence. Sentences that carry no label are usually narrative filler.

## 15.2 Dated claims and inferred dates
- **Thesis**: a price level, a target or a "current" figure is valid only on the date it was stated.
- **Mechanism**: many commentaries mention prices, targets and calendar events without a clear date. When the date is missing, it is often inferred from the content (the event mentioned, the release referenced). An inferred date can be wrong by months, and a wrong date can make an old call look current or a current call look old. Before quoting any level, establish its date from the source and re-check the figure against the primary series.
- **Trade-off**: an undated commentary can still be useful for the mechanism it describes, while its numbers are discarded.
- **Does not hold when**: the claim is a structural fact (a rule, a formula, a contract term) that does not depend on the date. Those can be checked directly.

## 15.3 Test forecasts against a record
- **Thesis**: a forecast is worth what its track record shows, measured on the same question over time.
- **Mechanism**: record each forecast with its date, its horizon, its exact wording and any probability. Score it when the horizon ends. Look at the whole set, not at the best item. Bold targets are common; the record shows whether the speaker's stated confidence matched their hit rate.
- **Patterns to notice**: a series of targets that move every time the price moves (adjusting to the price, not predicting it); a long list of "still valid" calls with no date for their failure; selective recall, in which the hits are repeated and the misses are not mentioned; a forecast reversed without an explanation.
- **Trade-off**: a record needs time and patience, and a short record says little. A speaker with a long, consistent record has still given a sample, not a guarantee.
- **Does not hold when**: the speaker makes a single event call that is pre-announced and binary; scoring such calls needs the base rate of the event (15.4).

## 15.4 Probability language and base rates
- **Thesis**: a claim such as "very likely" or "70%" has a meaning only if the speaker is calibrated, meaning that events called at a given probability happen at about that frequency.
- **Mechanism**: compare the stated probabilities with outcomes across many calls. Where no record exists, compare the claim with the base rate of the event class: how often did a similar move, in a similar setting, happen anyway? A claim that a rate cut or a rally is "likely" needs the frequency of that outcome in comparable periods (ch13 on sample size and rare events).
- **Trade-off**: base rates are estimated on different samples and can mislead for a unique situation.
- **Does not hold when**: the situation is unprecedented. Then the honest answer is "no base rate", not a number invented for the occasion.

## 15.5 Incentives and conflicts
- **Thesis**: the framing of commentary follows the incentives of the speaker. Declaring a conflict does not remove its effect.
- **Mechanism**: common incentives include affiliate links, sponsorship or marketing arrangements with an issuer or broker, paid courses or subscriptions, personal positions (declared or not), and an audience that rewards strong views. Each one tends to favour a particular conclusion. The rule in this model: read the commentary as the speaker's interest, and check whether the conclusion would change without the incentive.
- **Trade-off**: some speakers with incentives are still accurate about mechanisms. The incentive matters most for the forecasts and recommendations.
- **Does not hold when**: the incentive is absent and disclosed, and the speaker's claims are verified by a third party.

## 15.6 Narrative versus mechanism
- **Thesis**: a story explains a move after it has happened; a mechanism says what should happen next, and under what conditions it would fail.
- **Mechanism**: ask three questions. Which measurable variable carries the effect? What would the variable have to do for the story to be wrong? Has the same story been used for opposite moves? A story that explains both a rise and a fall, by different actors, is not a mechanism yet.
- **Trade-off**: narratives help people remember and act consistently. They are weak evidence about timing.
- **Does not hold when**: the mechanism is a well-established accounting or contractual identity; those do not need a narrative test.

## 15.7 Statistics inside commentary
- **Thesis**: numbers in commentary are often selected from windows that suit the claim.
- **Checks**: is the window stated, and does it start and end at convenient points? Is a win rate quoted without the average gain or loss (ch04 of `trading-methods-and-technical-analysis` on win rates)? Is a seasonal statistic quoted from few years, with a count of the windows scanned? Are "always" and "never" claims tested against the full history? Does a backtest report costs, out-of-sample results and the number of trades (ch13 of this skill)?
- **Trade-off**: a clean statistic is easy to remember and hard to verify. The verification is the cost of using it.
- **Does not hold when**: the statistic is an accounting fact from a primary source, reported with its definition.

## 15.8 Data quality of the source
- **Thesis**: a transcribed or summarised source can change a number or a unit.
- **Mechanism**: automatic transcripts, quick summaries and secondary articles introduce errors in names, currencies, decimals and dates. A figure read as one value in one summary and another in a second summary signals that neither is safe. Quote only what the primary source states, and mark any figure that could not be checked.
- **Trade-off**: checking everything is slow; prioritise figures that drive a decision.
- **Does not hold when**: the purpose is to understand a mechanism, not to use a number.

## 15.9 Protocol: eight questions before using any commentary
1. What kind of content is each claim: observation, mechanism, forecast or recommendation?
2. Is each date stated, and is the figure still current?
3. Is the forecast recorded, with a horizon and a probability, and what is its record?
4. What base rate applies to the event, and how does the claim compare with it?
5. What incentive does the speaker have, and does the conclusion depend on it?
6. Which measurable variable carries the mechanism, and what would falsify it?
7. Is the statistic's window, sample and cost stated?
8. Has the number been checked against the primary source?

If most answers are "no", the commentary can still illustrate an idea, but it cannot support a decision.

## 15.10 Positions in the debate (contested)
- **Whether forecasts by commentators add value**: the evidence on professional market predictions is mixed and frequently negative on the direction of markets, but better on the direction of data over short horizons (general knowledge; re-check the studies). This model does not settle it.
- **Whether a record of past calls predicts future calls**: some argue that skill persists; others that luck is mistaken for skill in small samples. The record is needed in either case.
- **Whether incentives always bias commentary**: some hold that disclosed incentives are harmless if the audience discounts them; the evidence on disclosure is not uniform.

## Common mistakes
- Quoting a level without its date.
- Treating a target as a forecast with a known hit rate.
- Counting a story that explains both outcomes as a mechanism.
- Accepting a win rate without the average gain or loss.
- Trusting a summary's figures without the primary source.
- Letting a declared conflict stop the critical reading.
