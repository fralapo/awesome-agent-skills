# Chapter 18: Tax Concepts That Change Returns

> Educational concepts, not tax or legal advice. No country's rates or thresholds are stated as current. Illustrative rates are labelled as such; for one country's dated rules see `personal-finance-italy`.

## Core Idea
In this model, tax changes what an investor keeps through four levers: **timing** (when gains are taxed), **netting** (which gains and losses offset), **base** (what the tax is charged on) and **leakage** (taxes withheld before the money arrives). Two investments with the same pre-tax return can end with different amounts after tax. The rules that set timing, base and netting usually matter more than the headline rate.

## 18.1 Realisation and deferral
- **Thesis**: when gains are taxed only on sale, unrealised growth compounds untaxed, and the benefit grows with the holding period.
- **Mechanism**: derived illustration with an illustrative rate of 25% on the gain (not any country's rate) and 8% gross growth. Holding without selling and paying one tax at the end leaves a factor of 1.08^n minus 25% of the gain: about 3.75 times the start after 20 years (after tax). Taxing each year's gain instead gives a growth of about 8% × (1 − 25%) = 6% a year, and about 3.21 times the start after 20 years. The gap widens with the horizon. A tax on each year's gain is therefore a different regime from a tax on realised gains, and the two must be compared on the same timing.
- **Trade-off**: deferral concentrates the bill at the moment of sale, which may fall in a high-income year or a year when money is needed.
- **Does not hold when**: the regime taxes unrealised gains each year, the holding sits in an exempt or yearly-taxed wrapper, or the gain is never realised because the asset passes through an estate under other rules (see 18.7).

## 18.2 Dividends and buybacks: equivalence without tax
- **Thesis**: without taxes and frictions, a payout as a dividend or as a share repurchase does not change the value of the holder's stake. A holder who sells part of the shares in a buyback receives the same value as one who receives a dividend.
- **Mechanism**: this is the Modigliani–Miller result (general knowledge). A dividend is a payment that reduces the price by about the same amount on the ex-date (chapter 04, 4.13); a buyback does the same for a holder who sells, and leaves the holder who does not sell with a larger share of the same company. With taxes, the cheaper route depends on how each is taxed and on the holder's ability to defer.
- **Position**: some argue dividends carry psychological value (regular cash, discipline) that the theory does not count; others argue that a dividend is only a choice of how to return cash, not a source of return. Both can hold.
- **Trade-off**: a dividend gives cash flow and can encourage spending; a buyback gives the holder control over timing of the tax.
- **Does not hold when**: taxes differ between dividends and gains, or transaction costs of selling are large.

## 18.3 Netting losses against gains
- **Thesis**: the tax base is the net of gains and losses within a defined pool, and the pool's rules decide what offsets what.
- **Mechanism**: regimes differ. Some net gains and losses within the same category of income; some allow losses to be carried forward for a limited number of years; some forbid offsetting a loss in one category against a gain in another; some apply anti-avoidance rules to sales made only to realise a loss (general knowledge; check local rules). A loss has value only if it can be used. An unusable loss is worth nothing, whatever it looked like on a statement.
- **Trade-off**: realising a loss lowers tax now, but the sale also changes the exposure and resets the cost basis. The benefit is mostly deferral, not a saving.
- **Does not hold when**: no gains exist to offset, or the regime does not allow the loss to be used.

## 18.4 Cost basis, lots and the taxable share of each sale
- **Thesis**: the tax on a sale depends on how much of the proceeds is gain, which depends on the cost basis of the units sold.
- **Mechanism**: tax equals rate × (proceeds − proportional cost basis). With a low basis, most of each sale is gain; with a basis near the market value, little is. Lot-selection rules (first-in-first-out, average cost, or specific identification where allowed) change which units count as sold and so change the tax. Some systems charge a percentage on the balance of an account, not on gains; then the base is the balance, and the charge is due even with no gain.
- **Trade-off**: specific identification can reduce the taxable amount but needs complete records.
- **Does not hold when**: the regime taxes a flat balance or the whole proceeds.

## 18.5 Withholding tax and treaty relief
- **Thesis**: a foreign dividend can be taxed at source before it reaches the investor. The rate and any reclaim depend on the country pair, the legal form of the holding and the documents the investor provides.
- **Mechanism**: the source country withholds a percentage of the dividend. The statutory default is often high, and treaties or the fund's domicile can reduce it. An investor may reclaim the difference through documentation, or may be unable to. The same index can therefore leak different amounts depending on the fund's domicile (chapter 16). Illustration only: a 30% default reduced to 15% by a treaty or domicile. Check the current treaty and the fund's documents before relying on any figure.
- **Trade-off**: a lower-withholding domicile may come with higher fees or a different replication method. Compare the total leakage, not the withholding alone.
- **Counter-view**: if the investor's home regime credits the foreign tax, the net leakage at home can be small. The result depends on the home regime.
- **Does not hold when**: the holding is in a tax-exempt account, or the foreign tax is fully creditable.

## 18.6 Charges on balances and wrappers
- **Thesis**: a charge on the balance of an account, and the tax treatment of a wrapper such as a pension or insurance product, are sets of rules on entry, growth and exit. Compare all three points, not the headline.
- **Mechanism**: a balance-based charge applies to the average or year-end balance above a threshold, whatever the return; a wrapper may deduct contributions, defer tax on growth and tax the benefit on exit. The logic of contributions versus direct investment is in chapter 14, section 14.8. Exit rules, penalties and minimum durations can outweigh the deferral.
- **Trade-off**: a wrapper trades flexibility for a tax position; the position is only worth the flexibility lost if the holding period is long and the rules stay in place.
- **Does not hold when**: the investor needs the money within the wrapper's restrictions, or the rules change before the planned exit.

## 18.7 Estates, gifts and deemed sales (concept)
- **Thesis**: regimes differ in whether unrealised gains are taxed at death, at the transfer of ownership or when a person leaves a country.
- **Mechanism**: some regimes reset the cost basis of inherited assets to market value, so heirs pay tax only on later gains; others carry the original cost forward or tax the accrued gain. Some tax gifts or inheritances above thresholds. An exit tax deems holdings sold when the holder changes tax residence. Design parameters of an exit tax include the threshold, the share due immediately, any instalment plan and guarantees, and whether losses offset gains. Position: such rules prevent the migration of gains out of a tax base; critics argue that they tax unrealised value and are hard to value and to enforce.
- **Trade-off**: a reset of basis at death favours heirs and reduces the lifetime tax on gains that were never realised; carry-over avoids that loss but taxes gains that were never sold.
- **Does not hold when**: the investor stays resident and the rules are not expected to change; in any case, check with a qualified professional.

## Common mistakes
- Comparing funds on pre-tax returns when their tax timing differs.
- Assuming an unusable loss has value.
- Treating withholding as an extra fee rather than a tax that may or may not be reclaimed.
- Taking a balance-based charge for a gain-based tax.
- Quoting a rate without its base, threshold and year.
