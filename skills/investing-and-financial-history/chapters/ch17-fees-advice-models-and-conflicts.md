# Chapter 17: Fees, Advice Models and Conflicts of Interest

> Educational concepts, not advice on any adviser, bank, insurer or product. Fee levels are illustrations or dated snapshots.

## Core Idea
In this model, cost is the one variable an investor can measure before deciding, and it compounds on the whole balance every year. The way an adviser, distributor or bank is paid determines which products they tend to recommend. Pay is not a verdict on quality; it defines the conflict the investor has to check. Chapter 04 gives the cost stack of a fund; this chapter covers the fees of the person or the wrapper selling it.

## 17.1 Fee drag as compounding arithmetic
- **Thesis**: a constant annual fee on the balance multiplies ending wealth by a factor below one, and the gap widens with the horizon.
- **Mechanism**: with gross annual return `g` and fee `f` charged on the balance, the ratio of ending values after `n` years is `((1 + g - f) / (1 + g))^n`. Worked examples (derived here, not taken from any product): at `g = 7%` over 30 years, a 1% fee leaves about 75% of the fee-free ending value, and a 2% fee leaves about 57%. Each fee is charged on a balance that has already compounded, so the drag grows over time.
- **Trade-off**: the arithmetic ignores what the fee buys (planning, service, behaviour). Whether a fee is worth paying is a separate question from how much it costs.
- **Does not hold when**: the fee is paid once, on a balance that is not compounding, or it buys a guarantee of known value that is priced separately.

## 17.2 Entry charges and the years needed to recover them
- **Thesis**: a one-off charge is equivalent to a lower annual return until it is recovered.
- **Mechanism**: an entry charge `c` reduces the invested amount to `(1 - c)`. Compare it with a product that has the same gross return, no entry charge and an annual cost `d` higher. The cheaper annual cost of the first product recovers the entry charge after `ln(1/(1 - c)) / ln(1 + d)` years. Derived example: a 3% entry charge equals about three years of a one-point annual cost gap. The calculation ignores interest on the charge itself and assumes the rest of the cost structure is equal.
- **Trade-off**: a short holding period makes entry charges dominant; a very long one makes annual charges dominant.
- **Does not hold when**: the holding period is long and the annual cost gap is large (then the annual cost decides), or the charge buys something measurable.

## 17.3 Fees as a share of the expected return
- **Thesis**: the same fee takes a larger share of a lower expected return.
- **Mechanism**: a 1% annual fee on a 6% expected gross return takes about one sixth; the same fee on a 3% bond return takes a third. A 2% fee on a 6% expectation takes a third. Converting the percentage to money helps: 1% of 500,000 is 5,000 a year, paid whether the year is good or bad.
- **Trade-off**: presenting the fee in money makes it concrete but depends on the balance, which changes; present both the percentage and the amount at a stated balance.
- **Does not hold when**: the expected return is high and the fee buys a service that is clearly needed.

## 17.4 How advisers and intermediaries are paid
- **Thesis**: each pay model carries its own conflict. No model is conflict-free, and the conflicts are different in kind.
- **Mechanism** (conflict to check against the trade-off):
  - *Percentage of assets under management*: charged yearly on the balance. It rewards keeping and growing the balance, and it discourages moves that shrink the base even when they are right. It becomes disproportionate on very large balances, where the percentage can exceed the value of the work.
  - *Fixed or flat fee*: charged per year or per plan, not on the balance. It rewards efficiency and does not penalise growth, but a small balance can pay a high cost per unit of service.
  - *Hourly or project fee*: charged on time or on a deliverable. It rewards time spent, so it needs a cost limit and a written scope.
  - *Commission or product-based pay*: paid by the product provider, or built into a sales charge or an annual charge that is shared with the seller. It rewards selling the products with the higher payouts, and the cost is often invisible to the investor.
  - *Subscription or platform*: a fixed fee for a tool or model portfolio. It rewards selling the subscription, and it may carry affiliate links or sponsored choices.
  - *Fee-only with no product payments*: commonly described as the low-conflict model. It still carries conflicts (for example over the level of the fee or over product recommendations made for the adviser's own platform), so it is not conflict-free.
- **Trade-off**: each model moves the conflict; read the total cost and every payment source, not the label.
- **Does not hold when**: the adviser sells no products, has no platform or affiliate link, and all payments are disclosed in writing.

## 17.5 Retrocessions and channel costs
- **Thesis**: when part of a product's charge goes to the seller, the investor pays a cost that the seller earns, often without seeing it as a separate line.
- **Mechanism**: a manufacturer issues the product and a bank or network distributes it. Part of the annual charge is passed to the distributor as a distribution fee, sometimes called a retrocession. Share classes sold through distribution channels can carry higher ongoing charges than the institutional or direct class of the same fund; the difference finances the channel (general knowledge; check the share class). An adviser who recommends the higher-cost class may be paid by it.
- **Trade-off**: channel fees pay for access, service and advice for small investors. The rival view is that the same service could be paid directly, and that hidden payments reduce the investor's power to compare (position).
- **Does not hold when**: the cheapest available class is the one recommended and any service fee is charged separately and transparently.

## 17.6 Insurance-wrapped and unit-linked products: layers of cost
- **Thesis**: a product that combines an insurance contract with investment funds has several cost layers, and the total is often well above the funds' own charges.
- **Mechanism**: entry costs (a percentage of contributions, often front-loaded), an annual management charge on the fund, the charges of underlying funds, an insurance or administration charge, and exit penalties (a surrender charge that declines over several years). The total cost can be expressed as the rate at which all charges reduce the investor's return; this is a result to compute from the contract, not a figure to take from a brochure. Brochure "net" returns are often stated after some layers and not others.
- **Counter-view**: insurance elements (a guarantee, a death benefit, a tax treatment in some systems) have value. Compare the guarantee's cost with the price of an equivalent guarantee bought separately, and only then decide whether the wrapper pays.
- **Does not hold when**: the guarantee is valued and priced separately and the investor holds the product to the end of its term.

## 17.7 Reading a cost disclosure
- **Thesis**: a total annual cost in money, over a stated horizon and balance, is more informative than the individual percentages.
- **Mechanism**: a complete disclosure covers entry, ongoing, transaction, performance and exit costs. It shows the money cost at a stated balance and a projection with and without costs, and it names a comparable product. Summary cost indicators in regulated documents have assumptions (holding period, balance, return) that should be read before the number is compared (general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: full disclosures are long; summaries can hide tail costs such as exit fees and performance fees with a hurdle.
- **Does not hold when**: the product has one flat, stated charge and nothing else.

## 17.8 Evidence on the value of advice (contested)
- **Thesis**: advice may be worth its cost through planning, behaviour and avoiding costly errors. The evidence that advice adds net return through product selection is weak.
- **Mechanism**: value can come from setting up a plan (pensions, debt, estate, tax-aware structure), from restraining decisions in downturns, and from consistent rebalancing. These effects are hard to see in performance tables, because the benchmark is usually an index and not the plan the investor would have followed alone.
- **Rival view**: much advice is sold on products and recent performance, and its net value after fees is negative. Studies of advised portfolios often report net underperformance against benchmarks, but the samples, benchmarks and the counterfactual are disputed (position).
- **Trade-off**: the price of a plan against the cost of an avoidable error. The second is large but rare; the first is certain.
- **Does not hold when**: the adviser sells no products and the fee buys a specific, fixed deliverable.

## 17.9 Questions to put in writing before paying anyone
1. What is my total yearly cost in money, from every source, at my balance?
2. Do you or your firm receive any payment from a product provider, a distributor or a platform?
3. Which share class or product do you recommend, and what does its total cost add up to?
4. Where are my assets held, in whose name, and what protection applies if the intermediary fails?
5. What does leaving cost: notice, penalties, transfer fees, and how long does it take?
6. What is written down about the recommendation, and who reviews it?

## Common mistakes
- Comparing fees only as percentages, without the money equivalent.
- Assuming a fee-only label removes every conflict.
- Judging advice only by the performance of the products it sells.
- Ignoring exit penalties on insurance-wrapped products.
- Accepting a "net" return that is stated after some cost layers and not others.
