# Chapter 11: Decisions Under Uncertainty

> Educational concepts and history, not investment advice or a forecast.

## Core Idea
In this model, a decision is judged by what was known and weighed **when it was made**, not by how it turned out. Because investing outcomes contain large amounts of luck, even the best process produces bad results sometimes, and a bad process produces good results sometimes. Learning from outcomes alone teaches the wrong lessons.

## 11.1 Good decision, bad outcome
- **Thesis**: separate *decision quality* from *outcome quality*.
- **Mechanism**: a good decision needs to know what risk is taken, why, and what outcome is expected. If you can state those at the time, you can later evaluate the decision. Taking market risk to earn the market premium to reach a goal is sensible regardless of the outcome of any given decade.
- **Examples, dated**: a retiree one year earlier or later, identical decisions, had 36% less wealth because of the starting period (ch01); an investor holding value stocks from 1980 faced about two decades of bad outcomes before the expected one appeared; looked at in March 2000, value had trailed growth for 5, 10, 15 and 20 years, and looked at in March 2001 it had beaten growth across all those windows (ch03). Owning an individual stock that doubles was a poor decision with a good outcome.
- **Trade-off**: process-focus can become an excuse (you can always call a loss "bad luck"). Counter: keep a written decision log with the expected distribution and revisit assumptions, not just results.
- **Does not hold when**: the same bad outcome repeats too often for luck to explain.

## 11.2 The three evaluation questions
- What risk am I taking?
- Why: what compensation do I expect, and is the compensation documented and persistent?
- What outcome do I expect, and over what period?
- Add: what would make me revise (a change in facts, not a change in price)?

## 11.3 Illusion of control and the outcome-based sales pitch
- **Thesis**: someone will always be doing better than your plan.
- **Mechanism**: there will always be a fund, stock, asset or cousin that beat the index; that result is used to sell the strategy. A good outcome for an active fund does not make it a good fund (ch03). Evaluate the process and the distribution of results, not the best story.
- **Does not hold when**: the outperformer has a verifiable, repeatable, cost-adjusted process.

## 11.4 Commitment and sticking with a strategy
- **Thesis**: a sound strategy only works if you can stay with it long enough.
- **Mechanism**: premiums arrive unevenly; the value tilt example shows a decade of lag within normal variation. Pre-commitment (written rules, automatic contributions, rebalancing bands) reduces the odds of abandoning at the bottom.
- **Trade-off**: persistence is also how bad strategies survive; ask whether the *reason* still holds, not only whether you are uncomfortable.
- **Does not hold when**: new information falsifies the reason for the strategy.

## 11.5 Sizing under uncertainty
- **Thesis**: size positions to survive being wrong.
- **Mechanism**: expected-utility thinking from decision theory says that for the same expected return a risk-averse investor should hold less of a volatile asset, especially when other wealth is correlated with it (your job, your business, your fund; ch04, 4.6). Averaging down on a loss is not a plan unless the original thesis still holds (rogue-trader cases, ch10).
- **Does not hold when**: ruin is impossible and gains are capped.

## 11.6 Forecasts and the limits of prediction
- **Thesis**: most market indicators (yield curve, valuation, narratives) give imperfect information on timing.
- **Mechanism**: a flat or inverted curve may precede a recession by a quarter or by years; valuations are only loosely related to near-term returns; a book on an imminent crash was on bestseller lists before 1987 without anyone predicting the date (ch09). Use indicators to check that your plan can survive the scenario, not to trade it.
- **Practical**: hold an emergency fund, diversified holdings and an allocation matching your tolerance (ch04).
- **Does not hold when**: you have a mandate for tactical decisions and an audited process.

## 11.7 Decision checklist (educational)
1. Write the goal, horizon and the need (ch01, 1.5).
2. Write the risk, the expected compensation and the evidence (ch02, ch03).
3. Check concentration and shared exposures (ch04).
4. Check price versus assumptions (ch05).
5. Check biases that apply (ch08).
6. Check for fraud signs if you cannot verify the structure (ch10).
7. Decide the rule for review and for what would change your mind.

## Common mistakes
- Judging a decision by the last result.
- Switching strategy right after the worst period.
- Confusing a story with a distribution.
