# Chapter 02: Market Efficiency and How Prices Form

> Educational concepts and history, not investment advice or a forecast.

## Core Idea
In this model, a price is the market's compressed estimate of discounted future cash flows, given risk. **Efficiency** is a claim about how fast and how well competition squeezes new information into that price. It is a benchmark and a hypothesis, not a promise that prices are "right".

## 2.1 What the hypothesis says
- **Thesis**: in an efficient market prices always fully reflect available information.
- **Mechanism**: competition for profit, low transaction costs and readily available information mean that anyone who knows an asset is worth more tomorrow buys today, pushing the price up until the edge is gone. New information is random, so price changes are random. Hence a *random walk*: an early statistical observation (about 1900) that prices wander, and a later theoretical explanation (about 1965) as a **fair game**: prices move because expectations adapt to news, not because of a pattern.
- **Trade-off**: the claim is deliberately strong so it can be tested; it was never "the market is perfect". Perfect efficiency is an ideal that real markets approach.
- **Does not hold when**: trading is costly or restricted, information is private, or participants cannot or will not arbitrage (limits to arbitrage, 2.5).

## 2.2 Testable predictions
- **Thesis**: efficiency predicts three observable things.
- **Mechanism**: (1) price changes are unpredictable from past prices; (2) professional managers on average do not beat the market after costs and good past records do not persist; (3) prices adjust quickly to news (event studies).
- **Trade-off**: evidence is broadly consistent with all three, but "broadly" is doing work: some pockets (small, illiquid, restricted markets) show slower adjustment.
- **Does not hold when**: the test uses a poor model of required returns (2.3).

## 2.3 The joint hypothesis problem
- **Thesis**: you can never test efficiency alone.
- **Mechanism**: any test asks two things at once: are prices efficient, and is my model of *normal* returns right? A return pattern that the model cannot explain is either a mispricing or a flawed model. Historically, patterns first labelled "anomalies" under a one-factor model (small stocks, cheap stocks, low-beta stocks) were later absorbed by multi-factor models, which moved the argument rather than ending it (see ch03).
- **Trade-off**: this makes efficiency unfalsifiable in the strict sense, which critics call a weakness and defenders call a feature of every theory that needs a model.
- **Does not hold when**: you can compare two assets with identical cash flows priced differently (rare, e.g. closed-end structure quirks, general knowledge).

## 2.4 Why efficiency matters to investors
- **Thesis**: it justifies the baseline strategy of owning the market cheaply.
- **Mechanism**: if price changes are unpredictable, forecasting them (stock picking, market timing) does not raise expected return, and it adds cost and risk. The case for broad low-cost index funds (ch03 for evidence, 2.6) follows.
- **Trade-off**: cheap and broad beats expensive and narrow on average, but says nothing about *which* broad portfolio or what mix.
- **Does not hold when**: you have an information or cost edge, or you have goals other than average return.

## 2.5 Rival views
- **Position, behavioural**: investors are boundedly rational, so mispricing can persist (ch08). Limits to arbitrage explain why it may not be corrected: an arbitrageur faces funding limits, mandates and the risk that the mispricing widens before closing. A manager who may be judged on short-term relative results will accept lower expected return to avoid long periods of lagging.
- **Position, reflexivity**: prices do not merely reflect fundamentals, they can change them (ch09). A rising price eases funding, which improves the firm, which lifts the price: a positive feedback loop that can run until it reverses. This is rival to efficiency, and also to behavioural finance if the latter only treats mispricing as an error without its effect on fundamentals.
- **Position, information paradox (general knowledge)**: if prices were perfectly efficient nobody would pay to gather information, so prices could not become efficient; markets must therefore be *efficient enough* that the marginal analyst roughly breaks even after costs. It is a conceptual rebuttal to "perfect" efficiency, not to the practical case for cheap diversification.
- **Does not hold when**: the claim is that nothing ever misprices; no serious version says so.

## 2.6 The arithmetic of active management
- **Thesis**: before costs, the average active dollar earns the average market return; after costs it earns less.
- **Mechanism**: the market is the sum of active and passive holders, so the average over all holders equals the market. If passive holders earn the market return minus a very low fee, active holders as a group earn the market return minus a larger fee and trading cost. Active management is therefore a zero-sum game before costs and a negative-sum game after.
- **Evidence as stated, dated**: weighted average fee about 0.19% for index funds against about 0.85% for active funds without advice (Canada, about 2024); a 1991-2020 study of US equity funds found about 54.8% underperformed an index fund even before their fees and about 30.3% beat it after fees; a periodic scorecard reported fewer than 6% of US equity funds beat a broad benchmark over 20 years to mid-2024. Early work (1968) on 115 managers (1945-1964) found no skill beyond chance.
- **Why before-fee underperformance (see 3.5)**: skewed stock returns make a concentrated subset more likely to miss the few big winners.
- **Persistence**: past top performers rarely repeat. Two explanations: luck, and skill that is real but is captured by the manager through fees as the fund grows (diseconomies of scale).
- **Trade-off**: you give up the chance of a standout win, and (position) you accept the market's drawdowns.
- **Does not hold when**: a market segment is illiquid or inefficient, or the fund is cheap and specialised in a documented factor (ch03).

## 2.7 Anecdotes versus evidence
- **Thesis**: a famous winner is an anecdote, not a refutation.
- **Mechanism**: in a field with no controlled trials, large datasets and out-of-sample tests play the role of evidence; testimony of successful investors is at the bottom of the hierarchy. Asking *why one person won* has no scientific answer, like asking why one smoker lived to 98. In this model, a celebrated track record can often be restated as exposure to known risk factors plus leverage and discipline, which is the joint hypothesis again.
- **Trade-off**: the framework explains averages and distributions, not individuals.
- **Does not hold when**: a track record is long, audited, and survives factor adjustment (very rare).

## 2.8 Price changes with valuation: the long-run lies
- **Thesis**: part of any long-run outperformance is a change in valuation, which cannot repeat forever.
- **Mechanism (dated, as stated)**: from 1980 to 2020 one large market beat developed markets outside it by about 2% a year, but after adjusting for the rise in its price relative to cyclically adjusted earnings only about 0.4% a year remained. A 2022 study framed the extra return as partly luck (disasters that did not occur) and partly learning (investors judging it safer). Expected return falls as price rises: a noisy relationship, "not gravity".
- **Trade-off**: knowing this does not tell you when valuations will revert.
- **Does not hold when**: a structural change really does lower required returns.
