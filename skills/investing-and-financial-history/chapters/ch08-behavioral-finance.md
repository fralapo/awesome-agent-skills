# Chapter 08: Behavioural Finance

> Educational concepts and history, not investment advice or a forecast.

## Core Idea
In this model, people economise on thinking: they take **bounded rationality** shortcuts, accepting a satisfactory answer instead of the optimal one. In daily life that is efficient; in markets the shortcuts become systematic, predictable errors. Behavioural finance studies those errors. It is a rival to, and sometimes a complement of, the efficient-market view (ch02).

## 8.1 Bounded rationality versus utility maximisation
- **Thesis**: traditional theory assumes people compute probabilities and maximise utility; behavioural theory says they satisfice.
- **Mechanism**: like accepting the first discounted item without checking other shops, investors stop researching when an answer feels good enough. Shortcuts let us recognise patterns fast, but patterns in randomness mislead.
- **Trade-off**: understanding your own limits lets you design around them (rules, defaults, automation).
- **Does not hold when**: stakes are small or the decision is repeated with fast feedback.

## 8.2 Cognitive errors: belief perseverance
- **Thesis**: once we hold a view we defend it.
- **Mechanism**: **confirmation bias** (weighing supportive evidence more), using one favourable report while ignoring ten warnings; **illusion of control** (overestimating influence on outcomes, strongest when you did a lot of research or work at the company); **overconfidence** (feeling that thoughts and effort affect a share price).
- **Does not hold when**: you commit in advance to disconfirming tests.

## 8.3 Cognitive errors: information processing
- **Thesis**: framing changes decisions.
- **Mechanism**: **mental accounting** (treating money differently depending on its label, e.g. a "bonus" versus "savings"); **anchoring** (fixating on a past price or purchase price); **recency / extrapolation** (assuming recent performance continues, shown by retirement-plan contributions to employer stock rising with its ten-year performance).
- **Does not hold when**: relevant base rates are known and used.

## 8.4 Emotional and preference errors
- **Thesis**: feelings drive trades.
- **Mechanism**: **loss aversion** (a loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases); **disposition effect** (selling winners too early and holding losers hoping to break even; many losers never recover); **endowment effect** (valuing what you own above what you would pay: would you buy it today at this price?); **familiarity** (equating the known with the safe, as with employer stock); **herding and fear of missing out**; **performance chasing** (buying what recently did well, shown for retail investors across funds and countries).
- **Trade-off**: some biases protect against ruin; the same instinct that avoids loss also sells the bottom.
- **Does not hold when**: rule-based processes remove the decision.

## 8.5 Behaviour and the value premium (link)
- **Position**: the same biases are offered as one explanation for factor premiums (ch03): glamour is overpaid for, neglect is underpriced. Limits to arbitrage allow it to persist.
- **Does not hold when**: the premium is a risk premium (the two accounts are rival, not exclusive).

## 8.6 Narrative, social proof and community
- **Thesis**: assets whose price depends on belief build a "community" around the holding.
- **Mechanism**: owners are encouraged to promote the asset to each other, which is functionally recruitment; ordinary equities rarely come with a community that talks about them all day. Celebrity endorsements and club-like access are marketing, not evidence of value (ch10).
- **Does not hold when**: a community also reports real, verifiable use and cash flows.

## 8.7 Mitigation (practical, educational)
- **Mechanism**: write the reason for a decision before taking it; separate *decision quality* from *outcome* (ch11); use pre-committed rules (rebalancing, a cap on single positions); run a checklist to look for disconfirming evidence; delay big moves after news; avoid checking prices constantly.
- **Does not hold when**: you cannot follow the rule when it hurts; then simplify the rule.

## Common mistakes
- Believing you are immune because you know the list.
- Treating hindsight clarity as proof the signal was visible.
