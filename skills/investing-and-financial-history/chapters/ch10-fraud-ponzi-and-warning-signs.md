# Chapter 10: Fraud, Ponzi Schemes and Warning Signs

> Educational concepts and history, not investment advice or a forecast. Case summaries are brief and dated; allegations are described as such.

## Core Idea
In this model, fraud works by **exploiting the shortcuts that make trade possible**. Society runs on default trust: nobody checks every fact before every transaction. A fraud sells a story that matches what people already want to believe, and its survival depends on a flow of new money or on investors choosing not to verify.

## 10.1 Why we trust
- **Thesis**: trust is economically necessary and has an optimal level above zero.
- **Mechanism**: checking everything would be as absurd as inspecting a cow's health before each bottle of milk. Basic due diligence would expose most deceptions, but there are too many facts to check. Repeated small transactions discipline sellers (a bad milk seller loses the customer); **one large lump-sum** investment does not, so large frauds target one-off, high-value, unverifiable deals.
- **Paradox**: a high-trust society attracts more fraud and also enjoys more commerce, because it does not need personal acquaintance for every deal; a low-trust society has less fraud but less business. The optimum is not zero fraud.
- **Does not hold when**: stakes are high and verification is cheap; then trust is negligence.

## 10.2 The anatomy of a Ponzi scheme
- **Thesis**: a Ponzi scheme pays earlier investors with money from later investors and promises returns that no real activity generates.
- **Mechanism**: (1) a story of exclusive or special returns; (2) early investors are paid and testify; (3) the flow of new money must grow to cover promised payments; (4) a shock (a market fall, a wave of redemptions, an investigation) breaks the flow and the structure collapses. Returns that are smooth, high and independent of the market are a hallmark.
- **Variants**: affinity fraud (a community vouching for each other); a fabricated colony or territory (one 1820s case sold land and a made-up currency in a place that did not exist and caused many deaths); sale of assets that do not exist; forged documents.
- **Does not hold when**: a business pays dividends from real profits.

## 10.3 Accounting and corporate fraud
- **Thesis**: big corporate frauds combine a complex structure, a charismatic frontman and attacks on critics.
- **Mechanism, case (dated)**: a payments firm reported revenue from third-party partners that investigative journalists found had a handful of staff and no relationship with the card networks; the firm booked fake transactions for about a decade; about 1.9 billion euros said to be in trustee accounts turned out not to exist; the firm filed for insolvency in 2020. Features: a hybrid business that is hard to compare with peers; adjusted, non-standard financial statements; acquisitions of small companies through "oddly structured" deals; reliance on a single reputable auditor and prestigious holders; complaints about short sellers; a regulator that banned short selling of the stock and filed complaints against reporters rather than investigating the firm.
- **Mechanism, case (dated)**: a blood-testing startup claimed hundreds of tests from one drop; it raised over 700 million dollars, was valued at about 9 billion at its peak (dated), had a board of prominent people with no medical experience, used outside labs while claiming proprietary tests, and sent inaccurate results to real patients; it was exposed by investigative reporting and shut down; its founder was later convicted (2022). A coffee chain in China reported inflated sales (as much as 200 million dollars a quarter) and was valued at several billion dollars at its peak (dated) with the support of major investors (dated).
- **Mechanism, case (dated)**: a crypto exchange and its trading arm, praised in a bank's customer testimonial, collapsed in days in 2022 after a competitor's disclosure; billboards and celebrity endorsements were marketing, hidden losses and dead assets were the substance.
- **Trade-off**: innovators must pitch the future, but must tell investors what the technology does *today*, not what it may do someday (a regulator's summary).
- **Does not hold when**: skepticism can itself be wrong: some short sellers lie, so check the evidence, not the messenger.

## 10.4 Rogue traders
- **Thesis**: unauthorised trading grows when losses are hidden and the same person controls trading and settlement.
- **Mechanism, case (dated)**: a futures trader in Singapore, who also ran back-office settlement, put errors into a hidden account, doubled down after losses ("the market only has to retrace half as far"), and lost more than the bank's capital in 1995, which brought down the oldest merchant bank in the UK. A natural-gas trader at a hedge fund bet on calendar spreads, with a position so large that it moved the market; the fund collapsed in 2006.
- **Mechanism, general**: concentration of roles; averaging down; hidden accounts; managers who fail to ask questions because the trader is profitable.
- **Does not hold when**: roles are split, positions reconciled independently, and limits enforced.

## 10.5 Fraud in a crisis
- **Thesis**: rapid, large, lightly supervised spending attracts fraud.
- **Mechanism, dated**: in a pandemic relief programme, agencies not designed to pay out aid, old IT systems and few restrictions on applicants led to an estimated 280 billion dollars stolen and 123 billion wasted or misspent, about 10% of 4.2 trillion distributed (as stated in an analysis of the time). After disasters fraudulent donation sites and scam emails routinely spring up.
- **Does not hold when**: payouts are staged and verified.

## 10.6 Why smart people fall for it
- **Thesis**: confidence, emotion, authority and social proof override analysis.
- **Mechanism**: people who feel too smart to be fooled are likelier victims; fear, anger and greed shut down analysis; investors believed *prestige* (famous backers, famous auditors) over visible facts; when an investor presented photos of empty offices a fund manager had a secretary call the number and "all was well" because someone answered; investors reacted emotionally because they did not want to believe they had been fooled. Social proof, deference to authority and loyalty to an early position are strong. The more innovative the business and the stronger the narrative, the more problems are overlooked.
- **Does not hold when**: you have a verification procedure independent of the seller.

## 10.7 Warning-sign checklist (educational)
- Returns that are high, steady and uncorrelated with markets.
- Complex structure or opaque revenue source that you cannot trace to a customer.
- Non-standard "adjusted" metrics, hard-to-compare business model.
- Related-party deals, odd acquisitions, missing cash.
- Management that attacks critics, short sellers or reporters instead of answering.
- Authority (names, boards, celebrities) offered instead of evidence.
- Pressure to act fast, to keep it secret, or to bring in friends.
- One person controls recording, execution and reporting.
- Access to funds depends on one custodian or document you cannot independently confirm.
- Dependence on new money to pay old promises.
- You feel strong emotion (excitement, fear of missing out) about it.
- Nothing here proves fraud; it is a prompt to verify.

## 10.8 What to do instead (conceptual)
- Verify independently: the existence of the customers, assets, and custodian; do not rely on the seller's contacts.
- Separate roles and reconcile.
- A common principle is to hold investments whose structure can be explained in a sentence.
- A common principle is to size positions so that any single failure is survivable (ch04).

## 10.9 Impersonation, fake endorsements and deepfakes
- **Thesis**: modern scams borrow a trusted face rather than build trust slowly.
- **Mechanism**: the usual chain is a fake endorsement (a cloned profile, a group chat, an advertisement with a synthetic voice or face of a public figure), then a platform that shows invented gains, then withdrawals that require "tax", "fee" or "verification" payments that escalate until the victim stops. Sunk-cost thinking makes the victim pay one more fee; later waves target the same person with "recovery services" asking an advance fee. Mass messages are written badly on purpose: they filter for the naive, whose time is cheap to exploit. Romance variants invest months because attacker time is cheap. Real withholding is deducted from a payout, never prepaid; a payout released by paying first is a tell.
- **Mechanism, cheap checks**: nobody seeks you out to offer a profitable investment; urgency is the lever; call back on a number you found yourself; read the real domain backwards from the first single slash; two confirmations arriving through the same channel count as one; caller names and display names can be spoofed; a transfer is not checked against the account holder's name. Realistic guaranteed returns track the low-risk rate of the day, so a promised "guaranteed" return far above it (a figure around 10% is a heuristic threshold) cannot be sustained. Prefer intermediaries listed in an official register.
- **Does not hold when**: you initiated contact with an authorised firm through channels you verified.

## 10.10 Pyramid and referral structures, including in digital assets
- **Thesis**: a structure that pays members for recruiting members fails by arithmetic.
- **Mechanism**: with 5 recruits per member, level 10 needs 5^10, about 9.8 million people, so most late joiners lose. Variants (general knowledge) present themselves as staking, trading bots, "membership" tokens or yield programmes with daily returns and referral bonuses; in the digital-asset form, value is the token itself, which the operator can print, so the story that "it is not a pyramid because there is a token" does not change the flow of money. Smart contracts can keep paying while the token underneath collapses: the contract does not fail, the currency does.
- **Does not hold when**: a bonus for a genuine referral is a small, capped marketing cost and income does not depend on recruiting.

## 10.11 Yield, platforms and counterparty risk
- **Thesis**: a yield far above the market is the price of a risk, and a platform adds its own risk to the underlying one.
- **Mechanism**: a platform promising 8-10% on deposited stable assets must lend or deploy the funds; clients bear the underlying risk plus the platform's insolvency, mismatches between the currency of liabilities and assets, and a split of creditors across related entities. In a reported failure (illustrative), liabilities of about 377 million units against assets mostly in risky positions implied a recovery near a quarter, with administrator costs paid by creditors (uncertain figures). In credit the upside is capped at coupons and principal while the loss can be total; a typical recovery in default was about 10% of principal (illustrative), so a 58% fall on a bond can hurt more than a 90% fall on a single share because a fixed income was expected. Small-ticket lending and crowdfunding carry the same risk with strong information asymmetry: a platform's guarantee is not independent of the platform; diversifying across look-alike borrowers does not remove it (10 defaults in 100 loans is 10% of capital, often the whole promised return); that others have joined is not due diligence; and a return that looks guaranteed is a warning sign (position). Funds blocked at a platform may not be recognised as a loss for tax purposes (jurisdiction-specific).
- **Does not hold when**: collateral is segregated, audited and held outside the platform.

## 10.12 Additional red flags
- A product that cannot be explained in a sentence or whose returns cannot be traced to a counterparty.
- A deposit asked into a third party's account.
- Platforms where only the operator can freeze withdrawals, or where withdrawal needs a fresh payment.
- A fee ladder: layered entry charges, fund-of-fund costs and surrender penalties that consume a large share of expected return (ch04, 4.12).
- Frequent switching proposed by a seller (more than about once a year) and a mixed product that trails a mix of indices by its costs.
- Authority borrowed from a regulator's logo, a bank's name in a testimonial or a famous backer.

## Common mistakes
- Treating a credible name as due diligence.
- Believing that fraud only happens in obscure places.
- Paying a "release fee" to recover money already lost.
- Confusing a working smart contract with a sound asset.
