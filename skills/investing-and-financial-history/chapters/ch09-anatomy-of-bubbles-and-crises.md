# Chapter 09: Anatomy of Bubbles and Crises

> Educational concepts and history, not investment advice or a forecast. Causes are *proposed*; economists disagree, and cases carry the rival account.

## Core Idea
In this model, bubbles and crises share a small set of ingredients: a **real change** that justifies enthusiasm, **cheap or abundant credit**, a **new financing story** that detaches price from cash flow, **leverage and interlinked promises**, and a **trigger** that turns a self-reinforcing loop into a self-reinforcing collapse. Individual cases vary in which ingredients dominate.

## 9.1 Reflexivity: the feedback loop
- **Thesis (position, rival to efficiency)**: market prices can **change the fundamentals** they are supposed to reflect.
- **Mechanism**: **negative feedback** is self-correcting (a high oil price cuts demand, raises supply and brings the price back). **Positive feedback** is self-reinforcing: a rising share price lowers funding costs, which lets the firm grow, which raises the price. A positive loop eventually reaches a climax (a reversal point) and then feeds on itself in the opposite direction. Two cardinal principles: prices distort fundamentals, and markets have an active role in shaping them. Both can be aborted at any time by intervention or negative feedback.
- **Mechanism, benign case**: abundant cheap capital let a retailer undercut rivals who had to show profits; a later cloud-infrastructure business supplied real earnings, so no bust followed. The same loop can end well or badly.
- **Trade-off versus behavioural finance**: behavioural work explains the mispricing; reflexivity adds the effect of mispricing on real outcomes.
- **Does not hold when**: price has no channel to fundamentals (e.g. a mature firm with fixed funding).

## 9.2 A generic bubble anatomy (synthesis)
1. A real innovation or policy shift (new technology, new financial instrument, debt consolidation).
2. Easy money: low rates, new credit channels.
3. Early winners and a story (stage of "this time is different").
4. Metrics replaced: profit and valuation dismissed, growth or price becomes the metric.
5. Leverage and new entrants (loans to buy stocks, mortgage loans to marginal borrowers).
6. Fragility: interlinked liabilities, little capital behind the promises.
7. Trigger: rate rise, news, a large failure, a loss of confidence.
8. Feedback loop in reverse: forced selling, runs, credit freeze.
- **Does not hold when**: a boom is justified by results (hard to know in advance).

## Case cards
Each card: what happened, ingredients, proposed causes, rival account, lesson. Details marked "(general knowledge)" are background context.

### Card A: South Sea Company (Britain, 1711-1720)
- **What**: a government in heavy war debt swapped its debt for shares of a company with a monopoly on a trade with a region that the country was at war with; the company later arranged more swaps as its price rose.
- **Mechanism**: so long as the share price rose, the company could issue shares to creditors at a premium and profit from the swap; the price became the engine of the scheme, not trade. Rumour and political events lifted the price; competing speculation in a French scheme (Card B) added momentum.
- **Proposed causes**: debt restructuring dressed as an investment; political patronage; insider gains; weak disclosure. The collapse in 1720 (general knowledge) hurt many investors.
- **Rival account**: some historians argue parts of the swap were rational debt management and the mania was one among several (general knowledge).
- **Lesson**: when issuing new securities depends on the old price, the price is a funding mechanism and any halt is fatal.

### Card B: The Mississippi scheme (France, about 1716-1720)
- **What**: a financier introduced paper money, a state bank and a company with rights over a colony and tax collection; shares soared; "millionaire" entered the language.
- **Mechanism**: fractional reserve and paper money were ideas that worked in principle; money can be multiplied beyond metal supply, as bankers already did (a goldsmith lends part of deposits, keeping reserves); the weakness is that an outside event can trigger simultaneous withdrawals, leaving reserves exhausted. The scheme tied the value of the notes to the shares, so a share fall became a currency crisis (general knowledge for the collapse).
- **Proposed causes**: overissue of notes and shares; linking a bank, a trading company and public debt in one structure; hype about a colony that did not exist as advertised.
- **Rival account (general knowledge)**: some economists credit the architect with sound monetary insights (credit money does support commerce) while condemning the implementation.
- **Lesson**: good ideas fail when their financing is pyramided on belief.

### Card C: The 1920s boom and the Great Depression (US, 1920-1932)
- **What**: postwar prosperity, consumer goods, easy credit; ordinary people began investing, then borrowing to invest; banks speculated with customer funds; wealth doubled and stocks rose about 218% from 1922 to 1929.
- **Mechanism**: production and earnings slowed while prices kept rising; interest rates rose; two crash days in October 1929 (record volume, falls of about 12-13% on each of two days) turned panic into forced selling by leveraged holders; the Dow fell about 90% from its peak over about three years.
- **Proposed causes (contested)**: speculative excess and margin debt; tight monetary policy and bank failures; collapse in the money supply; trade restrictions (the last three general knowledge).
- **Rival account**: whether the crash caused the depression or revealed an economy already weakening is debated ("the theorised start" of the depression).
- **Lesson**: leverage plus contagion spreads a market loss into the real economy through banks.

### Card D: The 1987 crash
- **What**: the Dow fell 508 points, about 22.6% in a day (19 Oct 1987), the largest one-day fall in the record; no recession followed and the market recovered within about two years.
- **Mechanism, proposed**: (1) valuations had risen faster than earnings after a five-year bull market; (2) news days before (a proposal to end tax breaks for takeover financing, a larger trade deficit, a weaker currency, talk of devaluation); (3) a large options and futures expiry compounded stress; (4) **portfolio insurance**, a synthetic option strategy that sold as prices fell, and index arbitrage linked the futures and cash markets; order systems were overwhelmed and trades were reported late.
- **Rival account**: portfolio insurance probably explains a part, not all: it was about 19% of sell volume on the day by one estimate (about 6 of 31 billion dollars), and other markets without such products fell as much or more. An alternative is that after a long bull market everybody was primed to read a decline as "the crash", so price falls prompted selling (a reflexive explanation).
- **Lesson**: strategies that look like insurance for each holder can be destabilising when many follow the same rule; liquidity is not guaranteed.

### Card E: Long-Term Capital Management (1998)
- **What**: a large hedge fund run by renowned academics and traders lost most of its capital and required a coordinated rescue (general knowledge on details).
- **Mechanism (general knowledge)**: leveraged bets that related prices would converge; a regional crisis widened spreads, margin calls forced sales.
- **Lesson**: partners had most of their savings in the fund *and* owned the management company, so the effective concentration was even higher than the stated 80%; a simple expected-utility analysis would have advised a smaller stake (ch04, 4.6).
- **Rival**: investors prefer to see managers with skin in the game; some concentration is rational for control.

### Card F: The dot-com bubble (1993-2002)
- **What**: a user-friendly browser made the internet accessible; web traffic rose about 1000% in a month; the number of users rose from about 2.6 million (1990) to 45 million (1995) and more than doubled each year; a browser firm went public in 1995 and its price roughly doubled on the first day; an online seller of airline tickets listed in 1999 with a 10 billion market value after large early losses; a social site closed up 606% on the first day in 1998, and later fell to pennies.
- **Mechanism**: low rates; profitability dismissed in favour of growth, revenue, "eyeballs"; spending on advertising and acquisitions to grow; analysts and bankers vouched for losses as future profits; companies added a ".com" to their names; a fear (the Y2K bug) triggered equipment spending; first-day gains of hundreds of percent; people left jobs to trade. By late 1999 the tracked internet stocks had a market value of about 450 billion dollars against annual sales of about 21 billion, and combined losses of about 6 billion.
- **Proposed causes**: real technological change, loose monetary policy, a new IPO machine (banks raising the offer price repeatedly), conflicted research.
- **Rival account (general knowledge)**: some argue the infrastructure built was valuable and the bubble financed it; several survivors became extremely large.
- **Lesson**: real innovation and a bubble are not exclusive.

### Card G: The 2008 financial crisis
- **What**: the largest shock to the global financial system in almost a century; a mortgage lender failed in August 2007, a fund suspension followed, a British bank suffered the first retail run there in well over a century.
- **Mechanism**: after the dot-com crash and 2001 the policy rate went to 1%, the real short rate was negative for 31 months; investors hunted for yield; house prices rose about 124% from 1998 to 2006 and the price-to-income ratio rose from about 3 to about 4.6; lenders sold mortgages on rather than holding them, lost the incentive to screen, and eased criteria progressively; mortgages were pooled into securities and into riskier tranches (CDOs) and synthetic versions; rating agencies paid by issuers gave top ratings; insurers sold credit default swaps with far too little capital behind them; subprime borrowers defaulted from 2007 (by October 2007 about 3% of loans were in foreclosure and 7% a month past due), and prices of the securities fell.
- **Proposed causes (contested)**: banks and financial innovation; borrowers who overstated income; loose monetary policy; regulation and incentives; rating agency conflicts; a government push to expand mortgage lending through its sponsored enterprises.
- **Lesson**: when each link in a chain is paid for volume and can pass the risk to the next, nobody holds it until it lands on a balance sheet unable to bear it.
- **Link**: monetary and sovereign context in `economics-finance`.

### Card H: Regional bank runs (2023)
- **What**: a crypto-focused bank wound down after customers withdrew billions of dollars over a quarter (Q4 2022; verify, dated); later a large regional lender lost more than 100 billion dollars of deposits in one quarter and was sold to a bigger bank with a loss-sharing deal (dated; amounts as stated at the time).
- **Mechanism**: a business model unprepared for rising interest rates (long-dated bonds or fixed-rate mortgages at a loss), deposits above insurance limits, and a client base that all acted together; a coordinated deposit of about 30 billion dollars by large banks did not stop the run, and the regional lender was sold roughly six weeks later (dated).
- **Proposed causes**: rate risk management, supervision, social-media-speed runs.
- **Lesson**: for a bank, confidence is part of capital; runs can be fast when depositors are few and informed.

### Card I: An AI infrastructure build-out (2023-2026, dated and unsettled)
- **What**: a surge in spending on chips, data centres and model training by model developers, cloud providers and chip makers, accompanied by very large share-price gains and, at times, sharp single-day moves (one cloud provider rose about 30 percent in a day on contract news; another fell about 10 percent in one session when its backlog was found to depend heavily on one customer; dated snapshots, unverified here).
- **Mechanism**: (1) circular financing: a chip maker invests in a model developer, the developer buys cloud capacity, and the cloud provider buys the chips, so one dollar can be counted as revenue by several parties; (2) backlog and contracted-revenue figures (remaining performance obligations) count signed contracts not yet delivered or paid, so their value depends on the buyer's ability to fund them; (3) the useful life assumed for the chips determines reported profit (5.6); (4) the builders of an infrastructure are not necessarily the firms that capture its profits (the networking-equipment boom of 2000, whose leader took over 20 years to regain its peak); (5) subscription software was sold off on the fear that AI could replace it.
- **Proposed causes of valuation**: real demand and productivity gains, scarcity of advanced chips, competition among giants not to be left behind.
- **Rival account**: some hold that each major technology had a bubble without disappearing (railways, radio, internet; an online retailer lost 95 percent in 2000 and survived), that only second-tier startups are overvalued, and that the established firms are funded by cash flow; others hold that circularity, long asset lives and unfunded contracts are the 1990s pattern (9.2 steps 4 and 6). Whether the firms' spending earns a return is not yet known.
- **Signs of euphoria (not predictions)**: firms changing business to the fashionable theme and gaining several hundred percent on the announcement; no profit but a story (compare Card F).
- **Lesson**: real innovation and a bubble are not exclusive; list who pays whom, check cash against reported profit, and keep direction and timing separate.

## 9.3 Reading a boom (checklist, educational)
- Is the price being justified by cash flow or by the next buyer?
- Is new issuance financing the old price?
- Is credit growth faster than income?
- Are metrics being changed to keep the story alive?
- Who is paid by volume?
- If a loop reverses, who is forced to sell?
- None of this predicts timing.

## 9.4 Testing a "this time is different" claim
- **Thesis**: the phrase is not wrong in itself; a claim that a regime has changed is testable only if it names the variable that changed and how it would be measured.
- **Mechanism**: a valid claim of a structural break specifies (1) the mechanism (for example a lower discount rate, a new source of cash flow, a change in the rules of lending or of index membership), (2) an observable that should have moved with it (cash flows, default rates, margins, supply), and (3) what would show the claim to be false. Without those three, the phrase repeats a story that earlier episodes also used. Past booms that were explained as new eras often had a real innovation alongside a financing loop.
- **Trade-off**: some regime changes are real (the rise of index funds, lower transaction costs). Rejecting every claim of change would miss them.
- **Does not hold when**: the claim is backed by measured changes in the variable it names and survives the falsification test.

## Common mistakes
- Treating the trigger as the cause.
- Assuming a boom is a bubble because it looks like one: only afterwards is it known.
