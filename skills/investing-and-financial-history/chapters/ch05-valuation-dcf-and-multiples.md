# Chapter 05: Valuation: DCF and Multiples

> Educational concepts and history, not investment advice or a forecast.

## Core Idea
In this model, an asset is worth the **cash it will give its owner from now on**, discounted for time and risk. Price compared to that worth is a *question*, not an answer: every valuation is as good as its assumptions.

## 5.1 The discounted cash flow (DCF) logic
- **Thesis**: a going-concern business is worth the present value of its future free cash flows.
- **Mechanism**: imagine buying a machine that prints money: you do not care about its colour or what it printed for the previous owner, only about what it will give you from now on. So forecast cash for years 1 to N, add a **terminal value** that summarises all later years (a growing perpetuity or an exit multiple), and discount every amount by a rate that reflects (a) the **time value of money** (cash later is worth less) and (b) the **cost of capital** (the return required for the risk of the business). Sum the discounted flows to get an enterprise value; adjusting for net debt gives equity value, divided by shares for a per-share estimate.
- **Trade-off**: the model forces you to state assumptions (growth, margins, reinvestment, discount rate) explicitly, which is its main benefit.
- **Does not hold when**: cash flows are too uncertain to forecast (early-stage firms), or the asset is better valued on liquidation value or on replacement cost.

## 5.2 The model is only as good as its inputs
- **Thesis**: the estimate is fragile because the discount rate and the terminal value dominate.
- **Mechanism**: small changes in the discount rate or the long-run growth rate change the value a lot, especially when most of the value lies in the terminal value (general knowledge). Rough forecasts rarely come true, so do not put undue weight on the output.
- **Mechanism, good use**: reverse the question. Use conservative assumptions: if the value is still above the price, confidence rises; or ask which growth and margins the current price already assumes. The DCF is a way to rationalise a price, not to predict it.
- **Does not hold when**: used to justify a conclusion already reached (confirmation bias, ch08).

## 5.3 Multiples
- **Thesis**: a multiple is a ratio of price to a fundamental, a quick relative gauge of how much you pay per unit of earnings, sales or book value.
- **Mechanism**: the price-to-earnings ratio divides price by earnings per share (a firm with 1 million shares at 30 and 2 million in net income has a P/E of 15). A **trailing** multiple uses past earnings and is backward-looking; a **forward** multiple uses expected earnings and relies on forecasts (expected earnings of 2.5 million give a forward P/E of 12). Others: EV/EBITDA, price-to-book, price-to-sales, and industry-specific ones. Like the price per pound at a butcher, a multiple lets you compare cuts of different size.
- **Mechanism, use**: a multiple means little alone; compare with peers, with the company's own history, and with its growth and risk. Moves of the multiple tell different stories: if earnings are expected to rise while the price falls the multiple has contracted (the market values the profit less); if earnings fall while the price rises it has expanded (people pay more for less).
- **Trade-off**: speed and comparability against hidden assumptions (accounting, leverage, cycle position).
- **Does not hold when**: earnings are negative, cyclical peaks or troughs, or accounting differs between firms.

## 5.4 Mean reversion of multiples (position)
- **Thesis (position)**: some investors buy when a multiple is below its long-run average and expect it to revert, earning a return even if earnings are flat.
- **Mechanism**: the assumption is that extremes are temporary overreactions to news.
- **Counterargument**: the average itself may shift (rates, growth, composition of the index, accounting); a low multiple can be a distressed company (a value trap), and a high one can be deserved. The relationship between valuation and subsequent return is noisy, "not gravity" (ch02, 2.8), and timing from it is unreliable.
- **Does not hold when**: structure has changed.

## 5.5 Valuation in bubbles (link)
- **Thesis**: bubbles arise when the justification for the price moves from cash flow to the *expected price of a future buyer* (ch09).
- **Mechanism**: during a boom, profitability and valuation are dismissed as "outdated metrics" while revenue, users or the share price itself become the metric; in the end, discounted cash flows reassert themselves.

## 5.6 Reading a company's numbers: margin, buybacks, dilution and asset lives
- **Thesis**: four small mechanisms explain many reported-profit surprises, and each can be checked with arithmetic.
- **Mechanism, margin sensitivity**: net margin is the share of revenue that becomes profit. With a 9 percent margin (revenue 100, profit 9, costs 91), cutting the selling price by 30 percent at constant costs gives revenue 70 and a loss of 21. Thin-margin firms are therefore fragile to price wars, tariffs and input costs; a firm whose margin fell from above 20 percent to about 10 percent has halved profit on the same sales.
- **Mechanism, buybacks**: when a firm repurchases and cancels shares, each remaining share owns a larger slice of the same profit, so earnings per share rise even if total profit is flat. It is a way of returning cash; (position) it can also signal that management sees few better uses for the money, which matters when flat revenue is hidden behind rising per-share figures.
- **Mechanism, dilution**: a new share issue that an investor does not subscribe to lowers their percentage. Example: a stake of 0.04 percent before several funding rounds is smaller after each one unless the investor puts in more money; the value can still rise if the price per share rises faster than the dilution. Check whether the amounts are in the same currency and at which valuation date.
- **Mechanism, useful life of assets**: the cost of long-lived equipment (chips, servers, machinery) is spread over its estimated life through depreciation. Lengthening the assumed life from, say, 4 to 6 years lowers the yearly charge and raises reported profit with no change in cash. The test is whether the assets are really still useful at that age (technology can become obsolete faster) and what cash is being spent to replace them: compare free cash flow with reported profit.
- **Mechanism, priced-in expectations**: a share price reflects expected future earnings; a firm can report flat results and fall, or report a drop and rise, depending on whether the result beat what was priced in. A fall of more than half from a peak, in a business whose sales are still growing, usually reflects a change in expected margins or growth, not in last year's sales.
- **Trade-off**: simple checks versus the risk of over-reading one metric.
- **Does not hold when**: the firm's accounting differs from peers or there is a one-off item; compare several years and cash flow, not one line.

## 5.7 Valuing a private stake with comparable multiples (worked example)
- **Thesis**: when a company is not listed, an estimate can come from the multiples of listed peers, with all the caveats of 5.3.
- **Mechanism**: take a set of comparable listed firms; compute market value divided by revenue and market value divided by EBITDA for each; drop the extreme values; use the average or median (illustration: revenue multiple about 2, EBITDA multiple about 6). Apply to the target: with revenue 60 and EBITDA 24 (in millions), the revenue method gives about 120 and the EBITDA method about 144 (24 times 6); an analyst might take a conservative multiple of 5 and get 120. The range, not a point, is the output. The value is a discounting of uncertain future flows, so any figure supplied by the owners (customers, leads, targets) is a claim to verify, not a datum.
- **Mechanism, return on a project**: a simple return measure is gross profit divided by invested capital; halving the capital spent doubles the measure, which is why capital-light claims deserve scrutiny of what was left out.
- **Trade-off**: fast and transparent versus sensitive to the peer set and to the owners' figures.
- **Does not hold when**: no listed peer has a similar business model or growth, or the private firm's figures are unaudited.
- **Caution**: an IPO price is an agreed price in a placement process; implied values of an early stake are only illustrative and depend on the stake size, the rounds in between and share classes.

## Common mistakes
- Treating a DCF output as a price target.
- Comparing multiples across industries with different growth and risk.
- Using a trailing multiple at a cyclical peak.
