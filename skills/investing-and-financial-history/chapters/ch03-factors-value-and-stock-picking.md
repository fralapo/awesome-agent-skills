# Chapter 03: Factors, Value and Stock Picking (Contested Ground)

> Educational concepts and history, not investment advice or a forecast. Everything here is a dated snapshot of an argument still open.

## Core Idea
In this model, asset pricing asks why some diversified portfolios earned more than others. **Factors** are characteristics (size, relative price, profitability, investment, momentum) that explain return differences. Whether those premiums are *risk compensation* or *persistent mispricing*, whether they will continue, and whether an ordinary investor can capture them are the contested questions.

## 3.1 From one factor to several
- **Thesis**: a single market-beta model explained roughly 60% to 80% of return differences between diversified portfolios; adding factors lifted this above 90%.
- **Mechanism**: the one-factor model says expected return depends only on sensitivity to the market. Three robust departures were recorded: small stocks earned more than beta predicted, cheap (high book-to-market, "value") stocks earned more than expensive ("growth") stocks, and low-beta stocks earned more than predicted. A three-factor model added a size factor (small minus big) and a value factor (high minus low), and in tests on 25 portfolios sorted by size and book-to-market (July 1963 to December 1991) explained about 83% to 97% of variation, with near-zero unexplained return ("alpha") on almost all. A later five-factor model added profitability (robust minus weak) and investment (conservative minus aggressive) and is said to explain about 90% to 95%.
- **Mechanism, tools**: time-series regression of a portfolio's returns on factor returns yields factor loadings (exposures), an alpha (what the factors do not explain) and an R-squared. Factor-adjusting an apparently skilled active fund often shows its outperformance came from tilting to small and value stocks, which can be copied at lower cost.
- **Trade-off**: a better model of normal returns weakens the case for manager skill and strengthens the case for cheap tilts, but adds complexity and tracking error.
- **Does not hold when**: factors are chosen after seeing the data (3.3).

## 3.2 Risk or mispricing (position)
- **Position, risk**: value stocks are often distressed, levered and uncertain; they fall more in bad times, so investors demand a premium. If this is risk compensation, there is theoretical reason it would persist after being published.
- **Position, behavioural**: investors over-extrapolate the glamour of growth stocks and neglect cheap ones, a systematic expectation error. Persistence then relies on limits to arbitrage, including the agency problem of a manager who cannot afford years of lagging the benchmark.
- **Honest status**: unresolved and hard to test; both explanations have empirical support. Momentum has weaker theory: no sensible risk story, and high turnover makes it expensive to capture.
- **Does not hold when**: you need one answer; the practical choice is whether you believe a premium exists *and* can bear its worst periods.

## 3.3 The factor zoo and the sniff test
- **Thesis**: published factors multiplied (over 300 had been counted by 2016), and many do not survive.
- **Mechanism**: academic incentives reward discovering factors; many are repackagings of older ones or the result of data mining. A screening checklist: **persistent** (across time), **pervasive** (across countries and sectors), **robust** to alternative definitions, **investable** after costs, and **sensible** (a reason it should exist). Pre-registration-style discipline: observation, hypothesis, then out-of-sample test.
- **Trade-off**: strict screening risks rejecting a real but fragile factor.
- **Does not hold when**: the test is run on the same sample that suggested the factor.

## 3.4 The value premium: the argument with the data
- **Thesis (position)**: value should have a positive expected premium.
- **Data as stated, dated (US)**: value beat the market by about 4.9% a year in 1963-1991 and about 2.4% in 1926-1963 (earlier period). After publication, 1991 to mid-2019, the premium fell to about 1.2% a year and was not statistically distinguishable from zero. Over 3 and 10 years ending 2019, value trailed the market by about 6.9% and 2.2% a year. A simulation reported about a 23% chance of a negative premium over 23 years and about 9% over 10 years: long droughts are consistent with a real premium, and the high volatility of the premium means that data cannot reject either "unchanged" or "declined".
- **Counterargument, the sceptic's**: published anomalies often shrink after publication; the US record is one market; a weak premium for decades would be hard to distinguish from a premium that is gone; mispriced-growth narratives (large technology firms) may simply have been justified; and tilts add fees, taxes and tracking error.
- **Counter-counterargument**: in other regions over the same post-1991 window value did better (e.g. about 6.1% a year in Japan, about 5.4% in Asia-Pacific, about 3.0% in emerging markets, about 0.3% in the UK and slightly negative in Canada, to mid-2019), and relative cheapness of value against growth hit extreme percentiles in 2019 by one measure; yet that measure used to argue for adding value is itself *timing*, which the same people usually advise against.
- **Mechanism, decisions**: the argument for a tilt is as a diversifier with an independent risk that can pay when the market's premium is negative; the argument against is that you must accept years of lagging. In this model the test is: would you still hold it after ten years of underperformance? (ch11).
- **Status**: contested; this chapter reports the debate, not a verdict.

## 3.5 Stock picking and concentration
- **Thesis**: individual stocks are riskier than the market and, on average, lose to it.
- **Mechanism**: stock returns are **positively skewed**: most stocks lag, a few mega-winners produce most of the market's gain. A small random basket is therefore more likely to miss the winners than to include them.
- **Data as stated, dated**: US stocks 1926-2016: only about 42.6% had lifetime returns above one-month bills, about 30.8% beat the market, more than half had negative lifetime returns, about 12% lost everything. US large-cap universe 1980-2020: 42% had negative absolute return, about 66% trailed the market, about 10% were "mega-winners"; about 44% suffered a catastrophic loss (-70% from peak, never recovered). Most catastrophic losers were consensus "buy" before the fall, and the losses hit profitable, moderately levered and fairly valued firms as well, caused by unhedgeable events (commodity prices, policy change, competition, fraud). The median 10-year single-stock return trailed the market by about 0.8% a year; stocks in the top 20% over five years trailed by about 1.9% a year over the next ten.
- **Counterargument**: concentration is the only way to beat the market; stock picking skill exists for informed or specialised investors; losses are limited to what you stake. Also: survivor and construction details matter, and a skill that is rare may still be real.
- **Mechanism, the investor's side**: idiosyncratic risk is uncompensated (ch01); the *only free lunch* is diversification (ch04); a concentrated portfolio is one where a single holding reaches about 10% (a threshold from a study of portfolio volatility, which rises little below it).
- **Why people do it anyway**: overconfidence, illusion of control, familiarity (employer stock), endowment effect, disposition effect, extrapolation: employees whose firms did worst over ten years put about 10% of contributions in company stock, those whose firms did best about 40% (a dated study). See ch08.
- **Does not hold when**: you hold a small "play" slice and can afford to lose it; or your concentration is the business you run (ch11 on skin in the game).

## 3.6 Reading a track record
- **Thesis**: any outcome can be found afterwards; selecting on it is not evidence.
- **Mechanism**: there will always be an active fund or stock that beat the index; its record is used to sell the strategy. Test instead whether results persist, whether they survive factor adjustment and costs, and whether the sample is chosen after the fact.
- **Does not hold when**: ex-ante, rule-based, audited and replicated over time.
