# Chapter 01: Risk and Return

> Educational concepts and history, not investment advice or a forecast.

## Core Idea
In this model, risk is the **chance of not getting what you need**, and return is the payment for bearing the kinds of risk the market pays for. Two questions keep apart what is often blurred: *what is risk?* (a definition problem) and *which risk is paid?* (a pricing problem).

## 1.1 The risk-return relationship
- **Thesis**: an asset that is more likely to hurt you must promise more on average, or nobody holds it.
- **Mechanism**: prices adjust until expected returns compensate for risk. A lender who sees a borrower as more likely to default demands a higher rate; the same logic ranks government debt, large-company debt and speculative-grade debt (see ch06). A higher expected return is a *consequence* of higher risk, never a free feature.
- **Trade-off**: expected is not realised. A premium is an average over long samples and many outcomes, so any single period can be negative.
- **Does not hold when**: the "return" is advertised rather than priced (a promised high, steady yield with no visible risk is a warning, see ch10).

## 1.2 Two definitions of risk (position)
- **Thesis (position)**: the industry measures risk as **volatility** (standard deviation of returns, or beta against the market); a rival view says risk is the **probability of permanent loss or of failing a goal**.
- **Mechanism, for volatility**: easy to compute from price history, comparable across assets, central to portfolio maths; in an efficient market a wobbling price reflects uncertainty.
- **Mechanism, against volatility**: (1) it counts upside moves as bad; a steadily falling asset can look "calm" while a rising one looks "risky"; downside deviation partly repairs this. (2) It looks backwards: a firm that sells its stable division becomes riskier today, yet its past volatility says nothing until the new regime shows up in prices. (3) Short-term price swings matter little to someone who does not have to sell, and a lot to someone who does. This is the case for defining risk by the goal and the horizon.
- **Rival**: the volatility camp answers that investors, and especially traders, are in fact hurt by swings (margin calls, forced sales, mandate limits), and that nothing better is as measurable. Neither camp claims the other measure is meaningless.
- **Does not hold when**: you must report risk for regulation, leverage limits or fund comparison; then volatility-type measures are the shared language even if you add a goal-based view.

## 1.3 Compensated versus uncompensated risk
- **Thesis**: only some risks earn a premium.
- **Mechanism**: **market (systematic) risk** cannot be diversified away and is rewarded on average. **Idiosyncratic (company-specific) risk** can be diversified away cheaply and therefore has no positive expected return attached to it (see ch04). Holding it is a bet without a premium.
- **Trade-off**: removing idiosyncratic risk also removes the chance of a spectacular outlier win.
- **Does not hold when**: you have genuine, costly-to-copy information (rare, see ch02).

## 1.4 The equity risk premium: long-run, uncertain
- **Thesis**: stocks have on average paid more than short-term safe assets, over long samples across many countries.
- **Mechanism**: a global long-run estimate of about 4.2% a year over safe assets (since 1900, 2020-era data, includes markets that went to zero) is a *sample average*. Over any 10-year window stocks trailed safe bills about 6% of the time in developed markets outside the US, about 12% in Canada and about 15% in the US (as stated, dated).
- **Trade-off**: a positive premium over decades coexists with lost decades. Two investors who made identical decisions and worked the same years ended retirement with materially different wealth purely because of the starting date (a dated illustration: retirement one year apart with overlapping periods gave about 36% less wealth to the later one).
- **Does not hold when**: the sample is short, the market is a single country that survived (see ch04 on survivorship), or valuations begin extreme.

## 1.5 Three dimensions of a personal risk profile
- **Thesis**: "how much risk" has three separate answers, and the allocation must satisfy the strictest.
- **Mechanism**: (1) **behavioural loss tolerance**: whether you hold through a fall instead of selling at the bottom; (2) **ability to take risk**: whether you can keep a risky asset without being forced to sell it to pay your living costs; (3) **need to take risk**: the return required to meet the goal. A person can have high tolerance but low ability, or the reverse.
- **Mechanism, tolerance parts**: willingness to engage with uncertain outcomes, general preference, financial knowledge, experience, subjective risk perception, and **risk composure**, which is only revealed by living through a decline. Psychometric questionnaires with demonstrated reliability and validity rank people against a reference population; they do not prescribe a portfolio.
- **Trade-off**: a portfolio that is "optimal" on paper but abandoned in a crash performs worse than a plainer one you keep.
- **Does not hold when**: need exceeds capacity; then the goal, savings rate or horizon must change, not the risk dial.

## 1.6 How much is "enough" (general knowledge, position)
- **Thesis (position)**: a target for how much capital is enough comes from the spending you need, the horizon and the sustainable withdrawal you can tolerate, not from a round number.
- **Mechanism**: the same logic as 1.5: need (the amount) meets ability (the horizon and the buffer). Any withdrawal rate is a rule of thumb that depends on the period examined, as 1.4 shows.
- **Does not hold when**: spending is uncertain (health, dependants) or a pension covers part of it.

## Common mistakes
- Treating a low-volatility history as low risk.
- Treating a high past return as evidence of a low-risk premium.
- Mixing up *tolerance* (feeling) with *ability* (arithmetic).
