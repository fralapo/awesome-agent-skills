# Chapter: Fears, Anxiety, Addictions and Psychological Disorders

## Core Idea

This lens, psychoanalytic in orientation, returns to a single explanatory thesis: symptoms that look unrelated (insomnia, smoking, social anxiety, panic, compulsive cleaning, hoarding, gambling, eating disorders, drug use, depression, narcissism, sadism and masochism) are read as defenses against painful childhood experiences that were *repressed* (rimossi), most often caused by the parents. The child erases what it cannot bear so it can survive, blames itself so the parent can stay "good", and the adult goes on living present situations through that old template (the parental *imprinting*). On this account the symptom is a "container" for anguish, not a vice or a weakness of will. Willpower, fear-based messaging, guilt and purely behavioral "reconditioning" treat effects and leave the cause in place. Lasting relief, in this model, comes from making the original experience conscious, mainly through dreams, and feeling the pain and anger tied to it. Some of this overlaps with mainstream psychology (repression, projection, effects of maltreatment, the cost of suppressing emotion). The single-cause, near-universal version of it, and the rejection of heritability and of brain chemistry as a cause, are contested or contradicted by current research (see Caveats).

## Frameworks Introduced

**Symptom as container, not vice (sintomo-contenitore).** In this model smoking, drugs, bulimia, gambling, rituals and nervous habits all "contain" an anguish the person often does not know about. There are no vices and virtues, only causes and effects. Blaming or frightening the person raises the anguish and so increases the need for the container. Applied to smoking, the claim is that what binds people is the ritual gesture, not nicotine (illustrated by smokers who keep the habit with nicotine-free cigarettes).
When to use: to reframe a behavior in a character, an audience or a campaign brief from "bad habit" to "what is this doing for them?"

**The fear gradient (paura → ansia → angoscia → attacco di panico → follia).** In this vocabulary *fear* has a known object. *Anxiety* is fear without an object that attaches to any event ("I'm afraid something will happen to me"). *Anguish* is paroxysmal anxiety with racing heart and sweaty hands. A *panic attack* is strong anguish with no known cause ("something terrible is happening to me"), a terror one cannot defend against. *Madness* sits at the far end.
When to use: as a descriptive vocabulary for intensity of fear in writing or reflection, never as a diagnostic scale.

**Repression → displacement → present-day trigger.** The mind erases the original event, then looks in the present for reasons to be afraid, even trivial ones (a badly filled form, a bill, an undeclared change). Example: a bank executive afraid to pay a bill at the counter. A *phobia* is a "false target" (falso bersaglio): fear moves onto something containable, like spiders, so the real cause can stay hidden. "Conversion" (conversione) of trauma, as applied to gambling, means the original pain is swapped for a less painful event (a match, wins and losses) whose fears are lived out instead.
When to use: to explain why a reaction is out of proportion to its trigger.

**Child-before-adult (sentirsi bambini davanti a un adulto).** Social anxiety, in this view, is the adult feeling like a child in front of an adult again. The parents' judgment is projected onto others, especially authority figures and famous people. Stage fright is located in an invisible "army of parents" (esercito di genitori) in the audience.
When to use: to understand anxiety in front of bosses, clients and audiences; the corrective stance proposed is "adult to adult".

**Split parent (genitore buono / genitore cattivo).** The child splits the parent into one who protects and one who strikes. It keeps the good image and projects the bad one outward, so nursery school, school and the outside world become places of punishment. This parallels Melanie Klein's good breast / bad breast.
When to use: to explain fear of the outside world alongside idealization of the family.

**Self-blame to preserve the parent.** The child would rather think "I am worthless" than "my parent is wrong", because that keeps the hope of improving and earning love. The model returns to this in guilt, hoarding (no identity), anorexia ("marked", guilty body), masochism and compulsive washing ("dirty conscience").

**Quantity, not quality (Freud).** Following Freud, disorders are treated as a matter of degree. A trait becomes a disorder when it is strong enough to damage relationships and the ability to work. Narcissism runs from "a small applause" to "the whole theatre"; diagnostic categories mostly help clinicians find their bearings.
When to use: to avoid binary "normal vs. sick" framing and treat traits as a continuum.

**Three planes of illness (psychosomatics).** The model separates the body's hereditary predisposition, the environment that wounds or welcomes, and the personal reaction ("if we stay attached to our tormentors we suffer more"). It claims all illness has a psychic component of varying weight. Psychosomatic illness comes mainly from not having been able to react, especially with anger, as a child. Autoimmune illness is kept apart: psyche weighs heavily there but is not the only cause.

**Anger owned vs. thirst for justice.** Anger comes from wrongs suffered and may be handled in many ways (cooling off, detaching, aggression, keeping it in), all respectable. What the unconscious needs is for anger to be *recognized*, not necessarily acted out. *Revenge* is personal and repairs helplessness. *Thirst for justice* wants guilty parties named and innocents proclaimed; this lens calls it an illusion and traces it to wanting justice from one's parents. The prescription is to say "I want this", taking responsibility, rather than "this is just".
When to use: in negotiation and conflict, to separate "I have a right" from "this is my need or preference".

**Understand, neither forgive nor condemn.** Understanding is not approval. Dutiful forgiveness is described as presumption (placing oneself on a higher plane) that blocks anger. The model also distinguishes two kinds of peace: with one's own feelings, or with the authority that orders forgiveness.

## Key Concepts

- **Rimozione (repression).** Freud's defense, treated here as universal. The child erases what it cannot bear, sometimes whole years, and the terror stays and resurfaces later (panic, depression, obsessions). "Nobody can say 'I remember everything'": whoever has a repression does not know it.
- **Imprinting / impronta.** The parents' mark on how we expect the world to treat us. Draws on Konrad Lorenz's critical windows (roughly the first three years in humans, though later years are held to count too) and Pavlov.
- **Mente condizionata (conditioned mind).** Built by family, school and society. It begins as a defense that wipes out painful memories, then stays switched on and keeps us on alert when there is no need. At night it "chatters" and scolds: the internalized parents, the "controllers" (controllori), who stop us letting go (insomnia: "a lion in the garden", but the danger is in the head).
- **Grillo parlante / giudice interno (talking cricket / inner judge).** The self-monitoring voice that tells us how to please. It removes spontaneity and empathy and, paradoxically, makes us less likeable.
- **Engrammi dinamici (dynamic engrams).** Emotional grooves from early experience that the mind applies to the whole outside world. If the parents devalued us, we expect devaluation from everyone.
- **Coazione a ripetere (repetition compulsion), this model's reading.** We choose partners like our parents because we are still waiting for the good that never came. Once the truth is seen, the waiting stops.
- **Bambino interiore (inner child).** The silenced child self. It may get angry when rediscovered; forgiving the parents too early "shuts it up" again.
- **Senso di colpa ≠ colpa (guilt is not fault).** A feeling of inadequacy passed on by parents, society and religion. It shows as fear that being looked at means being criticized, and as an immediate defensive justification when criticized. The child cannot be at fault. Step one: "guilt feelings are not faults".
- **Identità carente (deficient identity).** Our first identity comes from what our parents tell us about ourselves, and a negative identity amounts to none. Hoarders surround themselves with things to build an artificial boundary. Losing the "right to exist" (diritto di esistere) buries feelings and the capacity to choose.
- **Porta dei sentimenti (the single door of feelings).** There is only one door. Opening it releases joy and pain together, so people who fear pain also inhibit positive feelings and drown themselves in work instead.
- **Timidezza vs. carattere.** Introversion and extroversion are innate temperament without fear. Shyness, in this model, is fear passed on by the parents.
- **Narcisismo (narcissism).** A defensive strategy of the unloved child, who trades love for admiration. The narcissist reads others well in order to manipulate them (low voice, pauses, a spellbinding tone) without identifying with them. Never feeling loved enough, he devalues those who love him and loves the idea of love rather than the person. He performs "in hysterical form": present, but without putting the soul into it. People who were themselves unloved fall for it. The model calls it an illness, not wickedness, and holds that narcissists can be good or bad.
- **Sadismo / masochismo.** In this reading the sadist identifies with the tormenting parent so as not to recognize himself as a victim. The masochist's formula is "do what you want with me, but save me". The model claims an exclusive origin in repressed abuse (flagged contested).
- **Nervosismo (nervousness).** Repetitive, compulsive discharge of tension: lip and cheek biting, hair twisting, tics, rocking, the bouncing leg, bruxism, nail biting, counting rituals, cleaning what is already clean, compulsive buying. Nails and teeth are read as "weapons of offense" held back. Normal movement accompanies expression; never moving at all is anesthesia, not calm (the motionless gurus). Artistic inspiration nourishes rather than drains.
- **Logorrea / mutismo (compulsive talking / near-silence).** The compulsive talker addresses an imaginary listener who finally listens, using words as a shield against anxiety. The near-silent person believes they must be brilliant to be accepted. Halting speech may be holding back aggression. Quietness in itself is not treated as pathology.
- **Euforia compensatoria (compensatory euphoria).** Always "up" to avoid depression, then collapsing under a serious event. The image for bipolarity is living "above or below the line" of reality.
- **Male di vivere (the ache of living).** A near-universal background pain, compensated with love, sex, shopping, gambling, bulimia, extreme sports and drugs.
- **"La pigrizia non esiste" (laziness does not exist).** Persistent apathy or boredom is a sign of low energy or suffering, a separation from feelings.
- **Vivere per interposti avvenimenti (living through proxy events).** Matches, films, stars: living through someone else's events without asking "how does this concern me?" (gambling and extreme fandom).
- **Corpo innocente (innocent body).** This model's reading of anorexia: extreme thinness gives a child's "innocent" body that can be shown without fear, because fat is tied to guilt. Bulimia "puts a cork" on an anguishing emptiness.
- **Paura della morte (fear of death).** In this lens (after Freud) the deepest fear, hidden by modern society, carrying all the childhood fears. People "work as if immortal". Defying death with extreme sports or heroics does not dissolve it.

## Mental Models

1. **Disproportion signals history.** If a reaction is far larger than its trigger (panic over a bill, collapse after mild criticism), the model reads the present event as a hook for an older, unremembered one.
2. **The other is not your parent.** Much hurt from criticism comes from its resonance with parental judgment. Seeing the critic as who they are, someone attacking for their own reasons, reduces both fear and anger.
3. **People respond to what you emanate.** Others react more to inner state (fear, serenity, determination) than to words or gestures. Typical examples: someone halting a violent aggressor through determination rather than strength; a speaker who improvises in front of thousands once the "parents behind the shoulders" are gone.
4. **Removing the symptom moves the problem.** Swapping one mania for another, forcing someone to eat, or quitting by sheer willpower, in this model, only relocates the anguish.
5. **Fear and guilt messaging backfire.** Threatening warnings ("live for your loved ones") raise anguish and so, the model argues, reinforce the behavior. A question ("why do I do this?") opens more than a judgment.
6. **Not knowing you are unwell.** Suffering looks normal: filling every hour, fearing others, doing things "absolutely". A difficult childhood gets retold as "it went fine". Image: the man who wore short sleeves in winter and began to feel cold as awareness grew.
7. **Proximity to authority amplifies anxiety.** The more a person embodies a parental role (rank, fame, judge), the more the child-adult script activates.
8. **Victim selection.** The model claims bullies pick the psychologically, not physically, fragile, projecting onto them the weak part they hate in themselves, and that a person who does not accept the victim role is chosen less often. This is a claim of the model and risks victim-blaming if applied to real cases.
9. **Comparison is a trap.** Being good at something is a quality, not the worth of the person. Ranking people by social role puts the other back in the parent's seat.

## Applications

- **Understanding motivations.** When a person, user segment or character acts against their own interest (relapse, compulsive buying, staying with a harmful partner), the "what is this containing?" question gives a richer hypothesis than "lack of willpower". Keep it a hypothesis, never an attribution about a real individual.
- **Writing characters.** This material gives a coherent psychoanalytic toolkit for fiction:
  - the narcissist who seduces with pauses and a low voice but never feels loved enough;
  - the compulsive talker telling a story that "starts from my aunt, my uncle…";
  - the hoarder with thirty pairs of unworn shoes;
  - the sadist re-enacting what he suffered, and the masochist pleading "save me";
  - the euphoric who collapses;
  - the character who insists their childhood was perfect;
  - phobias as false targets;
  - insomnia as an inner lion in the garden.
  
  Use these as dramatic logic, not clinical truth.
- **Communication and campaigns.**
  - Avoid guilt and fear as the main lever on health and social topics; test threatening tones with data. The claim that they backfire is plausible but unproven.
  - Do not sell identity through objects, and do not lean on low self-esteem.
  - In gambling-adjacent communication, avoid "lucky day" and "win it back" framing.
  - Do not present thin beauty ideals as the single cause of eating disorders.
  - Never promise healing; point to professionals.
  - Fan communities react emotionally because the team or brand works as an extension of the self.
- **Meetings, feedback and teams.**
  - Evaluate the work, not the person, so the "inner judge" is not triggered.
  - Treat a defensive, instant justification as insecurity and answer calmly.
  - Give a person who is being corrected room to object without reading it as rebellion.
  - Admit your own mistakes and tiredness as a leader.
  - Read persistent low initiative as a possible sign of distress rather than laziness; ask before judging.
  - Interrupt a compulsive talker kindly and briefly; the feared offense usually does not come.
  - Reduce uncertainty (clear steps, timelines, owners) instead of offering reassuring slogans.
  - Protect pauses and sustainable workloads.
- **Public speaking coaching.**
  - Spontaneity beats over-rehearsed performance.
  - Focusing on the content reduces stage fright more than fighting it (the actor who loses his fear once acting).
  - Treat powerful clients adult to adult.
  - A monotone delivery, detached from what one feels, comes across as boring.
- **Coaching-style reflection (non-clinical).** Some questions an agent can offer: "What does this criticism touch in me?" "Is this my need, or am I appealing to what is 'right'?" "Who does this person remind me of?" Do not steer users toward recovering memories or confronting family. That belongs in therapy.
- **Audience insight.** Hostile comments without arguments can come from beliefs being challenged, so moderate without taking it personally. Audiences carry fears of being wrong and being judged; design messages with that in mind.

## Caveats

- **Status of the model.** These are theses of a popular psychoanalytic lens, not scientific evidence. Present them as the model's positions ("in this model…"), not as established psychology.
- **Single-cause claim.** The recurrent claim that nearly every disorder (panic, OCD, depression, bipolarity, psychosis, eating disorders, addictions, gambling, sadomasochism, stuttering, tics) comes from repressed childhood trauma, often sexual abuse, is a generalization. Clinical research points to multifactorial causes: biological, genetic, environmental, learning and social.
- **Heritability.** The model's statement that mental illnesses are not inherited at all contradicts current genetic research, which finds inherited vulnerability interacting with environment. Treat it as a contested claim.
- **Brain chemistry.** "Chemistry is only a consequence of emotion" is not supported. The model does value psychiatric medication as a "container" that stabilizes the person enough for psychotherapy, and for psychosis it recommends medication plus psychotherapy together.
- **Psychosomatics.** Chronic stress and emotional state do affect immunity and health. Claims that all illness has a psychic cause, that cancer does not arise without depression, or that a blood value normalized after recognizing anger are anecdotes. Read as medical guidance, they are dangerous.
- **Abuse statistics.** "95% in the family", "one child in three" and "there is always abuse behind certain fantasies" are unsourced figures and generalizations. Reading symptoms, fantasies or dreams as proof of abuse is not clinically valid and must never be used to judge or accuse anyone. Real cases need qualified services.
- **Other therapies.** The model dismisses CBT, EMDR, breathing techniques and behavioral treatments as surface-level. CBT in particular has strong evidence for anxiety, social anxiety and OCD.
- **Self-help tips not to relay.** The model's folk remedies for acute panic (stamping feet, clenching fists, food, a sip of liquor or coffee, Bach flowers, checking iron, magnesium and potassium) are non-medical opinions. An agent should not pass them on as advice. Ruling out physical causes is the one point consistent with standard practice.
- **Suicide.** The model's view that suicide is an understandable escape from unbearable pain, deserving respect rather than judgment, is an opinion. It must never be presented as guidance. If anyone may be at risk, direct them immediately to emergency services or a crisis line.
- **Recovered memories.** Retrieving repressed memories through dreams is psychoanalytic theory and scientifically controversial: memories can be constructed or suggested.
- **Ideology mixed in.** Opinions on patriarchy and matriarchy, religion and the Church, vegetarianism, free will, drug legalization, politics and gender differences ("men rationalize, women are open to interiority") are interpretations and should not be presented as psychology.
- **Esoteric content (excluded).** Esoteric beliefs associated with this tradition (the "black/negative astral", entities, reincarnation, chakras, "evolutionary magic", real possession) are excluded here as non-psychological.
- **No clinical use.** This chapter is not clinical advice and supports no diagnosis or treatment. Anyone with real symptoms (persistent insomnia, panic, compulsions, disordered eating, substance problems, depression) should see a qualified professional: GP, psychiatrist or licensed psychotherapist.
