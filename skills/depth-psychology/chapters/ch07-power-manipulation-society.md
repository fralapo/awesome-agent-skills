# Chapter: Power, Manipulation, Violence and Society

## Core Idea
This lens gives one psychoanalytically oriented reading of power, conformity, cruelty and collective hostility: they come from childhood experience with the parents. What the model calls the "conditioned mind" (*mente condizionata*), built by parents, school and society, carries the internalised parents. Adults project those parents onto leaders, parties, majorities, gods and enemies. They idealise or demonise. They obey. Or they move from victim to perpetrator and take out repressed pain on someone weaker. The proposed way out is awareness rather than goodness or willpower: see your real parents clearly, feel the old anger and grief, and get back critical thinking and empathy. The mechanisms it leans on (projection, repression, transference, identification with the aggressor) come from psychoanalysis. The claim that they are the *single* cause of war, rape, femicide or bullying is this model's interpretation. A practical layer on workplace respect and assertiveness is added separately as a "Workplace respect ladder"; it is experience-based advice, not research.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The Conditioned Mind vs. the Reasoning Mind:** The *reasoning mind* solves problems (maths, organisation, research). The *conditioned mind* is installed by parents, teachers, surroundings and media. It acts as an inner judge, needs fixed categories and certainty, and in this model is "where external power lives inside us". It is set against the *soul* (*anima*), the capacity to feel your own and others' states.
When to use: to separate a belief someone has checked from one they inherited, in themselves or in an audience.

**Parent Projection onto Authority:** In this view people who have never really looked at their parents transfer the same dependent, idealising stance onto God, politicians, bosses, parties and "the majority". They stop judging what those figures do and vote for them again despite disappointments, as a child forgives a parent.
When to use: to explain blind loyalty (to a party, brand, leader or consultant) that survives contrary evidence.

**Idealisation / Demonisation and "Staying on the Line":** *Idealising* means seeing only the good. In this model it is a search for the good parent you never had. *Demonising* means seeing only the bad: you vent rage at a failing parent you cannot accuse. The healthy position is "staying on the line": credit merits and faults, and separate what you know from what you assume. Accusations of idealisation or demonisation should be backed with data.
When to use: when evaluating a controversial figure, competitor or testimonial, or when an audience polarises around a person or brand.

**Black-and-White Thinking as a "Chest of Drawers":** The mind files people and facts into fixed drawers ("good" or "bad") and reacts with irritation when a "bad" person does good. The model separates healthy categories (recognising a cat, citing Kant) from moral pre-judgement. It also separates *evaluating* ("this person is dangerous") from *judging* ("he is evil, I hate him"). The antidote is to say "it seems to me" instead of "it is so". Mainstream clinical psychology also recognises dichotomous thinking as a cognitive distortion.
When to use: when reading polarised social reactions, or when phrasing feedback so it doesn't trigger defences.

**Victim-to-Perpetrator Switch (Identification with the Aggressor):** A mistreated child represses the memory but keeps the suffering. To avoid feeling like a victim, the child sides with the violent parent. As an adult, power over someone weaker numbs the pain and seems to wipe out the hated weak inner child. In this model the "line between the two roles is very thin". It is used to explain cruelty to animals, bullying, rape, femicide, wartime atrocities and plain "real" malice, defined as pleasure in making others suffer. Caveat built into the model: "whoever tortures was tortured" does not run backwards, since not every victim becomes a perpetrator.
When to use: to frame the psychological hypothesis behind cycles of abuse, and to avoid explaining violence only by "lack of values" or "bad manners".

**Two Kinds of Power (after Adler):** (1) *Fear-driven power*: "I become important so nobody attacks me". It is defensive and can be defused by reassurance. (2) *Power as pleasure in dominating*, which the model calls cruelty. Behind ambition it sees fear of death ("if I'm special, maybe I won't die"). Power is treated as one useful reading frame among several (power, repression, projection), none complete on its own.
When to use: to decide whether to reassure or to set firm limits with a controlling person.

**Neurotic vs. Rational Sacrifice ("Sacrificial Mania"):** *Neurotic sacrifice* has no purpose. It is a barter with an authority ("I suffer, so God or the parent will spare and love me"), which the model traces to monotheistic, mainly Catholic, upbringing. Women sacrificing for husband and children, or men for bosses, follow the same template. *Rational sacrifice* serves a real goal (little money, so skip the holiday). When the barter is "betrayed", for example by a death, people get angry.
When to use: to spot over-availability, unpaid overtime or self-denial that is really a bid for approval.

**Conditioning vs. Plagiarism (Coercive Control):** The *conditioned* person still has a mental sense of right and wrong. The *plagiarised* person (Italian *plagio*, roughly coercive persuasion) has lost it, believes their own feelings are wrong, and depends on someone else to tell them. Typical sources: sects, dictatorships, parents, spiritual masters. A real path makes you *more* sensitive to others, animals and nature. It does not turn anyone into a guru or detach them from emotion.
When to use: as a checklist for manipulation in groups, coaching, leadership or communication (see Applications).

**Mental Contagion (after Le Bon):** In groups and households, attitudes pass from the *stronger* to the *weaker*, not from the better to the worse. Contagion grows with time spent together and with the number of people who share a view. Examples are Le Bon's crowd and Hitler's rallies, and between spouses the more dominant partner. People who think they are unconditioned are, in this model, the most conditioned.
When to use: to understand how a leader's style spreads through a team, and how imitation and social proof work.

**The Majority Myth:** Politically, a majority passes laws but does not guarantee justice, which is why minorities have protections. Psychologically, "the majority" stands in for the parents, so what is common feels right and natural. "Natural" is used for convenience: everyone reads the "laws of nature" in their own favour. Plurality of information and cultures (ancient Rome as example) makes a people smarter. A single culture makes people swallow everything. Conformity and social proof are well documented in mainstream psychology.
When to use: against "everybody does it" arguments, and when designing a decision group.

**Proportionate Aggression and Stacked Anger:** Humans have both too much aggression and too little *healthy* expression of it, because children learn to swallow it. Repressed anger from childhood stacks on top of present emotion. That produces outsized reactions, or a withdrawal for fear of exploding. Aggression is legitimate when it answers real harm *and is proportionate* to it; ignoring the provoker is the alternative. The model also separates *hatred and rancour* (wanting the other to suffer) from *anger* (a child's helpless pain). Forgiving out of duty covers the anger without dissolving it.
When to use: when facing a disproportionate reaction. Ask what is fuelling it before answering on the merits.

**War as Collective Projection (from the Einstein–Freud letters "Why War"):** Fears and grudges repressed in childhood attach to an external enemy (rival party, team, neighbour, boss, people) whenever events seem to confirm them. When the enemy is truly external and visible (air-raid shelters as example), solidarity rises and neuroses drop. The ethical test is identification with civilian victims. The model concludes no war is just, not even a defensive one. That is a value position, not political analysis.
When to use: to see how personal history amplifies conflicts, and why putting faces on victims changes how a conflict is perceived.

**Workplace Respect Ladder:** The goal is to make disrespect *stop*, not to win the argument. People who disrespect take the path of least resistance, so make that path costly: friction, not walls. Golden rule: "what you let pass becomes the new normal." Three mistakes make disrespect free: (1) hyper-performance so as "not to give them a hook", (2) unlimited availability, (3) venting to others with no consequence for the offender. The ladder:
  - *Level 1 (interrupted in a meeting):* stop and look at the person. Hold 2 full seconds of silence without filling it. Resume from the exact word where you were cut off, at the same pace. No "sorry", "never mind", "I just wanted to say" or "maybe I'm wrong".
  - *Level 2 (it repeats):* in private and calmly, ask one short question instead of making a speech: "When you interrupt me in front of others, is it because you want me to change how I work, or does it just happen?" Then stay silent until they answer.
  - *Level 3:* remove the extras, not the work. No overtime, no off-hours availability, no favours. Do what the contract requires.
  - *Final move:* keep an alternative open (updated CV, interviews, other income, a savings cushion) so this job doesn't feel like the only one. Bosses come and go, and each time you don't let something pass you collect evidence of your worth.
When to use: practical assertiveness when someone at work interrupts you, belittles you or takes you for granted.

## Key Concepts
- **Conditioned mind (*mente condizionata*):** the internalised voice of parents and society. It needs certainty, judges, and is described as "a house of cards" afraid that removing one card brings it all down.
- **Internal parents / "parent passed to the other side":** part of the child "sides with the parents" and becomes the inner critic ("you're not capable"). It is a way to stay loved, because a child cannot admit to being unloved.
- **Repression and projection** (from psychoanalysis): the two basic defences in this model. Pain is forgotten but its emotion stays, then gets attached to present figures who resemble the parent (authority, economic or emotional dependence).
- **Transference:** real in therapy, but the therapist should not encourage it: no self-disclosure to patients, and a check on whether an idealised figure "does something" to be idealised.
- **Identification with the aggressor / victim–perpetrator switch:** see Frameworks.
- **Supremacy desire:** most people want to be superior in something (beauty, intelligence, fame, career), starting with wanting to be the parents' favourite. Recognising your own qualities is healthy. Needing nobody to be better than you is competition. Constant comparison is "an illness", and dropping it frees your self-image. The way out is to feel "as important as a plant in the woods". The Parsifal image: he loses the Grail because he does not ask questions.
- **Exploitation as "being special":** exploiting others feeds the illusion of not being "one of many", and so of not dying. Example: the line attributed to Caesar, "better first in a tribe than second in Rome". It harms the exploiter too.
- **"Good" as flattening:** conventional goodness is often obedience. "Good" people are inhibited yes-men. The real question is not "am I good or bad?" but "am I aware?". Judge what people *do*, not what they *are*.
- **Aggression as fear:** much hostile behaviour is a defence against fear and can change quickly when the person is reassured. Separate aggressing in self-defence (reassure) from aggressing for pleasure (oppose, and if needed report).
- **Others respond to your inner state:** people react to the mood under the words. Formally polite but inwardly hostile behaviour leaks through.
- **Cruelty as transgenerational chain:** parents who did their duty (school, care, money) without loving pass on what they received. To love a child means understanding and asking how they *feel*, not only how school is going.
- **Femicide hypothesis:** in this model the cause is severe childhood trauma relived as abandonment when the partner leaves, not a "loss of moral values". It claims men are more emotionally dependent on women because the first female figure is the mother. It separates *love* (freedom, a demand for reciprocity) from *dependency* (repeating a childhood trauma), and doubts that affectivity education in schools works ("affection isn't taught, it's received").
- **Narcissist (clinical sense):** needs to be loved but cannot love, because they were not loved. Tends to attract dependent partners.
- **Rape hypothesis:** the model rejects the idea of a perpetrator "badly brought up" in the etiquette sense. Example: courteous Nazis at Nuremberg: sensitivity matters, not manners. In this reading the perpetrator is an "ungrown child" turning unprocessed parental violence onto someone perceived as weaker.
- **Bully hypothesis:** the bully was raised with *too much* harshness and contempt, not too little. He hates in timid or disabled classmates the fragility his parents despised in him. He often stops when he meets someone stronger. Bullying also exists among adults (teachers, workplace harassment backed by threats of dismissal).
- **Screens as symptom, not cause:** video games and smartphones are a defence against unhappiness at home or school, not its origin. Constant connectivity is a search for confirmation that you are loved. Socially rewarded escapes (compulsive work, wealth, travel) are the same mechanism as frowned-upon ones.
- **"Void to fill":** repressed pain is compensated with food, gambling, sport, news, compulsive sex or consumption. The discomfort is always blamed on something missing now, never on the past.
- **Power as temptation (Tolkien reading):** Gandalf refusing the Ring ("I would use it for good and become its slave") illustrates that wanting power "for good" is still the conditioned mind. Orcs are tortured elves, an image of the abused turned abuser. Fathers who "bargain" affection (Denethor with Faramir) teach that love must be earned.
- **Leader as servant:** in this model's critique of initiatory schools, groups fail through power and sex. Insecure leaders pick the most devoted helpers rather than the best.
- **"Whoever insists on converting you isn't convinced":** certainty doesn't need others' agreement. Over-insistence signals insecurity.
- **Forgiveness:** a gift to the other, not "for yourself". It needs the other to ask for it, repent and make amends, or you to realise you over-judged. Turning the other cheek "teaches nothing". Self-respect includes distancing yourself from people who hurt you.
- **What you let pass becomes the new normal** (respect ladder): tolerated disrespect sets the baseline.
- **Friction, not walls** (respect ladder): raise the cost of disrespect instead of escalating.
- **Make your extras visible by removing them** (respect ladder): withdraw discretionary effort, not contractual work.
- **Career-mindset notes** (opinion, not research): don't decide too early who you are (after Oscar Wilde). Treat a "no" as a nail to pull out, not something to brood on ("be the boat, not the rock"). You are the average of the five people you spend time with: distance yourself from those who say it can't be done. "Fail fast, fail forward". "I can't" often means "I don't want it enough". Ask people further ahead directly. When people act, they talk about themselves, not you ("good man, not nice guy", after Matthew McConaughey). Others don't get a vote on your life. Don't carry everything like Atlas behind a "fine": asking for help, including from a psychologist, is taking your life back.

## Mental Models
- **Ask "what old pain is feeding this?" before answering the content.** A reaction out of proportion to its trigger is, in this model, old anger added to present anger.
- **Fear vs. pleasure test.** Is this person aggressive because they feel threatened (reassure them) or because they enjoy it (set limits and protect others)?
- **Evaluate acts, not essences.** "This piece of work has these problems" rather than "you are careless".
- **"It seems to me" over "it is so".** Hedged phrasing lowers defences and leaves room to disagree.
- **Strength, not goodness, spreads.** In any group, look at who has the most power to predict where attitudes will spread (mental contagion).
- **Loyalty that survives evidence is affective.** When a party, brand or leader keeps support despite repeated disappointment, suspect a parent-like bond, not a rational assessment.
- **Majority ≠ truth; "natural" is negotiable.**
- **Power changes people, often for the worse.** People who gain a role may change their attitude. Examples: Aragorn sighing under the crown versus politicians who seek power for themselves, and Roman emperors "gentle before the throne, beasts after".
- **Behaviour-only fixes miss causes.** Punishing the bully or regulating the game treats the symptom. Look at the system (family, school, team culture) that humiliates.
- **Over-performance makes disrespect free.** Working twice as hard "to give no hooks" signals that disrespect costs nothing. (respect ladder)
- **Silence is a move.** A 2-second pause, or one question followed by silence, shifts the burden back onto the other person. (respect ladder)
- **An alternative creates freedom.** Feeling you have options (savings, CV, other offers) changes how you hold boundaries. (respect ladder)

## Applications
**Understanding group behaviour**
- Polarised social reactions: content that shows nuance can be rejected by people who need clean drawers. Expect pushback on "both sides have a point" messages.
- Conformity and social proof are strong, documented levers. This lens's caution: use them honestly, and don't confuse "everyone does it" with what the client needs.
- Teams: a team of only "yes" people hides problems. Plurality of viewpoints and sources improves reviews and brainstorming. The model meeting is a family dinner where even the youngest child is heard.
- A leader's mood spreads downwards (mental contagion). Fear of the boss silences feedback, so create conditions where people can disagree and ask questions.
- Over-availability and unpaid overtime are often a bid for approval rather than efficiency. Tell useful sacrifice (budget priorities) apart from neurotic sacrifice.
- Overbearing behaviour in a team or among suppliers may be a reaction to a humiliating system. Address the context, not only the person.

**Manipulation red flags**
- Telling people *what to think* or feel, for example "there's no reason to be afraid", without exploring where the fear comes from.
- Blaming failure on the follower ("you didn't let go enough").
- Building dependence on one figure. Leaders who present themselves as a "beacon of light" or guru.
- Detaching followers from their emotions and calling it wisdom. Pronouncing verdicts. Imposing one path.
- Surrounding oneself with devotees instead of competent people. Promising "the secret" in exchange for devotion.
- Guilt- or renunciation-based appeals: they work, but this lens warns against them.
- Over-insistence to convert or convince, which in this model signals the persuader's own uncertainty.

**Persuasion ethics**
- Invite people to check things (trials, data, experience) rather than ask for belief. It builds more trust with sceptical audiences.
- Status and "feeling special" are common brand levers. This lens suggests weighing whether to use them or to avoid feeding comparison.
- Avoid content that has fun frightening or humiliating weaker beings (pets, children, vulnerable people). Audiences perceive it as cruel.
- On violence topics (gender violence, bullying, school), don't flatten complex phenomena into moral slogans, and avoid the cartoon "evil bully". Talk about causes and prevention, and use specialist sources.
- Put faces and stories to victims to encourage identification in conflict-related communication.
- Messages that attack a beloved party or brand trigger identity defences, not reasoning.

**Audience insight**
- Before condemning an audience habit (screen time, social media), ask what need it meets. For young audiences, interactivity and creativity count for more than information alone.
- The "void to fill" reading: consumption and entertainment can be compensation. It is useful as a hypothesis about motivation, not a diagnosis.
- Clients who seek visibility or prestige may be driven by perceived status (for example, an entrepreneur who wants to be seen as the local leader).
- An aggressive client or user may be afraid. Listening and reassurance often work better than confrontation.
- Audiences sense the attitude behind the words. Stereotyped or "prefabricated" replies, including AI-generated ones, get devalued.

**Storytelling**
- The improbable hero (Frodo, who cannot fight and does not know the road) is a reusable pattern: change comes from someone who isn't the strongest.
- Power as a corrupting ring, and the abused who becomes an abuser (orcs as tortured elves), are ready-made narrative frames for antagonists with depth.

**Workplace assertiveness (respect ladder)**
- Use the Level 1–3 ladder for interruptions and repeated disrespect. Drop apologetic fillers. Pull back discretionary extras instead of escalating. Keep options open.

## Caveats
- **Single-cause explanations are this model's interpretation.** It traces war, rape, femicide, bullying, animal cruelty, exploitation and political loyalty mostly to childhood mistreatment by the parents. Mainstream research points to multiple causes: biological, social, cultural, economic, group-level and temperamental. "Whoever tortures was tortured" is a conviction of the model, not a scientific rule. Bullying research names group, school and temperament factors. Wars have economic, political and historical causes.
- **What has mainstream support:** projection, transference and the weight of unprocessed childhood trauma (psychoanalytic and clinical concepts, though psychoanalysis remains a debated framework). Dichotomous thinking as a cognitive distortion. Conformity and social proof. Group and parental influence. The insecurity–control–power link. The link between harsh upbringing, guilt and self-sacrifice. Social comparison as a source of distress. The idea that status and power don't guarantee happiness.
- **Strong generalisations** that are opinion: that most parents do not love their children; that few people had a truly good childhood and those who think so have repressed it; that men are more emotionally dependent than women; the male-mind/female-soul polarity; that schools reward obedience; that affectivity education is useless.
- **Politically loaded claims** (national politics and media, democracy, Russia–Ukraine, the absolute rejection of any war including defensive war, economics of money printing and working hours) are value positions outside psychology. Present them neutrally and never as analysis.
- **Religiously loaded claims**, for example that sacrificial mania comes from Catholic and monotheistic upbringing, that Christian virtue reduces to obedience, and readings of Church history, are polemical views, not theology or historiography.
- **Clinical simplifications:** depression as "lack of energy"; addictions, anorexia and OCD as simple defences against childhood trauma; the claim that "video games harm nobody" (research finds effects that vary with time, content and individual vulnerability). The model's acute-panic tip (stamping feet, clenching fists) is a folk suggestion, not a treatment, and should not be relayed as advice.
- **Uncited data:** the electric-shock experiment, abuse statistics and other figures are quoted from memory without sources.
- **The respect ladder** is experience-based opinion, not psychological research. It fits a context where the worker has options. Situations of harassment or legal risk need specialist or legal support.
- **Esoteric content left out:** beliefs associated with this tradition about magic, a "negative astral" or entities feeding on suffering, alchemical stages (opera al nero and al bianco), reincarnation and a "magical chain" against the coronavirus have no scientific basis and have been omitted.
