# Chapter: Awareness, Consciousness and the Mind/Soul/Instinct Split

## Core Idea
This model of the person rests on three parts. The **mind** is split in two: a **reasoning mind** that deduces and is a useful tool, and a **conditioned mind** made of beliefs received from parents, school, religion, media and the era. The **soul** (anima) is simply the felt life of emotions, feelings and inner movement; the model explicitly rejects an invisible theological entity. **Instinct** is an innate "wisdom of the species" that includes reading other people and danger directly. Childhood pain is **repressed** (Freud's rimozione): the memory is erased but the feeling stays active. The feeling is **projected** onto the world, ties up psychic energy (anxiety, poor memory, low mood) and gets covered by **escapes**: screens, work, career, shopping, abstraction, even positive thinking. **Awareness** (consapevolezza) is therefore not knowledge or culture. It means *knowing what one actually feels*: accepting only what one has experienced inside, doubting the received dogmas (above all "the parents" and "God"), looking at what really happened in childhood, and finally accepting death. The end point is the **fall of the conditioned mind**, after which the reasoning mind serves the I and instinct returns. Some of this matches recognised psychology (stress harms memory, avoidance, projection, cultural conditioning). The strong generalisations, and the dream-only route to trauma, are this model's own positions.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Knowing vs. awareness ("sapere saputo" vs. "sapere sapiente")**: An old distinction. Erudition sits in the mind; awareness sits in "feeling" and is certain the way one knows whether it is day or night. A farmer can be highly aware and a scholar not at all. Culture can even become a **bulwark** for feeling superior. The image is the jug that pours and the glass that drinks: a passive audience absorbing notions. Parsifal fails at the Grail the first time because he does not ask the question.
When to use: when designing content or teaching that should make people ask questions instead of receiving packaged answers, and when spotting jargon used as armour.

**The three-part person: reasoning mind / conditioned mind / soul (+ instinct)**: *Reasoning mind*: a filter between inner feeling and the outside that suggests fitting behaviour, as in maths. *Conditioned ("artificial", "double") mind*: a "description of the world" plus an inner voice like Freud's superego but wider, because it includes society. The image is a castle that blocks the view. *Soul*: emotions, feelings, instinct and also an "ancient, uncontaminated" core. *Instinct*: the soul at birth, before conditioning. Mind and soul often pull in opposite directions, which is why we go silent before a boss or someone we like.
When to use: as a lens for internal conflict ("I know X but feel Y") in reflection or in character writing.

**Mind Matrix (collective conditioned mind)**: A moral (not physical) use of *The Matrix*. A collective conditioned mind makes us see the world through others' ideas and live "in the head". The problem is not what we see but how we interpret it. Leaning on Buddha, Jesus or one's parents is still leaning on others. The stance: "I ask to be listened to, not believed." Induced needs (car, holidays, designer clothes, career, the "right" family) rest on the belief that otherwise "we are nobody". Thrill-seeking (racing, compulsive sex, dangerous sports, constant travel) signals a "blocked" soul: "if you feel free only when travelling, there is a prison inside."
When to use: when auditing which audience needs are induced by status and comparison, and when adopting a transparent "check whether this resonates with you" communication stance.

**Schemas and anthropocentrism ("the human animal")**: Mental schemas change with era and place. Example: medieval belief in witches, with areas of anaesthesia taken as signs of possession, when they could come from severe suffering or psychosis. The deepest schema, in this view, is anthropocentrism, a form of egocentrism ("we're special, so God gives us eternity and denies it to animals"). All hierarchies descend from it: man over woman, adult over child, race over race, career over non-career. The need to feel superior comes from fear of feeling like nothing.
When to use: when checking one's own cultural and period assumptions before communicating.

**No free will, only a wider cell**: Here humans have no real free will. Body needs (hunger, thirst, pain, fatigue) limit it, and above all learned conditioning does. One can only "widen the walls of the prison" through a long inner path, and step one is admitting one is not free. The checks: separate the verifiable (the Earth is round) from personal, unverifiable experience (gods, demons), since "shared by many" does not mean true. Tastes (food, coffee) come from childhood memories. Feelings cannot be commanded, so swearing eternal love promises what cannot be guaranteed.
When to use: before choosing a creative angle, list inherited assumptions (gender stereotypes, "it's always been done this way"). In teams, separate what is verifiable from what is shared belief.

**Repression → projection → distorted reality**: The mistreated or unloved child erases painful memories to survive, but the emotion stays active and is **transferred onto others**. Someone whose parent could not stand them is sure that others cannot stand them, reads them as hostile, puts up invisible barriers, and so draws distance or even bullying. In a couple the projector acts like a child needing reassurance. Example: a small child on a train, sitting on a kind stranger's lap, keeps tense, feet pressed to the floor so as not to weigh on the stranger, because the child is projecting its parents. Another: a friend's casual elbow nudge read as hostility. Real security comes from being able to afford to be wrong, not from feeling right.
When to use: when someone reacts out of proportion. Consider projected expectations before assuming the message caused it, and clarify intentions and roles.

**Instinct: the wisdom of the species**: An innate endowment for survival that includes *perceiving others and danger*, not just fight/flight. The model splits an outward attack/flight instinct (called more "masculine") from an inward perceiving one (more "feminine"). Human forms of fight/flight/freeze include flattering the aggressor, freezing, fainting, screaming, all versions of animals "playing dead". Children sense who is dangerous, and the conditioned mind ("family is always good") overrides this. Childhood trauma leaves "closed doors" in instinct. A recovered instinct tells you whom to be with, whom to distrust, when to defend yourself. Removing conditioning does *not* make people violent, because identifying with others removes any pleasure in their suffering. Instinct lives in the body: techniques that detach from emotion detach from instinct. Original instinct is also life energy, repressed above all in sexuality and aggression, and recovering it means *first knowing oneself, then letting go*, not "being yourself" by venting.
When to use: as a reminder to treat bodily unease in a meeting or negotiation as a signal alongside the data, and to read some objections as defence (flight, freeze, flattery) rather than reasoned opinion.

**Speaking with the mind vs. with the soul**: Markers for speaking "from the soul": slower pace with reflective pauses, varied tone, eyes not fixed, more movement and expression, clarity that needs no interpreter. Markers for "from the mind": the same cadence and tone throughout, as in sermons and many lectures, plus acronyms and big words that need "interpreting". A useful format splits a talk half-and-half between prepared content and Q&A; speakers often feel more themselves in the spontaneous half. Labelling someone with a disorder is not understanding them.
When to use: in public speaking, video and presentations. Vary rhythm, pauses and tone. Start from the emotion you want to convey, then look for words. Write briefs without jargon.

**Escapes from reality (approved and disapproved)**: Disapproved escapes are alcohol, casinos, football fanaticism. Approved ones are study, work, career, wealth, shopping. In this lens both serve one function: not feeling one's own unhappiness. What we flee is our own emotions and painful past, not the outside world. Thinking itself can be an escape (catastrophic scenarios keep the mind busy). Many fathers retreat into work because family means emotion. Sleeping all Sunday works as anaesthesia. An escape is fine *as a pause*, a holiday or a break in therapy, and destructive *as a way of life*. The "great escape" is the refusal of death.
When to use: when reading consumer behaviour as a search for emotional relief, and when asking a hyperactive colleague or client what is really worrying them before adding tasks.

**"Passing the time"**: Filling every empty moment (phone on the bus, crosswords, TV one does not even like) covers the unease that surfaces when the mind is idle. Perceived time differs from physical time and shifts with age and mood. Almost nobody lives in the present: half the attention is in the past (anxiety from erased memories) and half in the future. The practice: switch everything off and stay alone. Meditation, if it only occupies or quiets the mind without understanding, is just brain rest.
When to use: as an attention insight (digital media lives in "empty" moments) and as a pause practice under pressure.

**"Feet on the ground" and the reality-descent disorder**: Grounding means feeling one's body (cold, tension, warmth) and handling concrete tasks: bills, bureaucracy, post, home security. People who are brilliant in the abstract and chaotic in admin are not stupid or lazy. They suffer from a "disorder of descent into reality", because coming down into the concrete brings childhood pain back up. A typical dream image: having to step down to a floor covered in pins. Phobias, in this reading, are displacement (fear of the parent moved onto an object). Obsessions (hand-washing, counting tiles, perfect order) are defences. Gambling, compulsive travel and power-seeking are flights, with power ultimately aimed at avoiding death.
When to use: when understanding procrastination and admin chaos without judgment, by simplifying and splitting bureaucratic tasks. In projects, ask whether new ideas and meetings are dodging a concrete knot.

**Experience vs. events (and wisdom)**: Experience is not what happened but what one *understood* about it. One can be eighty, with huge events behind them, and have no experience. Wisdom is knowledge of self and others that comes from feeling, not the head. It requires remembering what one suffered and feeling the resulting anger; understanding the parents "intellectually" is not enough. Fear is revalued as useful and attachment as the condition of love and identification. Maxims like "choose optimism", "don't attach" and "life is a game" (attributed to Epictetus, Buddhism, the Dalai Lama) are dismissed as inhuman. Example: a cat routs a small caiman because it senses its fear. Image: a well with poisoned water on top, which must be passed through to reach the good water.
When to use: for post-mortems ("lived through it" is not "learned from it", so analyse causes) and for imagining what the other person feels before addressing them.

**Positive thinking without inner positivity**: Forcing positive thoughts by will is self-conditioning, mind replacing soul, and lasts minutes at most. Will can make you persist in study or work but cannot change what you are inside. "Be yourself" assumes you know who you are. Real positivity has two sources: having been loved, or having worked through resentment toward those who hurt you. In a provocative framing, resentment toward an abuser is itself "positive" because it defends the self, and turning the other cheek feeds the bully. American-style relentless cheer is also criticised: progress needs sadness, the fall of schemas and broken equilibria. There are more serene people than happy ones.
When to use: as an antidote to hollow motivational tone. Imposed optimism rings false without facts behind it.

**Two-step self-knowledge: discovery, then release**: "Know thyself" means understanding *why*, not remembering what one did. It means meeting the dark, vulnerable parts. What we do not know, we cannot control: "losing control" means unknown parts are acting, for example staying with a partner one knows will leave. The model splits *discovering* a trauma from *releasing its emotions*. The second takes longer, and a trauma found "only with the mind" stays shut in a drawer. Practical methods: talk with one's inner child (of one's own sex) using simple present-tense questions without digging into the past; observe one's dreams, held to come from an uncontaminated, wise part; reclaim the "outlaw" parts (aggression, envy). One exercise is trying an aggressive face in the mirror. Citing Terence ("nothing human is alien to me"), executioner and saint both live in everyone. Recognising this reduces judgment, and understanding is not justifying.
When to use: for reflective prompts and for characters with split or disowned traits.

**The fall of the conditioned mind**: The final stage of a long inner path. The conditioned mind falls, not the reasoning one ("without a mind you don't exist"). Schemas fall first, then the past is reconstructed. At the end even a self-built protective figure (for example an imagined maternal deity) is let go, which can feel like a kind of suicide. Afterwards emotions come from inside rather than from others' opinions. The world looks "new" because it is no longer filed into boxes. When the protection falls, the threat falls with it. Pain grows, because one perceives others' suffering. The mind becomes a servant of the I. A fitting dream image is a queen served by a general.
When to use: as a narrative arc (dismantling inherited beliefs), not as a literal milestone to promise anyone.

## Key Concepts

- **Consapevolezza (awareness)**: knowing what one truly feels, accepting only what one has experienced, "recognising in a deconditioned way".
- **Mente ragionante / mente condizionata**: reasoning mind (a tool) vs. conditioned mind (received schemas, "the bandage over death").
- **Mente doppia**: the inner commentator voice, a superego extended to society.
- **Descrizione del mondo**: a received world-description that replaces direct experience. Citing Piaget, children pass through a "magical" period of feeling the world before adults impose that description.
- **Anima (soul)**: in a secular sense, the felt emotional life that "animates" us. It is "a seed to grow" and can regress to an "empty shell" of pure mind. Depression is the loss of hope. Disowning a part (the rebellious girl) means losing its energy; reclaiming it in adulthood returns that energy.
- **Rimozione (repression)**: erasing painful memories. The feeling persists and is displaced (the driver who fights a stranger).
- **Proiezione (projection)**: reading others through the template of the parents.
- **Subconscious vs. unconscious**: subconscious is what one knows but cannot recall right now; unconscious is what was repressed in childhood.
- **Emotions as fuel**: emotions are the "petrol" of psychic and physical energy. Repressing a memory takes its energy with it, which the model offers as an explanation for low energy and for poor memory.
- **Eidetic vs. conceptual memory**: photographic recall vs. grasping meaning. With age short-term memory fades and conceptual memory grows, which is the mark of ageing well. "What matters is not remembering but understanding." Memory also bends to what we want to believe (a paraphrase of Caesar).
- **Istinto ("saggezza della razza")**: innate perception and survival intelligence. Fight/flight/freeze/flatter.
- **Solve et coagula**: an alchemical tag for the method: dissolve, feel, understand, then draw experience. Used as a metaphor.
- **Opera al nero ("nigredo")**: a metaphor for self-knowledge as descent into the dark part that leads back to childhood.
- **Evasione dalla realtà**: approved and disapproved escapes from one's own emotions.
- **Disturbo di discesa nella realtà**: the model's coinage for trouble with concrete, bodily, administrative life.
- **Cervello destro / sinistro**: a popular framing that language and the alphabet pushed humanity from right-brain (emotion, image, music, unconscious) to left-brain (rationality). The goal is to unite them. Emotions dig "grooves" we fall back into, which is why reactions repeat. Men are said to tend to mistake their own fear for real danger, while women tend to let emotions out without observing them; the roles can be reversed. The hemisphere split is admitted to be a simplification.
- **Energy exchange (psychological reading)**: we "exchange" emotional climate with the people we live with, most of all with those close by. Fragile people absorb more. Mood (wanting to do, live, know) is the practical gauge. Fear of contagion and total isolation are both forms of suffering. A "clear conscience" is not elevation if the duty was dictated by authority. It draws on Le Bon and crowd psychology: a highly emotional crowd is more suggestible.
- **Fragility of calm states**: calm reached in protected conditions (samadhi, retreats) does not survive daily problems without real understanding of oneself.
- **"Spoken from experience or from reading?"**: a filter for judging guides and sources. Trust people who say how they know (read, heard, lived). Distrust promises of "extraordinary" results with no checkable process.
- **Judgment vs. prejudice / observation vs. evaluation**: noticing a fact (a colour, a stammer) is not judging the person's worth.

## Mental Models

- **Doubt is the engine**: asking questions about everything, including oneself and the speaker ("listen to me, don't believe me"), drives awareness. Most people prefer to stay children and have a parent tell them what is true.
- **Anxiety eats memory and presence**: attention tied up in what was erased (image: a lion in the garden, erased out of fear, that still absorbs the mind) leaves less for the present.
- **Feeling precedes chemistry**: emotions are mediated chemically, but here the feeling *drives* the chemical change. Everything can be read on physical, chemical and psychic levels.
- **Emotions are what gets recorded**: the brain mainly records emotions, and what counts is how many links between neurons we build, not how many neurons we keep (citing Cajal). Memorable messages touch something emotional.
- **Calm technique ≠ change**: Eastern or bodily techniques act on the immediate reaction but bring one back to the mind. Lasting change requires understanding.
- **Seeing others needs seeing the parents first**: those who never truly looked at their parents read everyone through the same frame. The other's emotions are understood with emotions, not with the mind.
- **Approved escapes hide in plain sight**: overwork and constant study can do the same job as drinking.
- **Death acceptance as the last schema**: accepting mortality (Lucretius, "tempus fugit" on Roman sundials) is what frees one to enjoy life, like being on holiday somewhere one is only passing through.
- **Cycles and rituals**: New Year and seasonal rites speak to a deep human sense of ending and renewal (Janus, the solstice "unconquered sun"). What counts at life's end is the awareness one has gathered, not the rules kept or milestones reached.
- **Distrust the infallible guru**: be wary of guides who pronounce verdicts and are not spontaneous, or who promise prodigies. Anecdote: a guru spat on in a lift who does not react is anaesthetised, not evolved. Submitting to others makes one lose part of oneself.

## Applications

- **Understanding motivations**: read compulsive busyness, screen-filling, thrill-seeking and status consumption as possible relief from unfelt emotion. Read admin chaos and procrastination as avoidance, not laziness. Read some objections as fight/flight/freeze/flattery rather than reasoned positions. These are hypotheses to explore, never labels.
- **Communication / persuasion**: vary pace, pause and tone, and start from the emotion before choosing words. Avoid acronyms and jargon. Prefer questions that make the audience think over packaged answers. Images reach people emotionally and words reach them mentally. Conceptual, emotionally anchored messages stick better than lists of data. People under stress take in less, so in workshops lower the pressure and focus on concepts. "Don't believe me, check whether it fits you" is a non-coercive way to build trust. Giving half the time to questions works well.
- **Research and briefs**: never assume what the other person thinks. Ask "how are you, what do you think" first. Separate the stated need from the underlying one. Remember that people believe what they like believing, so compare testimonies with facts. Audiences read facts through what they are predisposed to see; example: seeing a light in the sky and thinking "weather balloon", not UFO.
- **Coaching-style reflection** (not therapy): "Am I acting from my reasoning mind or my conditioned voice?"; "Which inherited assumptions am I carrying into this decision?"; "Is this busyness a pause or a way of life?"; "Have I lived through this, or learned from it?"; "What is my body telling me in this meeting?"; recognising one's own emotional reaction before calling it rational.
- **Writing characters**: the mind/soul split (knowing vs. feeling), the "glass" between a character and others, the abstract genius who cannot open the post, the father who hides in work, approved vs. disapproved escapes, a breakdown-and-renewal arc ("fall of the conditioned mind"), a disowned "outlaw" part that returns, and the woman who has child after child to avoid looking back at a painful childhood.
- **Team climate**: emotional climate spreads, and a tense leader affects the whole group. Emotional people are often silenced because they stir emotions in others, so give them room. Explain the reasons behind rules instead of imposing them, a parenting principle that transfers to teams. "Know yourself before venting" warns against impulsive communication. Security means being allowed to be wrong, so allow imperfect drafts.
- **Marketing / audience insight**: cyclical symbols (moon, wheel, acorn, seasons) and calendar rituals work as storytelling archetypes of renewal when used as *images*, not claims. A crowd in strong emotion is more suggestible, which puts an ethical duty on the communicator. Recognise induced status needs and use them consciously or decline to.

## Caveats

- **Status of the material**: this is a psychoanalytically oriented interpretive model, a reflection and communication knowledge base, **not clinical guidance**. Do not use it to diagnose, and do not suggest that anyone dig up trauma alone. The model itself points to therapists for that.
- **Aligned with mainstream psychology/communication**: stress and anxiety impair attention and memory; memory is reconstructive and biased by what we want to believe; short-term vs. long-term memory; emotion strengthens encoding; avoidance and experiential escape; fight/flight/freeze; cultural and social conditioning; the value of empathy, non-judgmental listening and separating observation from evaluation; emotional contagion and crowd suggestibility; calming techniques not replacing understanding; vocal variety in public speaking; scepticism toward charismatic gurus. Repression and projection are real but *debated* psychoanalytic concepts.
- **This model's own or contested claims**: dreams as the *only* valid route to childhood trauma ("recovered memories" are highly contested); that nearly everyone was abused; that phobias, obsessions or compulsive sexuality signal childhood abuse (clinical psychology recognises many causes); that therapies working on the present (e.g. CBT) leave trauma untouched; that about 90% of people quit analysis on discovering a parent's harm (no source); the right/left-brain history of humanity (a pop simplification); masculine/feminine instinct splits; total denial of free will (a philosophical stance); the claims that Freud was forced to recant and invented the Oedipus complex, and the statements on infant sexuality (contested by research); the readings of philosophers and Eastern traditions (personal and simplified); the claim that alien-abduction accounts are always substitute delusions for family sexual abuse (a hypothesis; such experiences are often linked to sleep phenomena such as sleep paralysis); a hypothesis linking Alzheimer's to being "detached from reality".
- **Excluded esoteric content** (one-line mention only): the wider tradition also discusses reincarnation, the "astral", magic, chakras and the third eye, alchemical stages, zodiac and memory, homeopathy and "water memory" (no scientific support), presences and contact with the dead. Where a psychological point (doubt, grounding, accepting death, distrusting gurus) was wrapped in this material, only the psychological point is kept here.
- **Historical and political asides** (matriarchy, Roman calendar, religion as business) are polemical readings and should not be cited as fact.
