# Chapter: Ego, False Self and Identity

## Core Idea
In this model the ego (the plain "I") is healthy and necessary and should not be "demolished", as many spiritual teachings demand. What needs taking apart is the **false ego**: a defensive self the child builds to please parents who could not love it as it was. A person's sense of worth, beauty, acceptability and identity is set like an "imprinting" by the parents' mirror, above all the mother's. When that mirror was rejecting, the child concludes "I am not worth it" rather than "my parent does not love me", and as an adult props up a **secondary identity** on outside supports: career, money, looks, partner, group, applause. Such an identity is rigid, easily hurt by criticism, and always measured by an **external reference** that never says "enough". The way out is not to manufacture self-esteem. It is to understand why it is missing: see the real parents, re-own the rejected parts (the inner child, fragility, anger), and move to an **internal reference** ("how does this resonate in me?"). Then the need to define, prove or display oneself drops away. Parts of this are close to attachment theory and psychodynamic ideas. The model's versions are absolute ("almost all parents", "only the parents") and are a theoretical position, not established findings.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Ego vs. false ego vs. conditioned mind**: This lens splits three things that popular spirituality lumps together. The *ego/io* is healthy self-respect and the capacity to see others ("I come first, then others, except one's children"). The *false ego* is a defensive persona built for parental approval. The *conditioned mind* is the set of received rules that runs the false ego. The thing to dismantle is the conditioned mind and its false ego, never the I. "Destroy the ego" teachings are criticised here for asking people to erase themselves in order to be accepted. Wisdom needs a firm I that can doubt, as Socrates did.
When to use: when separating healthy self-assertion from defensive self-inflation in a person or character, or when weighing "ego-death" style messaging.

**The child's choice: "I'm not worth it" over "they don't love me"**: The model's central mechanism. A child cannot accuse the parent it depends on, so it blames itself. Self-blame keeps hope alive ("if I change, I'll be loved"), but it hardens into lifelong self-devaluation. The mechanism recurs across the model's treatment of fragility, acceptance and liking oneself.
When to use: when explaining chronic self-blame, people-pleasing or "imposter" feelings in a reflective (non-diagnostic) way.

**Primary mirror → secondary identity**: Identity starts in the parents' gaze (mother first; the father strengthens what is already positive or negative). If the child is not welcomed, it shows itself to the world as unlovable: shy, closed or aggressive, depending on temperament. One parental judgment ("you're a liar") is felt as a collective verdict ("everyone will think I'm a liar"). The child then builds an opposite, approved identity, called here *secondary*. That identity later leans on group, partner, money, family status and image, and it holds only until a real pain arrives.
When to use: when reading why someone insists on appearances, status objects or a professional role as "who they are".

**External vs. internal reference (centrality of the soul)**: Most people, in this view, have no inner centre. Their worth depends on parents, teachers, institutions, audiences, grades, promotions. An internal reference means judging by "how it resonates in me" while still listening to others. The external reference is the internalised parent whose approval is chased through career, love, money and applause, and it always answers "not enough".
When to use: when diagnosing (in the everyday sense) over-sensitivity to feedback, or when designing feedback that separates the work from the person.

**The "can I change my mind?" test (fear of change)**: Here fear of change (of city, job, partner and above all of *ideas*) is clinging to the idealised image of the parents. That clinging later moves onto religion, parties and leaders ("I vote for the party even when it's wrong", like loyalty to a football team or a parent). The test: *Can I change my mind about my parents, politics, God, people?* Whatever cannot be questioned is a received schema. People with internal references can find out that a person or party is not what they believed without collapsing. People with only external references go rigid. Fear of moving house is read as fear of not being up to it, because the city was serving as an external anchor.
When to use: as a self-check in creative or strategic debates; when people resist a change, to ask whether they fear "not being good enough" more than they disagree on the merits.

**Recognising the false ego in action**: Signs the model names: stiffening and feeling wounded when someone doubts what we say we are; withdrawal or outsized aggression; needing to prove something to the parents (for example through career); an **internalised observer** that judges even when we are alone (the image is the "perfect English gentleman" who uses sugar tongs when nobody is watching). The remedy is to understand where it came from. Then other people's attacks stop hurting, because one sees *why* they attack.
When to use: when handling hostile feedback. Ask what the other person is defending before answering the content.

**Personal importance**: The conditioned mind builds a sense of being special (chosen race, favourite child of the gods) to escape a feeling of nothingness and to deny death. Hitler is cited as an extreme case of where this leads. Losing personal importance feels like depression to the mind and like lightness to the soul: other people's opinions stop mattering, one stops fearing ridicule, and opinions loosen. The warning for anyone on a self-development path: the biggest risk is "getting a big head". Stay simple and dignified, neither above nor below others.
When to use: when reading grandiosity, status-seeking, or "guru" self-presentation.

**Personality vs. depersonalisation**: Personality has an innate base (Jung's introvert/extravert; Liszt reading scores as a child). Family and school "depersonalise" the child by teaching that what it feels and thinks does not count ("be quiet, you don't understand", hiding things, routinely shooting down its ideas). Schools teach obedience over intelligence. Limits should be *reality's* limits (fire burns), not orders that leave the child unable to react. Remove the "authoritarian mind", not authority. Following Freud, psychic health is a matter of *quantity*, not kind: most people are partly depersonalised without knowing it.
When to use: when explaining learned passivity and dependence on rules or external figures in teams and audiences.

**The conscious mask (persona)**: Everyone builds a mask in childhood to be accepted. To know oneself one takes it off, then puts it back on *knowingly* for social life. You can tell you are unfulfilled when every criticism wounds the character. The advice: ask why the other person is speaking, not what they say about you.
When to use: when separating a brand's or person's public tone from inner authenticity.

## Key Concepts

- **Ego / io**: in this model, the healthy self, made of the body and the person one has to look after, self-respect, and the capacity to see others. Not to be destroyed.
- **False ego (falso ego)**: a defence, not a moral fault. It is a self built to please non-loving parents and later projected onto others, who are then experienced as judging parents.
- **Conditioned mind (mente condizionata)**: the received rules and beliefs that produce the false ego and the inner judging voice ("you're not worth it"). It is distinct from the **reasoning mind** (mente ragionante), which is a useful tool.
- **Imprinting**: borrowed from Konrad Lorenz. The first sense of acceptance and worth is fixed by the parents, and in humans the window is longer because rearing is longer.
- **Secondary identity**: an opposite or approved identity built in answer to a parental verdict, held up by possessions, looks, roles and group.
- **External / internal reference**: whether one's measure of worth lies with others (parents, institutions, audience) or in one's own felt experience.
- **Inner child (bambino interiore)**: the child part that has to be understood and loved by the adult. In this view it is the only real cure for low self-esteem ("no adult love compensates for the love that was missing"). One can also talk with it, with simple present-tense questions (see Chapter A2).
- **Internalised observer**: the judging gaze carried inside even in solitude, a sign of conditioning.
- **Personal importance (importanza personale)**: the mind's need to feel special and immortal.
- **Depersonalisation (spersonalizzazione)**: the model's term for the social flattening of innate personality. **Not** the clinical depersonalisation disorder.
- **Fragility as sensitivity**: fragility is a capacity to feel, intensified by adverse experience. It carries the risk of suffering and also the capacity for joy. It is a gift to defend, not a weakness. The "armour" protects in the moment but cuts one off from emotion.
- **Feeling beautiful/ugly**: an inner verdict on one's acceptability, not an aesthetic fact. "Feeling beautiful" equals self-acceptance. Typical examples: a very beautiful young woman in a group who says "I feel ugly"; Marilyn Monroe and Naomi Campbell, read here as insecure despite their looks.
- **Spoiled vs. contented child**: a "spoiled" child is given things to get rid of it. A child contented with affection does not keep demanding objects. Constant demands for things signal missing affection.
- **"Being found out"**: the insecure person expects that partner or employer will sooner or later discover they are worthless.
- **Comparison as self-devaluation**: constant comparison with others already signals low self-worth. One can measure oneself only against oneself. "Winner/loser" vocabulary signals a culture permanently at war.
- **Narcissist (popular sense)**: someone who seeks identity through applause, like an ageing actor who falls into depression when the applause stops. Someone who ends up with a narcissist should ask why they missed the signals, often because they needed that person.

## Mental Models

- **Self-esteem cannot be "installed"**: in this view self-esteem is not built by adult techniques. You understand why it is missing and re-parent the inner child. Promises of quick self-esteem contradict the model's thesis.
- **Others accept us as much as we accept ourselves**: the self-accepting person is not suspicious, gives off goodwill and is welcomed more easily. At a job interview they present themselves at ease, without constructed "scenes".
- **Fear of the judge, not the outcome**: when someone fears the *person's* judgment (teacher, examiner) more than the result (the grade), a non-accepting parent is in play.
- **Repetition in partner choice**: people who were not loved choose partners who do not love them, hoping "this time" to win love ("nobody loved by their parents picks a partner who doesn't love them").
- **Quantity, not kind**: collecting, shopping, tattoos and cosmetic surgery are not pathological in themselves. Obsessive *quantity* signals a gap in identity (curious collector vs. hoarder).
- **The surgery fallacy**: changing the body to be accepted (surgical leg lengthening, cosmetic surgery) does not touch the inner verdict.
- **Self-hate turned outward**: whoever hates their own fragility hates it in others, which the model links to bullying. Bullies side with the stronger party and repeat what they suffered.
- **Rigidity is fear**: someone who cannot be questioned is defending something they do not know about. Do not chase people who will not reason. The self-declared "authentic" person who sees everyone else as fake is often an aggressor who feels like a victim while attacking.
- **Role-based identity is brittle**: if identity rests on job, skill, beauty or culture, all of which are comparisons, it collapses with a demotion. "I am my emotions" is the alternative offered.
- **Spontaneity vs. aggression**: having different ideas and habits without harming anyone is spontaneity and is generally accepted. Aggression is rejected for other reasons. People confuse the two.
- **The ego-centred stance ("al centro del mondo")**: most people feel only themselves and see others like characters in a film, behind a "glass". They assume they already know what the other will say. It can also be negative ("everyone notices my flaws", "this misfortune shouldn't have happened to *me*"). The other is "another us": what we cannot understand in them is a part of ourselves we do not understand.

## Applications

- **Understanding motivations**: read status display, compulsive achievement, defensive reactions and approval-seeking as possible ways of shoring up a secondary identity built on an external reference. Treat this as a hypothesis to test by listening, not a verdict about anyone.
- **Handling hostile feedback / conflict**: before answering the content, ask "what is this person defending?". It is often their image of themselves, not the product. Do not chase someone who will not reason. A boss who raises his voice often fears not being heard: reassure him kindly rather than react.
- **Giving feedback**: separate judgment of the work from judgment of the person, because approval-dependent people hear personal attacks in neutral notes. Use specific, non-personal observations. Value proposals even when rejecting them ("good idea, but…"), a parenting principle transposed to teams.
- **Coaching-style reflection** (not therapy): the "can I change my mind about X?" test; "am I acting from an inner or outer reference?"; noticing when one reacts "like a child" (pleasing, over-justifying) with clients or superiors; "is my need for applause a pleasure or a need?" (the comparison: a good lunch vs. a sandwich).
- **Writing characters**: a strong engine for backstory. A character who fears the judge more than the outcome, chooses unloving partners, has a role-based identity that cracks on demotion, wears a conscious or unconscious mask, uses career "to prove it to the parents", or reads as "spoiled" when they are really emotionally neglected. The gentleman-with-sugar-tongs image fits the internalised observer well.
- **Marketing / audience insight** (with an ethical caveat): audiences marked by insecurity respond more to promises of *acceptance* than of perfection. Much advertising exploits the need for status and superiority, and this frame asks you to notice that lever and avoid it where it plays on inadequacy (beauty, "winner", status). Displayed beauty is not perceived value; authenticity and relationship matter. People bond with the emotions they feel around a brand more than with its self-description. Audiences that react violently to a campaign are often defending their identity, not the product.
- **Team management**: people who seem lazy, withdrawn or "too sensitive" may be struggling or picking up details others miss. Ask before labelling. An identity built only on a job title is fragile during reorganisations. Competitive rankings breed anxiety, so value different roles instead of comparing them.

## Caveats

- **Status of the material**: this is a psychoanalytically oriented interpretive model. Everything above is *the model's thesis*, not clinical evidence, and nothing here is clinical advice.
- **Aligned with mainstream psychology**: early attachment and caregiver responsiveness shape self-worth (attachment theory). Defensive reactions to criticism, the self-blame of maltreated children, and projection are recognised ideas, though psychoanalytic concepts are debated. Empathy as non-judgmental listening, the costs of emotional armouring, the value of separating observation from judgment, and confirmation bias and attachment to one's own schemas are also on solid ground.
- **This model's own or contested claims**: that self-esteem depends *only* on the parents and that no later relationship or therapy can compensate (mainstream research recognises later experiences and therapy); that *most* parents "could not love" or are "monsters"; that shyness and insecurity are *never* innate (only introversion/extraversion is accepted as innate, while temperament research is more nuanced); that the mother is the base and the father only reinforces; that the foetus perceives acceptance; that forgiveness is harmful or a mere schema; that "Incels" are sons of abusive mothers; the claim that someone who loses their I eventually commits suicide.
- **Terminology warning**: "depersonalisation" here is not the clinical disorder. "Narcissist" is used loosely. "Inner child" is a common therapeutic metaphor, not an autonomous personality in the clinical sense.
- **Excluded esoteric material**: parts of the wider tradition tie identity to reincarnation, the "astral", magic and alchemical stages. Personal importance is sometimes framed as a precondition for "doing magic"; the psychological point (letting go of the need to feel special) stands without it.
- **Use**: never use these frames to diagnose or label real people. Use them as hypotheses for empathy, communication and fiction, and check against facts and what the person actually says.
