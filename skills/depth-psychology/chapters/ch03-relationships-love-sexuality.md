# Chapter: Relationships, Love, Sexuality and the Sexes

## Core Idea
This model's central claim about adult love is that most of it is not love at all but a **re-enactment of childhood**: partners are chosen, idealised, feared and clung to as stand-ins for the parents who did not love us enough. Jealousy, fear of abandonment, emotional dependency, "choosing the bad boy", even some sexual difficulties are read as the *projection* of an unresolved parent onto the partner. Real love is possible and reciprocal, but only when part of us is free from the need to be confirmed, which requires **separating from the parents** (above all the mother), seeing them as they really were, and accepting one's own "inner child". Around this psychological core sits a strongly ideological frame: a patriarchal, religiously "sex-phobic" society that turned sex into sin and difference into hierarchy, and a polarity between **mind** (reasoning, organising, outward, symbolically masculine) and **soul/anima** (feeling, inward, symbolically feminine) that must be held in balance rather than one defeating the other. The attachment-related core sits close to mainstream ideas (attachment theory, projection, transference). The model's gender generalisations, its historical claims about matriarchy and its single-cause explanations do not.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The Partner-as-Parent Model (projection of the parents).** Whatever we do not remember about our parents we "re-play" onto others. In couples each partner seeks what was missing in childhood: in this account the woman tends to look for paternal protection and the man for maternal care. The partner is put "on a pedestal", which means refusing to see them as they are. A healthy union starts from understanding the partner's inner child without idealising them.
When to use: to explain why a relationship produces reactions out of proportion to the present facts, or why someone keeps choosing the same type of partner.

**Exogenous vs. Endogenous Jealousy.** Jealousy comes in two types. *Exogenous* jealousy responds to real behaviour: the partner flirts or keeps "a spare tyre in the car", read as a sign that the partner loves poorly and is insecure. *Endogenous* jealousy appears even when the partner behaves impeccably. It is traced to an early abandonment, usually by the opposite-sex parent, who stopped being affectionate as the child grew more independent. Endogenous jealousy shows up as interrogating the partner about exes and as a strange relief when a betrayal "confirms" the fear. Jealousy is neither a female trait nor a proof of love.
When to use: to tell apart a reaction to what someone actually did from a reaction to their own history.

**Abandonment = Re-lived Parental Abandonment.** The core formula: fear of being left by a lover is fear of being left by a parent. The sign is disproportion: when a break-up devastates someone even though the relationship was not a great love, or when a person who was tired of the partner collapses once the *other* says "I'm leaving", the pain points back to childhood. The suggested question is "which parent abandoned me?".
When to use: to interpret grief that is out of scale with the bond that was lost.

**Dependency as Prolonged Childhood (Dependency I and II).** Dependency on bosses, spouses, institutions or God continues dependency on the parents. Affective dependency is defined as "depending on someone who doesn't love you, hoping they will", which ends in self-blame ("I'm not good enough"). The unloved child splits the parents into a "good" one (the real, idealised parent) and a "bad" one (projected onto other people or the boss), and this split is the root of fear of others' judgement. The dynamics: when one partner becomes dependent, the other withdraws, because they feel used as a parental figure and not *seen*. Being servile ("I'll be the good child so they'll love me") does not produce love. People who were loved are not dependent, and they can tell their parents what they dislike.
When to use: to explain people-pleasing, fear of judgement, and relationships where one person serves and the other pulls away.

**Reciprocity as a Health Thermometer.** Reciprocity means answering *in the same tone* as what you receive: love with love, hostility with defence. It does not require equal feelings, so one can refuse kindly someone who is kind. It is treated as a principle of psychic and instinctual health, and a gauge of how much our mental schemas condition us. "Turn the other cheek" is criticised for training bullies. People who say "I'm good, the others are bad" often cannot get themselves respected. The model separates goodness from submission and prefers to talk about *health*. Exception: between parent and child reciprocity does not apply in the same way, because the parent welcomes the child as they are.
When to use: to judge whether a relationship (personal or professional) is mutual or has slid into subordination.

**Understanding over Forgiveness and Compulsory Love.** Forgiveness and compassion are *outcomes of maturity*, not duties. Imposed forgiveness is a form of *repression* (rimozione): it puts the forgiver "a step above" and betrays the self. It can also hide a wish to earn a rewarding-punishing God's favour, or masochism. Reconciliation needs two people, and "reconciling alone" happens with a figure of one's fantasy. Love cannot be commanded: a feeling is not an activity, and the will has no power over it. "Whoever cannot hate cannot love." The real duty is **awareness**: understanding why one loves or hates. Understanding does not mean excusing, and it does not stop you from stopping someone who does harm. With abuse of children, one should become aware rather than "good", and side with the victim.
When to use: to reflect on situations where someone feels guilty for not forgiving or not loving.

**Identification (immedesimazione).** Identification is picking up the other's emotional identity in that moment, without judging or absolving them. The method for understanding behaviour you dislike: ask what you would feel if you acted that way, since everyone carries "a spark" of what moves the other, violence included. If we cannot identify with someone who suffers, that person stands for a part of ourselves we reject. Hatred of gay people, other races, women or children is, in this reading, a failure of identification. Identification is separate from trust: trust has to be calibrated to the risk (€5 in a shop vs. €10,000 or moving in together).
When to use: as a perspective-taking step before judging a person or a group.

**The Caduceus / "Two in One".** Mercury's caduceus, two serpents intertwined so that neither wins, is read as the balance of the psyche. Inner opposites (instinct and mind, destructive and constructive parts) are to be **accepted and harmonised, not transcended** at a "higher" level. On this point the model explicitly departs from Nicholas of Cusa's *coincidentia oppositorum* and from Jung. Denying one's own capacity for cruelty ("nothing human is alien to me", Terence) is a form of repression, and what is erased is lost. The aim is to understand *why* one acts, not to be good.
When to use: to frame inner conflict as something to integrate, not eliminate.

**The Inner Complementary (anima/animus revised).** The search for "the masculine" or "the feminine" is really a search for an inner complementary part, whatever one's sexual orientation. Men seek their *soul*, women their *instinctual mind*. This reworks Jung: the animus is not a reasoning mind but an instinctual mind that "feels". Once this part is found, one no longer *needs* to be loved: a partner can enrich but is not a necessity. Without it, outside love never satisfies.
When to use: to frame the difference between wanting a partner and needing one.

**Sex as Co-created Ritual.** Sexuality goes wrong when it is reduced to tension release and to proof of worth (the "flags" of conquest). The proposal: sex as a creative, shared act that the partners agree on and talk through in advance. It is described as a playful, ritualised "performance" in which each person expresses parts of themselves that social life gives no room to. A role lived with real feeling is "used up" and leaves space for something else. Games with sadomasochistic elements are held to be healthy only when ritualised, light and humiliating to no one. The part that carries general value is the emphasis on communication and mutual consent between partners.

## Key Concepts
- **Mente condizionata (conditioned mind):** the layer of mind made of schemas and rules passed on by family, society and religion. It is distinct from mind as such ("without a mind you cannot live"). It is what turns difference into hierarchy and labels into identities. Its "fall" is the goal of inner work.
- **Anima vs. mente (soul vs. mind):** *anima* is everything felt inside (emotions, fears, pleasures) and *mente* is what reasons and organises toward the outside. They are coded symbolically as feminine and masculine, and the call is for harmony, not the victory of one side.
- **Mentalisation (mentalizzazione):** the thesis that humanity shifted from "soul-beings" who feel to "mind-beings" who organise, because life in ever larger groups needed it. Note: this is *not* Fonagy's clinical concept of mentalization, which means almost the opposite.
- **Projection / "the screen":** we project our parents onto the people we meet, as onto a screen. Figures we do not remember are the ones most projected.
- **Inner child (bambino interiore):** the sensitive part we had to repress as children. Loving others requires loving one's own inner child first. (Developed further in the family chapter.)
- **Separation from the mother:** described as the fundamental and rarely completed separation. It does not mean ceasing to love the mother. It means no longer laying childhood images over the mother and over others. Until it happens, one sees the world "through a porthole" or "a diving suit": less pain, but also less joy, and no real feeling of others.
- **Libido investment and heartbreak:** when someone is lost, psychic and physical energy stays "invested" in them, and it takes time to recover, in proportion to how important the bond was.
- **"Ghosts" and loneliness:** solitude itself is not sad. It becomes sad when we are "alone" with inner ghosts, harsh or unloving parental figures who make us feel unworthy of love. Desperate searching for company is a search for a benevolent adult who will chase them away.
- **Love vs. fear:** "you cannot love someone you fear". Fear produces aggression, like a growling dog, and this holds for spouses, bosses and a feared God.
- **"Water up to the throat":** the image of the person who was little loved and lives a relationship like a drowning person clutching a handhold without looking at it. They fail to see the partner, and the partner senses it.
- **Power games of the insecure:** not calling first, hiding that one cares, asking "do you really love me?" over and over. The question is useless because the partner's *behaviour*, not their words, is the answer.
- **"Saving the past":** choosing cold or elusive partners who resemble the parent who withheld love, so that winning them over feels like rescuing one's emotional history. Openly loving partners then seem less interesting.
- **"Foreign territory":** an image for true love, which is bi-directional and keeps a free part that looks at the other as unknown land.
- **Violence ≠ strength:** a reading of "why women choose scoundrels". A daughter who grew up with a dominant, violent father may confuse violence with strength and find a gentle partner "soft". Violence is seen as an *inability* to master one's aggression, so as weakness.
- **"I'm ugly" as a mask (incel analysis):** the claim that the conviction "I lose because I'm ugly" covers a deeper sense of inadequacy, linked to a cancelled early experience; such men often are not ugly. The incel is framed as seeking confirmation of worth through a woman's sexual acceptance rather than seeking love.
- **Disproportion as a signal:** a recurring heuristic. When a small criticism triggers huge anger, or a minor loss triggers devastation, the reaction "belongs" to the past. The work is to tell past anger from present anger.
- **Sessuofobia (sex-phobic society):** the thesis that family, Church and school treat sex as taboo and pleasure as suspect, because people who find pleasure do not sacrifice themselves for authority. Related ideas: the **double standard** (a man with many partners is "virile", a woman is condemned), obsessive need for sex as a way to calm abandonment anxiety rather than a sign of virility, and the claim that people who live sexuality well do not chase it daily but take care of it.
- **Healthy vs. obsessive reasoning:** reasoning that stays in contact with feelings vs. reasoning cut off from them.
- **Orientation as a spectrum:** the hetero/homosexual split is an arbitrary definition of the mind; each person has both components in different proportions, and the lived orientation depends heavily on social pressure (homosexuality is documented in about 1,500 animal species). The ethical line: respect for the weaker party. No sex with minors. Between consenting, aware adults sex is legitimate.
- **Homophobia as a symptom:** intense hostility toward homosexuality is read as a sign that the theme "touches" the person inside: fear of being dominated or "passive", tied to the supposed superiority of masculine over feminine. An intrusive "you are gay" inner voice is distinguished from actual orientation. (The causal attributions are flagged in Caveats.)
- **Value as arbitrary:** no sex, race or species is worth more. One can only say a person is *destructive* because they harm others. Equality is a social right, while evolution lies in diversity.

## Mental Models
- **Present reaction = past wound + present trigger.** Before reading a reaction as a judgement on the current situation, ask how much of it is out of scale. The model treats the excess as the historical part.
- **Behaviour over words.** In love and in trust, what the other *does* answers "do you love me?". Watch actions, not declarations.
- **Need blinds.** The more someone needs confirmation, the less they see the other person, and the other feels unseen and pulls away. Dependency produces the abandonment it fears.
- **Servility doesn't buy love or esteem.** In couples and with authorities, compliance without the ability to object breeds withdrawal, not respect.
- **Say it once.** A model of calm authority: explain a point clearly once, at most twice, then stop. Insisting signals a need for approval and hands power to the other person. Do not shout, say it once, and let it sink in.
- **Mirror statements.** Instead of "you hurt me", say "what you said hurt me because it reminded me of…". You speak about your own history instead of accusing.
- **Calibrate truth to the listener.** The "real woman" in this model does not always say everything: "we always speak as two", and a truth the listener cannot take in can wound.
- **Hold opposites.** The caduceus model: integrate instinct and analysis, destructive and constructive impulses, rather than cutting one off.
- **Trust is risk-weighted.** Trusting someone and how much you entrust to them are separate decisions.
- **Difference ≠ hierarchy.** When a difference gets read as "worth less", defensiveness follows. Stating that the parts are of equal value lowers the reaction.

## Applications
- **Understanding motivations.** Use the partner-as-parent and disproportion models as *hypotheses*, never as verdicts, when a person's reaction (to a break-up, a rival, a change of supplier or contact person) far exceeds the facts. Fear of being replaced explains a lot of over-reaction. Steady reassurance and transparency help more than self-justification.
- **Writing characters.** The material is rich for fiction: the daughter drawn to a "strong" man who confuses violence with strength; the partner who, tired of the relationship, falls apart when the *other* leaves; the lover who keeps choosing cold partners to "save the past"; the servile spouse who brings breakfast in bed and still feels unloved (the Socrates and Xanthippe reading); the endogenous-jealous partner who quizzes about exes; the man whose "I'm ugly" covers a deeper shame. These give inner logic and backstory to relationship arcs.
- **Communication and conflict.** Mirror statements ("it hit me because…"), saying a thing once and calmly, separating an opinion about a behaviour from a judgement of the person, and answering an aggressive person by first asking where the reaction comes from. These map well onto feedback and negotiation. Recognising one's own people-pleasing pattern helps one negotiate more firmly with clients and bosses.
- **Coaching-style reflection.** Reflective questions an agent can offer, without interpreting for the person: "Is this reaction in proportion to what happened?", "Is this relationship reciprocal?", "Am I serving in order to be loved?", "What would I feel if I behaved like them?". The agent must not tell someone their pain "comes from" a parent or from abuse.
- **Marketing and audience insight.** Several points: (a) Taboo and shame limit what an audience will hear on intimate subjects, so set the tone before choosing the message. (b) Need for approval and fear of "what people will think" are powerful levers; use them responsibly. Do not promise "the right person" or a product as the cure for an inner void. (c) An audience's idea of "strength" may mix up dominance and reliability, so check which one a brand evokes. (d) Gender segmentation should be a statistical tendency at most, not a rule. The model itself says to look at individuals, and its own stereotypes are a *risk*, not a targeting criterion. (e) Rigid gender codes (pink/blue, fixed roles) are strong social schemas worth testing rather than assuming. (f) Disproportionate hostility in comments may say more about the commenter than about the topic. (g) Messages that pair logic with emotion reflect the mind/soul balance. (h) Be wary of "final secret" guru marketing, which the model itself criticises.

## Caveats
- **Status of the material.** This is popular depth psychology mixed with esoteric belief. Nothing here is clinical evidence or clinical advice, and nothing here should be used to diagnose anyone.
- **What aligns with mainstream psychology:** that early attachment experiences shape adult relationships (attachment theory); that projection and transference exist; that jealousy and fear of abandonment can be linked to insecure attachment; that affective dependency is a studied phenomenon; that stress, mood and immune function interact; that homosexuality is not a pathology; and that communication and consent between partners matter. Even these are stated far more deterministically than research supports. Mainstream research accepts the influence of early bonds, but not linear rules.
- **This model's own single-cause explanations are contested.** Its claims include: abuse lies behind most fear of abandonment; incel identity and erectile or ejaculatory problems almost always stem from early sexual abuse by the mother (even dismissing physiological causes); homophobia comes from repressed homosexuality or paternal abuse; and imposed forgiveness is always harmful. All of these are unsupported positions, and some would be harmful if applied to real people. Sexual dysfunction has many medical and psychological causes. Incel communities are a documented radicalisation risk, but the single origin proposed here is not established.
- **Gender generalisations.** Statements such as "men seek sex to confirm worth and release stress, women seek affection", "women are more soul, men more mind", "women seek a protective father, hence age gaps", "male fatherhood is lukewarm", "testosterone changes character (castrated cats)", "gay men are more intuitive" and claims about mothers-in-law are sweeping generalisations. Gender differences are widely debated in research. Do not reuse them as facts about individuals or audiences.
- **History and religion.** The model's claims include a historical, peaceful matriarchy (Bachofen, mother-goddess figurines), the Amazons as a real people, the Church "inventing" marriage and prostitution to control sex, and Rome as a non-racist society. Scholars contest them or treat them as simplifications. Its readings of Jung, Cusanus, Freud and Buddhist and Christian texts are idiosyncratic. Brain lateralisation ("right brain = unconscious") is a popular simplification.
- **Political or moral stances** (on prostitution, marriage as an unkeepable promise, the "real woman" tending not to marry) are positions of this model, not findings.
- **Terminology trap.** "Mentalisation" here has nothing to do with the clinical term.
- **Esoteric content excluded.** Material on magic (right-hand/left-hand path, "black astral", entities, energy exchanges), alchemy, dreams as proof of inner "acquisitions", sex as a magical path, and contact with the dead was left out as belief, not psychology.
- **Sensitive topics.** Abuse, sexuality, femicide and suicide come up in this domain. Handle them factually. Point anyone in distress to qualified professionals, and never present these models as therapy.
