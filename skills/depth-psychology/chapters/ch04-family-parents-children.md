# Chapter: Family, Parents, Children and Childhood

## Core Idea
In this model, childhood is the root of almost everything: the quality of adult love, the relationship to authority, self-esteem, depression, empathy, even how one treats animals. The starting point is radical. The (patriarchal) family is the base institution of power: it places defenceless children in the hands of adults who can treat them as property, trains them in **obedience**, and hands down violence. Children survive by seeing their parents as good and blaming themselves. As adults they either repeat the oppression or keep submitting without knowing why, and they transfer the idealised parent onto bosses, politicians and God. The answer is to **"side with the child"**: listen to children, explain rather than impose, allow dissent, apologise, never hit, and do the inner work of meeting one's own **inner child** and seeing one's parents as they really were. Forgiveness of parents is not owed. The parenting core (no physical punishment, explanation over command, real listening, warmth and containment) is close to mainstream developmental psychology. The model's sweeping claims about how many parents fail to love, and its symptom-to-abuse readings, are not.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The Family as the Base Institution of Power.** The family is described as patriarchal in origin and organised around obedience. Concrete signs that children are treated as property: the smallest bedroom, doors without locks, parents who do not knock, no autonomy. School, work and Church carry on the training ("keep them imprisoned at desks instead of playing and creating"). The claim: whoever believes in "good" parents believes in politicians, bosses and God, so you cannot see the power that rules the world until you look clearly at what your parents did. The alternative, called "matriarchal", is not women ruling men but children belonging to "all the mothers": with several adults caring, one healthy adult is enough to rebel against mistreatment. Kibbutzim and elephant herds that keep the young in the centre are the examples. The blunt rule: you are not obliged to love your children, but you are obliged not to have them if you do not love them.
When to use: to analyse authority dynamics (submission or rebellion) as learned patterns, without psychoanalysing real individuals.

**Siding with the Parents vs. Siding with the Child.** Life is framed as a choice. One side is the parents (power, the mind, the stronger party). The other is the child (instinct, life, the weaker party). Siding with the parents means siding with the strong, and the model links it to violence toward one's own children and indifference to war, exploitation and animals. The child who was not accepted feels unlovable and adopts one of four strategies: throwing themselves into the outside world (career, money), depression, distraction (games, friends, books), or turning inward and becoming reflective. With very negative parents, the child may deny their sensitive part and ally with the strong "hidden parent", which in this reading produces bullies and violent parents. Preferring a child out of affinity (understandable) is distinct from preferring a *sex* (siding with the stronger).
When to use: to explain why some people identify with an aggressor and despise fragility.

**The Inner Child (bambino interiore), three layers.** The model distinguishes (1) the **historical child**, ourselves as children, especially the part we do not remember, and (2) a deeper **primordial/"divine" child**, which in an esoteric layer returns instinct freed of conditioning (left out here as belief). The inner child is the part we had to repress, especially its emotions, and it keeps carrying pain and anger if not contacted. It is to be **welcomed, not made to grow up**: the adult watches over it while it plays, as in a small garden. The contact method: simple, no-pressure questions (age, name, clothes, favourite food), without asking it to remember and without dragging it into adult life. A caution: we do not open up in front of malevolent people, because the mind closes when there is danger. When we rage or cry helplessly, it is often the child taking over.
When to use: as a reflective metaphor for emotional reactions that feel "younger" than the situation.

**Symptoms as Signals.** Many childhood symptoms are read as signals that the child is afraid of something real, not "normal" or passing. The list: nightmares, bedwetting, stuttering, shyness, kleptomania, sleepwalking, fear of the dark and the "bogeyman", tinnitus, sugar cravings, frequent jolting awake with a falling sensation. The interpretive moves:
- a nightmare refers to a real fear in the child's present life;
- bedwetting is a regression to escape the present, and getting angry at the child for it is a failure to love;
- kleptomania and sugar craving are attempts to give oneself "something good" that nobody gave;
- shyness differs from introversion. Introversion is innate: preferring one's own company and talking willingly with people who share one's interests. Shyness comes from someone who frightened the child.

The image: when someone limps, do you get angry that they limp, or ask where it hurts? A healthy child introduces themselves, shakes hands and does not fear strangers, so a sudden change deserves attention and gentle indirect questions, even in the form of a fairy tale.
When to use: only for the *general principle* of asking "why?" about a behaviour change instead of punishing it. Never as a diagnostic key (see Caveats).

**Why it is hard to separate from parents.** The thesis: unconsciously we experience parents as the **guarantee that we will not die**. As long as they stay "alive" inside us we feel safe, even at forty. Seeing them as they were means losing that protection and facing mortality. After discovering mistreatment one can (a) deny it, (b) acknowledge it intellectually without letting emotions out, so nothing changes, or (c) let the emotions emerge, so the inner parent "dies". That inner death brings loneliness, sadness and a sense of mortality, and then the fall of the conditioned mind. The claim that a parent "recovered" in adulthood repairs the parents, not the child, is the bridge to the model's view on forgiveness.

**No Duty to Forgive Parents.** The model distinguishes *forgiving* (giving freely, a religious act) from *excusing*, and *behaviour* (which can be commanded) from *feeling* (which cannot). An error made in good faith, such as pushing a child into a school in the belief it was good, is excusable if affection was shown elsewhere. Domination without respect is not. Forgiveness requires an explicit request and real repentance from the parent. Kindness in old age often comes from needing the children rather than from a change of heart, and money left "to repair" does not cancel the harm. Forgiving someone who knowingly did harm betrays one's inner child and risks depression or discharging rage onto others. Pain and anger, once expressed, run out on their own and do not need to be "overcome". The model openly contests therapists who recommend forgiveness for serenity.
When to use: to give permission *not* to force a feeling. Present it as this model's position, not as advice.

**Good-Enough Parenting, this model's version.** The positive programme:
- **Love, respect, no force.** A good father is simply someone who loves and respects his children and uses no force on them or his wife. "The father's slap and the mother's caress" is rejected. Hitting is overpowering and cowardice, and people who hit were hit.
- **Democratic family.** Children may contradict. A loving parent asks "explain your reasons" and gives *advice, not orders*. The opposite failure is also criticised: the father who only asks "what would you do?" and never states an opinion.
- **"Parent as if you were the grandparents."** Explain dangers rather than impose rules and insults, so the child understands it is done for them.
- **Expect not to know your child.** The child may be gay, atheist, religious, childless, or in a job you never imagined. "You have your aunt's character" is a way of not looking at the real child. Character is held not to be inherited, though physical traits are.
- **Ask real questions.** "Did you have a good time?" or "how did you feel?" rather than the ritual "how was school?". Friendships, relationships and the ability to defend oneself matter more than grades. If a child does badly or seems listless, ask why they are unhappy, not why they are lazy. Comparisons with cousins make a child feel doubly wrong. "I don't know what I want to do" signals self-distrust induced by judgement. Parents should say "I need to think about it" rather than judge.
- **Apologise simply** ("I was nervous") and accept the child's criticism. Admitting errors protects children from feeling always wrong. Never answer "I'm right".
- **Be honest or postpone honestly** ("give me ten minutes"). Do not call a child stupid. Do not compare.
- **Authenticity.** Children sense anxiety and, even more, inauthenticity: rules like "others are watching you" repeated without heart breed hatred. Example: the parent who smokes and tells the child not to.
- **Encourage for worth, not performance.** Encourage a child because they deserve it, not because "they're good". No brainwashing about abilities.
- **"Sacred distance."** Respect the child's intimacy: physical distance (for example, no nudity in front of them) but not emotional distance. Tell the truth, about grandparents and the world too. On religion, baptism is the parents' choice and not neutral, and it would be more honest to explain other faiths as well; a child pushed into a ceremony they do not understand experiences it as imposed.
- **Containment for newborns.** Warmth, contact, a quiet voice, being dressed and held soon after birth. Harlow's monkey experiment is the reference: the soft cloth "mother" was preferred over the wire one with the bottle. The mother (or whoever fills that role) is the child's first world, and the second figure is the "first door" to the outside. The sex of the figures does not matter; how they treat the child does.
- **Correct for function, not "goodness".** Explain how things work instead of making the child feel morally bad.
- **Montessori-style autonomy** strengthens self-esteem: letting children do things themselves.

**Sibling Dynamics.** The quality of sibling relationships depends mostly on how the parents treated the children. Unloved siblings become jealous rivals. A parent who cannot love one child (sensitive, weak, gay, or a girl) cannot really love any child, but the children do not see this and believe they are rivals. Three mechanisms:
- **Displacement:** aggression that cannot go toward the parents, who must be kept sweet as protectors, is turned onto siblings. The same happens socially and at work, as when a promoted colleague is hated by peers rather than by the bosses.
- **Identification with the aggressor:** a child who resembles the overpowering parent sides with them and hates the fragile sibling, who stands for their own unloved weakness.
- **Self-love mirrors parental love:** we love in ourselves what our parents loved in us and hate what they hated.

Birth-order typologies are called "a lie". The only real difference is that ageing parents are often more permissive with the youngest. To reconnect as adults, tell a sibling "I was hurt", not "you were hurt".

**True Maternity.** Motherhood is not making a child but an attitude of care toward every weak creature: "either you are mother to all or to none." Maternal feeling shows as being unable to bear the suffering of an animal, a weak person or an old person, and as tenderness toward other people's children (a partner's children, for example). The idealised picture is punctured. Pregnancy is something all animals do, and society and the market glorify it. A child is a heavy burden that changes nights, home, freedom and timetables. Children owe their parents nothing because they did not ask to be born. Having children for status, for a purpose in life, for a "citadel" of security or for care in old age is selfishness, and for care in old age "there are carers". Raise children without great anxiety, because the anxiety belongs to the mother, not the child. Show them that life is worth living through simple, free activities.

**Teaching Needs Benevolence.** Any teaching (school, family, religious, spiritual) gets through only if it carries at least *benevolence* toward the learner, and students feel whether the teacher likes them. Teachers should first look at their own wounds. Many teachers fear pupils because they mistake them for their own parents. A teacher should accept being questioned (otherwise they are defending a "puppet" of themselves) and should stimulate critical thinking rather than hand down data or dogma. A child who questions a teacher or therapist should be heard and helped to weigh the matter, without being told the adult is always right or always wrong.

## Key Concepts
- **Obedience training:** the model's name for the purpose it attributes to Western education: obedience over wellbeing or understanding.
- **"Good" parent idealisation:** children must see parents as good to survive, so they blame themselves. This idealisation later transfers to authorities and God.
- **Repression of childhood harm:** the mind erases harm received from parents, which then resurfaces through inner work (dreams, memories) and is discharged onto others as projection. Draws on Freudian repression.
- **The "hidden parent" (genitore occulto):** the strong, winning internal figure a rejected child may ally with, giving up their own sensitivity.
- **The mask:** the identity a child builds to resemble what they think the parents want. In an "initiatory" sense, truthfulness means removing it to see oneself, then wearing it again *consciously* for others.
- **Truth to oneself:** the truth that matters is the truth told to oneself. Imposed sincerity is a patriarchal tool of obedience. Lying is a normal defence in children and, within limits, in adults. A warning sign would be excessive fantastical lying or strong narcissism.
- **Judging vs. having opinions:** *judging* disqualifies the whole person. Having an opinion about a behaviour, or noticing a trait common in a group, is not judging. Understanding is not justifying.
- **Imprinting:** borrowed from Konrad Lorenz to stress that the first years are decisive. Letting children criticise their parents, with the parents explaining their own mistakes, gives the children the ability to judge and not to submit.
- **Emotions as fuel:** too little emotional "fuel" brings depression. A teenager who stays in bed is, in this reading, depressed rather than lazy: a "lazy" child is a child without energy, because painful experiences were buried.
- **Soul at birth (anima as a seed):** in its psychological reading, the capacity to perceive oneself and the environment. It grows with a warm, containing environment, curiosity, identification and communication with different beings, and it shrinks with closure, depression and indifference to others' needs. (Afterlife claims are excluded.)
- **Fear vs. respect:** "my father only needed one look" indicates fear, not esteem. Hierarchy (father, then mother, then children) replaces *feeling*, and imposed obedience damages trust.
- **Respect for animals as a test of feeling:** people who do not understand animals do not understand their own children or other people. Contempt for the weak (the child scaring pigeons while the parent says nothing) is learned from having been treated as weak.
- **The tragedy of not noticing:** the tragedy is not being unloved but failing to realise it. Understanding that the parents could not love frees one from feeling unworthy.
- **Rebellious adolescence not inevitable:** the claim is that loved children do not need to rebel.
- **The single parent can suffice:** the effect depends on how loving the parents are, not on their sex or number.

## Mental Models
- **Ask where it hurts, not why it limps.** Treat a sudden behaviour change as information about what the person is going through, not as defiance to suppress.
- **Explain once, then stop.** A rule explained clearly once (twice at most) and then not debated keeps authority without needing approval. The "say it once" principle from the relationships chapter, applied to parenting.
- **Advice, not orders, but with a view.** Invite the other's reasoning, then state your opinion clearly.
- **Displaced hostility.** When peers fight, check whether the anger is really aimed at an authority it is unsafe to confront.
- **Love can't be extracted by guilt.** Duties imposed through guilt (calls, birthdays, "after all we did") damage the relationship. The future relationship depends on the parents, not the children.
- **Benevolence is the carrier wave.** Content without goodwill toward the receiver does not land.
- **Authenticity is detectable.** Children, and audiences, sense when a rule or message is repeated without conviction.
- **Don't assume you know.** The healthy stance toward a child, or any person, starts from "I don't know who you are yet".
- **First-person disclosure.** "I lived it this way" opens a conversation. "This happened to you" closes it.

## Applications
- **Understanding motivations.** The idealised-parent → authority transfer helps explain why people put their trust in strong brands, experts or leaders, and why both reflexive obedience and reflexive rebellion toward bosses or clients can be learned patterns. The line "the pleasure of money is thinking about what others think" points to status as a driver of decisions. Use these readings to understand patterns, never to psychoanalyse a specific person.
- **Writing characters.** Strong archetypes and arcs: the bully who was a beaten child; the sibling allied with the domineering parent against the fragile one; the colleague hated by peers after promotion (displacement); the child whose sudden shyness or nightmares signal a hidden fear; the adult who keeps an ageing, neglectful parent "alive" inside as a shield against mortality; the elderly parent who leaves money to repair and is not forgiven; the beloved teacher remembered as a lifeline by a child unloved at home; the mother who, after a son comes out, asks to meet his friends and drops the prejudice.
- **Communication and management.** Explain the *why* behind rules (to teams and clients alike). Apologise simply and without defensiveness. Use open questions ("how did it go for you?") instead of ritual ones in briefs, interviews and feedback. Separate opinion about a behaviour from judgement of the person. Treat dissent as a resource in creative teams. Use Montessori-style autonomy with margin for error when training juniors. Avoid public criticism of fragile people. State decision criteria clearly so resentment falls on the process, not the people. Do not demand "enthusiasm" as a duty: you can ask for behaviour, not feelings. Calm, clear, contained tone lowers anxiety in a stressed client, echoing the points on containment.
- **Coaching-style reflection.** Prompts drawn from this material, offered without interpretation: "Would I be willing to hear my own child's criticism?", "Am I asking for a behaviour or a feeling?", "Am I correcting for function or for goodness?", "Do I know what this person actually feels?". Support the general principle that one does not have to force forgiveness, without endorsing the claim that forgiving is harmful.
- **Marketing and audience insight.** Messages to parents that are realistic about fatigue and sacrifice may be more credible than idyllic motherhood imagery. Portray parental and gender roles without stereotypes (the "father's slap, mother's caress" cliché is outdated). Audiences judge a brand by how it treats the weaker party (small clients, suppliers, animals). Avoid showing animals dressed up or frightened for comic effect. Messages backed by real conviction outperform façade messages.

## Caveats
- **Status of the material.** This is popular depth psychology with an esoteric layer. It is not evidence and not clinical or parenting guidance.
- **Supported by mainstream research:** physical punishment harms children (and is linked to aggression, in part to bullying); early attachment and warm, contingent care matter (Harlow's experiment is real); listening, explaining rules and allowing autonomy are associated with better outcomes; parenting style influences sibling relationships; displacement of aggression is a recognised mechanism; refusing *forced* forgiveness appears in some clinical approaches. The inner child is a metaphor used in some therapies, not a unified scientific construct.
- **This model's own contested or unsupported claims:** that the family is "the greatest evil"; that most parents do not love their children; that "one in three" or "one in two" children are abused (statistics without sources); that adult anxiety, phobias, insomnia or panic prove the parents did harm ("if you have symptoms, your parents hurt you" is explicitly *not* a clinical rule); that nightmares, bedwetting, stuttering, sleepwalking, shyness, sugar cravings and the like signal abuse (clinical psychology recognises multiple causes, including development, anxiety, medical and genetic factors, and many of these symptoms are common and transient); that forgiving parents is always harmful (many therapists disagree); that character is not inherited; that laziness does not exist; that birth order means nothing; that rebellious adolescence is unnecessary; that all bullies were beaten; that kibbutz children were less neurotic; and that a historical peaceful matriarchy existed (Bachofen and Gimbutas are heavily disputed). Extending Lorenz's imprinting to human children goes beyond what ethology supports.
- **Gender generalisations:** "men as primates are made to defend territory, not raise children", "women have children to find a role", "mothers avoid investigating the father", "women more soul, men more mind". These are generalisations, and some contradict the model's own claim that the parent's sex does not matter. Do not reuse them as facts.
- **Child-safety boundary.** The symptom readings must never be used to make accusations or diagnoses about a real child. Concerns about a minor go to a paediatrician or qualified specialist, and safeguarding concerns go to the appropriate services. An agent should say so plainly if asked.
- **Credentials.** The model dismisses professional titles and registers as nearly irrelevant ("only the healer's inner path counts"). Do not adopt that stance: recommend qualified, registered professionals.
- **Esoteric content excluded.** Material on reincarnation, the soul's survival after death, the "negative/black astral" that "devours", the "divine child" contacting the invisible world, lunar magic, entities and alchemical "secret fire" was left out as belief, not psychology.
