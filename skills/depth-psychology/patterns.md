# Patterns

Concrete, repeatable lenses. Each entry: When to use / How / Trade-offs.

All lenses are hypotheses for empathy, communication, research and fiction. None is a diagnostic tool, and none should be used to tell a real person what their pain "comes from".

## What Is This Person Defending?
**When**: hostile feedback, a heated objection, a client or commenter who reacts far beyond the content (Ch1, Ch7).
**How**: before answering the content, ask what the reaction protects: their self-image, a role, a belonging, a belief. Answer that need first (acknowledge, reassure, separate the work from the person), then return to the merits. Do not chase someone who refuses to reason.
**Trade-offs**: the model's claim that rigidity always hides an unknown fear is its own generalisation. Used aloud ("you're just being defensive") it becomes an insult and escalates. Keep it as a silent question.

## "Can I Change My Mind?" Test
**When**: strategic or creative debates; self-checks on any strong conviction (Ch1, Ch8).
**How**: ask "could I change my mind about this if the evidence changed?". Whatever cannot be questioned is treated as a received schema, not a tested view. A companion test: "could I lose this belief without harm?" If not, it is a need to believe, not an opinion.
**Trade-offs**: good as self-reflection, poor as a weapon. Some convictions are rightly firm (ethical lines, verified facts). The model ties fear of change to clinging to idealised parents, a psychodynamic interpretation without strong evidence.

## Disproportion Signals History
**When**: a reaction is far larger than its trigger: panic over a bill, collapse after mild criticism, devastation after a modest break-up (Ch3, Ch5, Ch7).
**How**: estimate the part of the reaction that fits the present facts; treat the excess as possibly carried from elsewhere. Respond to the present part calmly and leave room for the rest. In self-reflection: "is this in proportion to what happened?"
**Trade-offs**: in this model the excess is always childhood material, which is a contested single-cause claim. Never tell someone their reaction "belongs to the past"; it invalidates real present harm.

## Staying on the Line
**When**: evaluating a controversial figure, competitor, testimonial or leader; an audience polarising around a person or brand (Ch7).
**How**: list merits and faults separately; mark what is known vs. assumed; back any "idealised" or "demonised" claim with data. Phrase conclusions as "it seems to me" rather than "it is so".
**Trade-offs**: balanced content can be rejected by people who need clean "good/bad" drawers; expect pushback on "both sides" messages. False balance is also a risk when the evidence really is one-sided.

## Evaluate Acts, Not Essences
**When**: any feedback, review, or criticism of work (Ch1, Ch4, Ch5, Ch7).
**How**: "this draft has these three problems" instead of "you're careless". Separate the opinion about a behaviour from a judgement of the person. Value the proposal even when rejecting it ("good idea, but…").
**Trade-offs**: low risk and well aligned with mainstream feedback practice. It can feel slow or over-careful with robust, experienced teams.

## Fear vs. Pleasure Test
**When**: a controlling, aggressive or domineering counterpart (Ch7).
**How**: ask whether the aggression looks defensive (threatened, insecure) or gratifying (enjoys dominating). Defensive: reassure, lower uncertainty, give clear information. Gratifying: set firm limits, protect others, escalate or report if needed.
**Trade-offs**: hard to tell from outside, and the cost of misreading a predatory person as "just scared" is high. When unsure, set limits first.

## Mind vs. Soul Speaking
**When**: public speaking, video, presentations, judging a spokesperson's authenticity, writing briefs (Ch2, Ch6).
**How**: "mind" markers: same cadence throughout, acronyms, big words that need interpreting. "Soul" markers: varied pace and tone, reflective pauses, natural movement, plain words. Start from the emotion you want to convey, then choose words. Leave room for questions.
**Trade-offs**: the vocal-variety advice matches mainstream speaking practice. The mind/soul split itself is this model's metaphor, and its masculine/feminine coding is a stereotype not to reuse.

## Inherited vs. Verified
**When**: audience research, briefs, team decisions resting on "we've always done it this way" (Ch2, Ch8).
**How**: sort assumptions into verifiable (data, tests, experience) and inherited (family, place, media, habit, "everyone does it"). Do not bundle: one opinion does not predict another, so ask open questions instead of inferring.
**Trade-offs**: inherited beliefs are not wrong by default; many are tested by others. Treat religious or political convictions with respect and never present the model's view of belief as a verdict on its truth.

## Symptom as Container
**When**: a person, user segment or character acts against their own interest: relapse, compulsive buying, staying in a harmful situation (Ch5, Ch7).
**How**: replace "why don't they just stop?" with "what is this doing for them?". Look for the relief, belonging or distraction it provides. In communication, avoid guilt and fear as the main lever, since in this model they raise the anguish the behaviour contains.
**Trade-offs**: the single-cause version (every habit hides childhood trauma) is contested; real causes are multifactorial, including biology and genetics. Never use it to dismiss medical or professional treatment.

## Approved-Escape Check
**When**: a hyperactive colleague, a client buried in new projects, a team adding meetings instead of resolving one concrete knot (Ch2).
**How**: ask whether the busyness is a pause or a way of life, and whether new ideas are dodging a concrete problem. Overwork and constant study can serve the same function as frowned-upon escapes.
**Trade-offs**: some people are simply busy or enjoy intensity. Framing someone's work ethic as "escape" is presumptuous; keep it a private hypothesis or a self-question.

## Partner-as-Parent Lens
**When**: writing relationship arcs; understanding why someone keeps choosing the same kind of partner, boss or client (Ch3).
**How**: in this model partners are chosen, idealised and feared as stand-ins for the parents ("saving the past": winning over a cold partner who resembles a withholding parent). Use it to give characters inner logic, or as an open self-question: "who does this person remind me of?"
**Trade-offs**: close to attachment and transference ideas, but stated far more deterministically than research supports. The model's gender generalisations and single-cause abuse claims must not be reused. Never tell a real person their partner "is" their parent.

## Exogenous vs. Endogenous Jealousy
**When**: separating a reaction to what someone actually did from a reaction to one's own history (Ch3).
**How**: ask whether there is concrete behaviour behind the jealousy (exogenous) or whether it appears even when the partner is impeccable (endogenous). Respond to the first with a conversation about behaviour; recognise the second as one's own insecurity.
**Trade-offs**: real betrayal can be hidden; calling a partner's suspicion "endogenous" can be gaslighting. Use it for self-reflection or fiction, not to judge someone else's suspicions.

## Reciprocity Check
**When**: judging whether a relationship, personal or professional, is mutual or has slid into subordination (Ch3).
**How**: compare the tone you give and receive: love with love, kindness with kindness, hostility with defence. Notice serving "in order to be loved" and compliance without the ability to object, which in this model produce withdrawal, not respect.
**Trade-offs**: the model's rejection of "turn the other cheek" is a polemical stance. Strict tit-for-tat can escalate conflicts that de-escalation would solve. Does not apply in the same way between parent and child.

## Mirror Statements
**When**: expressing hurt in a conflict, feedback or negotiation (Ch3, Ch4).
**How**: "what you said hit me because it reminded me of…" instead of "you hurt me". First-person disclosure ("I lived it this way") opens a conversation; "this happened to you" closes it.
**Trade-offs**: aligned with mainstream "I-statement" practice. Over-used, it can excuse the other person's real behaviour; name the behaviour too when it matters.

## Say It Once
**When**: stating a rule, boundary or decision to a team, client or child (Ch3, Ch4).
**How**: explain clearly once, twice at most, with the reason behind it, then stop. Insisting signals a need for approval and hands power to the other person. Advice, not orders, but with a stated view.
**Trade-offs**: some messages need repetition to be heard (large audiences, complex change). Use "once" for boundaries and decisions, not for onboarding or teaching.

## Adult to Adult
**When**: anxiety before bosses, powerful clients, famous people or large audiences (Ch5).
**How**: notice when the script is "child facing parent" (over-justifying, pleasing, freezing). Reframe the counterpart as one adult with their own reasons. For stage fright, focus on the content rather than fighting the fear.
**Trade-offs**: a reflective reframe, not treatment. Persistent social anxiety is well treated by evidence-based therapy (including CBT, which this model dismisses); point people there.

## Manipulation Red Flags
**When**: assessing a group, coach, leader, guru, programme or one's own persuasion (Ch2, Ch7, Ch8).
**How**: check the list in [cheatsheet.md](cheatsheet.md): telling people what to feel, blaming followers for failure, building dependence on one figure, detaching followers from emotion and calling it wisdom, devotees over competent people, "the secret" for devotion, guilt or renunciation appeals, over-insistence to convert. The core distinction: a conditioned person keeps a sense of right and wrong; a coercively persuaded person has lost it.
**Trade-offs**: aligned with mainstream work on coercive control and cults. A single flag is not proof; look for clusters. Real coercive control or abuse needs specialist or legal support, not a checklist.

## Loyalty That Survives Evidence
**When**: blind loyalty to a party, brand, leader or consultant despite repeated disappointment (Ch7, Ch8).
**How**: suspect an affective, parent-like bond rather than a rational assessment. Expect messages that attack the loved figure to trigger identity defences, not reasoning; approach indirectly and do not ask the audience to admit they were wrong.
**Trade-offs**: parent projection onto authority is a psychodynamic interpretation; mainstream research explains loyalty through identity, group belonging and motivated reasoning. Do not use it to belittle supporters.

## Watch the Strongest (Mental Contagion)
**When**: predicting how attitudes spread in a team, household, community or crowd (Ch2, Ch7).
**How**: in this model attitudes pass from the stronger to the weaker, not from the better to the worse, and grow with time together and number of people sharing them. Look at who has the most power; a leader's mood spreads downward. Highly emotional crowds are more suggestible, which puts an ethical duty on the communicator.
**Trade-offs**: consistent with research on conformity and social proof. "Strength" is vague; influence also flows through expertise, warmth and network position.

## Neurotic vs. Rational Sacrifice
**When**: unpaid overtime, unlimited availability, self-denial in teams; "success through suffering" messaging (Ch7, Ch8).
**How**: ask whether the sacrifice serves a real goal (rational) or is a barter with an authority ("I suffer, so they'll spare and appreciate me"). Do not normalise suffering as the price of success in messaging.
**Trade-offs**: the model's claim that suffering never fosters growth contradicts research on post-traumatic growth, and its tracing of sacrificial habits to a specific religion is polemical. Hard work toward a chosen goal is not neurotic.

## Just-World Promise Audit
**When**: reviewing campaigns, onboarding, or any message of the form "do X and Y will happen to you" (Ch8).
**How**: flag implicit reward contracts. In this model, when the promised reward does not arrive, people feel a deal was broken and turn angry. Replace guarantees with honest probabilities and what the person controls.
**Trade-offs**: aligned with research on just-world belief. Some motivation needs clear cause-and-effect framing; avoid only the guarantees you cannot keep.

## Defence Intensity Reads Fragility
**When**: anticipating resistance to a message touching faith, party, family or a strong identity (Ch8).
**How**: in this model the angrier the defence, the less settled the conviction. Plan for a defensive counter-attack when proposing ideas that dismantle convictions; check yourself when you over-defend a weak position.
**Trade-offs**: intensity can also mean deep, well-founded care. Never use "you're angry, so you don't really believe it" against anyone; it is condescending and unfalsifiable.

## Who Tells This Story, and Why Did They Change It?
**When**: analysing brand histories, cultural narratives, myths, or a message retold by intermediaries (Ch8).
**How**: identify the teller, their interest and what changed between versions. In this model old myths were rewritten when power changed hands (Medusa from goddess to monster, the Furies tamed into the Eumenides).
**Trade-offs**: the source-and-interest question is sound media literacy. The model's specific historical claims (a primordial matriarchy, a single Great Goddess) are not scholarly consensus; do not cite them as history.

## Myth as Story Template
**When**: storytelling, character arcs, rebrands, change narratives (Ch6, Ch7, Ch8).
**How**: pick a pattern from the myth table in [cheatsheet.md](cheatsheet.md): descent and return (Orpheus), the monster that was once a goddess (Medusa), the tamed power (Furies → Eumenides), weaving and unweaving (Penelope), cutting the knot (Alexander), the question not asked (Parsifal), the improbable hero (Frodo), the ring of power, symbolic death as transformation. Use the image, not a claim.
**Trade-offs**: the readings are interpretive. Archetypes are not universal; test images with the real audience. Avoid sacred figures where audiences may feel their faith is being used.

## Dream as Personal Imagery
**When**: a user wants to reflect on a dream, or a writer wants a dream sequence for a character (Ch6).
**How**: ask "what does this object mean *to you*? what was the atmosphere?" before any symbol table. Meaning depends on the dreamer: a sky full of birds means something else to someone with a bird phobia. For fiction, dreams can show inner change without stating it (lost bag, collapsing house, a parent's symbolic death).
**Trade-offs**: dream-symbol readings are subjective, not a validated method. Never read a dream as evidence of trauma or abuse, and never steer a user toward "recovering" memories; recovered memories are scientifically controversial. Persistent nightmares call for a medical evaluation.

## Wish-to-Please Bias
**When**: interviews, focus groups, user tests, workshops, anything with a visible "conductor" (Ch6).
**How**: in this model, under hypnosis, in groups and around authority, people produce what the conductor seems to want. Avoid leading questions, hide your preferred answer, watch for group amplification, and ask the quiet ones separately.
**Trade-offs**: consistent with research on demand characteristics and social desirability. Hiding your view entirely can make a session feel cold; signal openness instead.

## Helping-Relationship Checklist
**When**: consulting, coaching, account management, any relationship where one person helps another (Ch6).
**How**: empathic without colluding; clear boundaries without emotional distance; stay "in the shadow" (plain language, no jargon, no superiority); non-directive (do not decide for the other); confidential; let the client decide when it ends; welcome their aggressive criticism and thank them for the courage it took.
**Trade-offs**: matches common ethical practice, though drawn from personal experience rather than official guidelines. It describes a stance; it is not a licence to act as anyone's therapist.

## Identification Step
**When**: before judging a person or group whose behaviour you dislike; writing antagonists with depth (Ch3, Ch7, Ch8).
**How**: ask "what would I feel if I acted that way?". Everyone carries a spark of what moves the other. Keep identification separate from trust: trust is risk-weighted, and understanding is not justifying.
**Trade-offs**: strong for empathy and fiction. With manipulative or dangerous people, identification must not lower your guard.

## Ask Where It Hurts, Not Why It Limps
**When**: a sudden behaviour change in a child, colleague or team member: withdrawal, low initiative, apathy (Ch4, Ch5, Ch6).
**How**: treat the change as information about what the person is going through, not defiance to suppress. Ask open, gentle questions ("how did it go for you?") instead of ritual ones. Read persistent low energy as possible distress rather than laziness.
**Trade-offs**: the general principle is sound. The model's reading of specific child symptoms (nightmares, bedwetting, stuttering, shyness) as signs of a hidden real fear is contested; such symptoms have many causes. Never use this lens to accuse anyone; concerns about a minor go to a paediatrician or qualified specialist, and safeguarding concerns to the appropriate services.

## Explain the Why
**When**: setting rules for teams, clients or children; training juniors (Ch2, Ch4).
**How**: explain the reason behind a rule instead of imposing it; give advice rather than orders, but state your view; correct for function ("this breaks X") rather than goodness ("you're bad"); allow dissent and apologise simply when wrong. Give autonomy with margin for error.
**Trade-offs**: aligned with mainstream developmental and management research. Emergencies and safety rules sometimes need compliance first, explanation after.

## Ask for Behaviour, Not Feelings
**When**: demanding "enthusiasm", gratitude, forgiveness or love, from a team, a child, a partner or oneself (Ch3, Ch4).
**How**: in this model feelings cannot be commanded; only behaviour can. Ask for the concrete behaviour you need. In reflection: "am I asking for a behaviour or a feeling?" One need not force a feeling one does not have.
**Trade-offs**: the model goes further and calls imposed forgiveness harmful; many therapists disagree and some approaches use forgiveness work. Support "you don't have to force it" without endorsing "forgiving is harmful".

## Source Filter: Lived or Read?
**When**: evaluating a guide, guru, expert, testimonial or consultant (Ch2, Ch6, Ch7).
**How**: trust people who say how they know (read, heard, lived) and who invite you to check ("listen to me, don't believe me"). Distrust promises of extraordinary results with no checkable process, infallible verdicts, and anyone "brandishing" a value they have not lived. A guide can take others only as far as they have gone themselves.
**Trade-offs**: sound scepticism. Lived experience is not the only valid source; credentials and evidence matter too, and dismissing professional titles as irrelevant is itself a red flag.

## Workplace Respect Ladder
**When**: being interrupted, belittled or taken for granted at work (Ch7).
**How**: Level 1: when interrupted, stop, look, hold two seconds of silence, resume from the exact word, no apologetic fillers. Level 2: if it repeats, one short private question ("is it because you want me to change how I work, or does it just happen?"), then silence. Level 3: remove the extras (overtime, off-hours availability, favours), not the contractual work. Keep an alternative open. Rule: what you let pass becomes the new normal; add friction, not walls.
**Trade-offs**: personal opinion and experience, not research, and it assumes the worker has options. Harassment or legal risk needs specialist or legal support.
