# Chapter 09: Negotiation Conduct and Scope Control

## Core Idea
Negotiation outcomes are decided less by the content of the argument than by *how* the conversation runs: your emotional state, whether the other side feels heard, and whether the two of you are facing the same problem. The pricing-specific part is simple: decide your final number and your walk-away before you start, make at most one explicit concession, and defend the scope with documents (change orders, approvals, written assumptions) rather than with goodwill. For principled negotiation (interests over positions, BATNA) see `marketing-mba` ch07; this chapter covers what the pricing material adds.

## 1. Before the Conversation
- Decide: your anchor (ch04), your target, your floor (ch03), and the one concession you would make and under what condition.
- Know your three options: hire you, hire someone else, do nothing. The client choosing another provider is a legitimate outcome.
- Know your capacity and numbers. If demand for your full-price capacity regularly exceeds supply, stop selling the lowest tier (ch12).
- Know your walk-away: willingness to leave is what makes the number credible. Do not feel forced to close because a client called; you can refuse a bad deal if you have runway.
- Money talk is not hostile: have it early and often; stress comes from avoiding it.

## 2. Feeling, Thought, Reaction
Read your own "train of thought" from the engine, not the caboose:
1. **Gut feeling**: a base emotion (pre-verbal).
2. **Thought**: the concern attached to it.
3. **Reaction**: what you say or propose.
Use a constructive feeling word ("uncomfortable", "confused") instead of "angry". Example script: "I feel a bit confused. I cannot see how we deliver all these features for this budget. Help me understand." Withholding your view to avoid friction reads as evasion; be direct without offending. Tone changes how a message lands more than the words. Do not mirror a combative tone, which turns a budget talk into a fight. Practise with a listener who notes which words make them defensive.

## 3. Three Barriers to Conversation (identity, appreciation, affiliation)
1. **Identity**: know your core values so a challenge does not hook your pride. Price is a business number, not your worth.
2. **Appreciation**: listen consciously for the first minutes before arguing; find the value and logic behind the other view; say out loud that you can see where they are coming from. Do not talk in the first minutes of a conflict.
3. **Affiliation**: move from "me versus you" to a shared problem: "What is your advice on how we meet as many of our interests as possible?" Reframe the other party from adversary to partner.

## 4. Concession Rules
- Anchor first, high but credible. Never move to meet a lowball; ask the client to name another number.
- Concede **once**, **explicitly**, **conditionally**, with a "this time only". Two concessions teach the client to keep pushing.
- Concede in the currency that costs you least and the client values most: payment terms, timeline, a bonus, a leaner option; not your core rate.
- Do not negotiate down to your final price; decide it first. Setting the price above the target leaves room to compromise and signals value.
- Do not negotiate the price call by call. Settle the price, then hold it. Pushing for discounts as a habit is often a sign of a difficult client (ch12).
- Do not discuss money on later sales calls; hand invoicing to a finance step.
- Say "no" to a low offer briefly and with a smile; do not argue or explain. Giving a reason gives the client something to negotiate.

## 5. Scope Control as Negotiation
Scope creep is a series of small negotiations you did not know you were in. Control it in writing.
- **Define "done"** objectively (rounds, approvals, quantity of concepts).
- **Scope of work** states what is included and what triggers a change order. Assumptions and client obligations (timely feedback, decision-makers available) are listed.
- **Change handling.** When a change arrives, give a separate estimate instead of absorbing it. Agree a mechanism upfront (a stated contingency, a change-order form) so you do not ask for more money at each change.
- **Keep approvals documented**; revisiting approved items is the usual source of disputes.
- **Timeline clause**: a long client delay reopens the bid.
- **Price headroom.** Charging more at the start gives margin to absorb small changes without billing each one; small invoices irritate clients most. Bounded flexibility builds reputation; treating every deviation as a fee negotiation wears the relationship down.
- **Early warning.** Raise a growing overrun as soon as it appears, framed as a decision for the client (ch08).
- **Decline prescriptive instructions** that will break the outcome; offer alternatives with their costs. Move friction into the contract so it is not a negotiation during delivery.

## 6. Client Contribution to the Process
Clients can be trained. A structured onboarding between signing and the first working call (short training videos on how the work runs and what to prepare; a pre-work worksheet such as a buyer persona; automated logistics so human touchpoints are meaningful; modules for repeated questions; a pilot with a small group first) turns wasted kickoff time into speed. One example cut a three-week turnaround to three days once clients did pre-work. Do not map the whole system upfront; let each iteration reveal the next gap. See ch11.

## 7. Ambiguity, Norms and the Cost of Asking (research on career negotiations)
Mechanism, not a verdict on anyone: the same behaviour is judged and rewarded differently depending on how clear the standards are.
- **Negotiation is broader than the rate.** In a survey of several hundred senior women, the most commonly negotiated items were a new position or opportunity, authority and scope, and recognition (credit, title, being named), more often than a raise; in lab studies only a small minority of people ask for salary at all. For a freelancer the equivalent list is scope, credit and portfolio rights, deliverable names, decision-makers, payment terms, a reporting line.
- **Ambiguity lets bias fill the gap.** When it is unclear *how* to negotiate (norm ambiguity) or *what* is negotiable and what a fair number is (structural ambiguity), people lean on schemas, past experience and cues from a gendered environment. In one study of business-school job offers, the pay gap disappeared in industries where graduates knew the going rate (about 70% of the sample) and exceeded 10% in the ambiguous industries; giving everyone the same standards removed the effect.
  - **For you:** research the market, collect offers and quotes, and ask people who know the space before naming a figure.
  - **For your clients and team:** publish a rate card or standard packages (ch11) and state what is negotiable, so a hesitant buyer is not guessing.
- **Self-advocacy carries a social cost.** In evaluation experiments, a candidate who negotiated was liked less and was less wanted as a colleague; the penalty was small or absent for men and far larger for women, and evaluators of both genders showed it. When the same women negotiated for someone else, they did as well as anyone. The inhibition is about asking for oneself, especially for money, not about skill. Feeling uneasy is an accurate read of the situation; plan around it instead of reading it as weakness.
- **Relational accounts reduce the cost.** Explain why the request is legitimate in terms the other person will accept, and signal care for the relationship and shared goals. Scripts tested in experiments mattered for the penalised group and little for the other. Use it only if it is honest. Example: "You are hiring me to protect your budget and timeline, so you would want me to agree terms clearly. After this we are on the same side."
- **Open with evidence, not a demand ('non-offer offer').** Put data points on the table (market research, comparable fees, other offers) and let the other side respond before a figure is demanded.
- **Information moves through networks.** People inside a group get the inside story (how someone got a raise or an opportunity) by chance conversation; those outside it do not. Build contacts who can give information, advice, advocacy and a place to rehearse your arguments aloud before the real conversation.
- People who feel powerful are more likely to act and negotiate, so do not wait until you feel overlooked.

## Example Phrasings
- "That number does not work for this scope. What would you come back with, and what does it need to include?"
- "I can do it for X on these terms, this time only. Future work is at my normal rate."
- "Before we go further: the change you describe is outside the agreed scope. Here is a separate estimate; shall I proceed?"
- "We are already over by this much. Would you like to scale back or extend the budget?"

## Common Mistakes
- Accepting every change at the original fee, especially early in a career.
- Letting the client keep changing shots or colours after approval without a change order.
- Negotiating price call by call; negotiating without a decided floor.
- Withholding your view, then exploding later.
- Explaining the number so much that it becomes negotiable.
- Treating small deviations as petty and not mentioning them until the budget is blown.

## Checklist
- [ ] Anchor, target, floor and the one concession decided?
- [ ] Listened and said back their logic before arguing?
- [ ] Scope, rounds, assumptions and change-order rule in writing?
- [ ] Approvals documented?
- [ ] Early warnings raised as choices, not complaints?
