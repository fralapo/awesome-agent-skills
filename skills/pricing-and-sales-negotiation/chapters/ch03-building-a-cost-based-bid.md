# Chapter 03: Building a Cost-Based Bid (the Floor)

## Core Idea
Never submit the raw sum of your day rates. A bid must cover the true cost of production (everyone who touches the job, including management you do yourself), overhead, a contingency for underestimation, and a separately labelled profit. This is your **floor**, the number below which the job loses money; value (ch01) sets how far above the floor you can go. Show the client one number; keep the build-up internal.

## The Build-Up (recurs in several forms; follow this order)
1. **People.** For each role: rate x days. Include a producer or coordinator for the duration and art direction prorated across the days it is needed, even if you perform those roles yourself. Price as if you had to pay someone else to do the job, then add your own margin.
2. **Mark up labour for underestimation.** Specialists always underestimate their time; a padded rate or day count (a typical illustration: 5,000 becomes 7,500, a 50% markup) is a normal internal practice.
3. **Overhead and tools.** Workstation or equipment rental for the job, software, shipping, working meals, transfers, stock-search time (not just the licence).
4. **Subtotal.**
5. **Contingency** stated openly (about 15% over the bid is the figure given for undefined scope; see the next section).
6. **Profit as its own line**, as markup on the subtotal. Range cited repeatedly: 10-30% (about 20% used as the worked example); agencies historically used 25-35%, occasionally up to 40%. Profit is a normal line item; call it markup.

**Worked example (illustrative, generic currency):** artist 500/day x 10 days = 5,000, marked up to 750/day = 7,500; producer 400 x 10 = 4,000; art director 900 x 5 days prorated = 4,500; workstation 500/week x 2 = 1,000; subtotal about 17,000; 20% profit about 3,400; bid about 20,400. The same ten-day job quoted as 500 x 10 = 5,000 cannot pay a team, cannot absorb an overrun and earns no profit.

Related benchmarks (single-source heuristics, use as sanity checks only): blended rate for a freelancer in the mid-60s to mid-70s per hour, a small studio above that; client-facing rate about 2x the internal cost per role; selling at roughly twice delivery cost; day rate of a facilitator or specialist rising mainly with group size and specialism.

## Undefined Scope: Bid as a Range That Narrows
Scope almost never shrinks, so price rises across the timeline; plan for it instead of being surprised.
1. **Order-of-magnitude (ROM)**: a broad range to test whether the job is feasible at all.
2. **Educated estimate**: a range based on stated assumptions, narrowed by discovery.
3. **Fixed bid**: only once the plan is definitive and priced from known quantities.
- Estimates made without discovery are often off by more than 10%.
- Without a defined scope, estimate high, or start from a baseline and add explicit variations.
- Make the **uncertainty premium explicit** (state a contingency, agree it upfront) instead of hiding it or asking for more later. Clients often hold their own contingency; a visible one protects both sides.
- Separate discovery from build: a paid discovery phase produces the scope, the budget and the approvals (ch05, ch07).
- Do not quote low to win and recover through repeated overage requests.

## Scope Drivers (for explaining why two projects differ)
Five factors set a project price: **style** (look and approach), **size** (number and depth of use cases; the depth of a user journey matters more than feature count; about three use cases is a sweet spot, five to seven is much more complex), **features**, **finishes** (stock vs custom photography, illustration, video, copywriting; copy is often under-priced and delays launch) and **time** (fast, good, cheap: pick two).

## Surcharges and Adjusters
- **Rush**: only for a real external date (an event, a launch). Ask whether the deadline is tied to something real.
- **Difficulty**: for demanding clients with many stakeholders.
- **Client size**: more stakeholders means more iterations, so price larger clients higher.
- **Scope of engagement**: ask whether they want design only or design, build and launch; do not try to sell everything. Ask about in-house teams and work with them.
- **Value engineering**: ask the builder to list the cheapest and most expensive way to deliver each feature; trade finishes, keep function.

## Payment Terms and Cash Discipline
- Common schedule: 50% upfront, 25% at about three-quarters of the way, 25% on delivery, net 30 at most. A frequent simpler split is 50/50 (signing and completion). A stronger opening ask is 100% upfront; expect to settle at 50/50 (one school of thought).
- Split large fees into instalments matched to the buyer's cash flow.
- Take a deposit before work starts. Do not discuss money on later sales calls; hand invoicing to a finance step.
- Keep early overhead lean; keep a line of credit for late payers; do not spend the advance as if it were profit. Judge a deal by what you keep after all costs, not by gross value.
- Agree a partnership exit clause before problems appear.

## Example Phrasings
- "Based on the assumptions in the scope, the range is X to Y. A firm number needs a short discovery phase."
- "The fee includes a 15% contingency for changes we agree on together; anything beyond that goes through a change order."

## Common Mistakes
- Pricing from gut; quoting hourly rate x guessed hours with no buffer or margin.
- Leaving out management, overhead, equipment and software.
- Showing hours to the client (invites a cost audit).
- Assuming a big contract means big profit; spend discipline decides it.
- Letting a client's cost consultant decide what is "padding" without a scope.

## Checklist
- [ ] Every role included, including management I do myself?
- [ ] Markup for underestimation, overhead, tools and contingency present?
- [ ] Profit as a separate line (10-30%)?
- [ ] Output is one number, backed by an internal breakdown?
- [ ] Scope written, assumptions stated, payment schedule fixed?
