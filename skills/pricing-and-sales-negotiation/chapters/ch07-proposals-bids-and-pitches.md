# Chapter 07: Proposals, Bids, RFPs and Pitches

## Core Idea
Bid, proposal and pitch are different documents for different situations, and a proposal is the **receipt of a conversation that already happened**, not a substitute for it. Do not bid a project whose scope you do not know; sell a paid discovery phase first and quote the build from its findings. Keep proposals short, outcome-first and built from a template. Spend proof and effort in proportion to the odds and the value.

## The Three Documents
| Document | What it is | When it is worth it |
|---|---|---|
| **Bid** | Time-and-materials price for a defined scope; made before the scope is known it is a gamble | Scope is clear (ch03) |
| **Proposal** | Self-contained document (scope, approach, timeline, deliverables, credentials) for several stakeholders, each reading for something different (price, results, process) | After a real conversation and qualification |
| **Pitch** | Competitive creative work among several vendors on one brief, often unpaid | Only if the project value covers the cost of losing |
A "third bid" slot in a competitive process is usually low probability.

## Structuring the Proposal
**Short form (value-based work):** one page plus a short cover letter, two pages at most. Show the **outcome, the deliverables and the price**. No bios or award padding. Use neutral, objective wording; keep yourself out of the sentence. Written after verbal agreement on the number; do not send a deck after the client has already said yes.

**Long form (corporate / RFP):** about 90% reusable from a template: qualifications, team bios, testimonials, approach, process (for example five steps), relevant projects chosen against the competition, timeline, budget. Change the cover letter, budget and timeline per client. Mirror the client's language and format. Use a statement of work for the project and a master agreement for the relationship.

**What goes in the scope:** fixed quantities (three concepts, three applications), revision rounds, assumptions, client obligations, what is excluded, what triggers a change order, payment schedule (ch03). Messaging separated from design.

**Pricing in the document:** one total with the breakdown held back, or three options (ch04). Do not show hours (invites a cost audit). Do not present a menu of next steps without prices: use the prioritisation grid below.

## Prioritisation Grid (rank needs and attach budgets in the room)
1. Capture every need without filtering.
2. Score each on important (impact) and urgent (needed now); a desirability/feasibility matrix confuses clients.
3. Highlight high scorers.
4. Assign a budget to each item together, treating early figures as "play money" to test trade-offs.
5. Result: a ranked roadmap with a price per item. Advocate for the client's goals: if a cheap option will not deliver, say so and move money to where it matters; ask what they want to spend before offering a benchmark, then state the benchmark and why.

## The Paid Discovery Phase
- Treat discovery and strategy as non-negotiable steps even when the client wants to skip them; skipping costs more later.
- Fixed-fee discovery or strategy before a build (example fee 10-15k for web and branding; 5-10k for strategy alone, excluding deliverables; raise it in steps, 5k then 10k then 20k).
- Two-phase digital engagement: paid discovery, then statement of work and build; an optional "inception" with wireframes, sitemap and style explorations speeds approvals.
- If a rough number is requested: a range labelled as based on stated assumptions, and say a firm estimate needs discovery.
- Escalation of commitment: meeting, then discovery, then proposal. Send a follow-up email for questions missed in the call.
- Strategy sells in conversation without visuals: "thinking" is shown by listening and talking; "making" needs visuals. Do not use visuals as a crutch. Heuristic (one school of thought): when strategy exceeds about 40% of a total project, clients resist.

## Responding to an RFP
Find the price range before committing to a number: ask for a floor and a ceiling (bracket, ch04). Contents: key people, references and major past clients, testimonials, contacts, your process, case studies chosen against the competition. Competitive posture: against a **large firm**, stress direct access to the people who do the work and a personal number, and name (without attacking) the risk of handoffs to juniors; against a **small firm**, stress track record and reliability. If the budget is below your floor, offer to refer or reduce scope. Skip RFPs with no budget signal and no relationship.

## Pitching to Potential Clients
- Give context before showing anything: unframed ideas get rejected.
- Frame ideas as answers to the client's stated challenges ("you asked for X, here is how we solve it"); judge the work against the brief rather than seeking praise.
- Involve the client in shaping ideas; people do not attach to work they had no hand in. Share ideas to excite the client without giving free spec work.
- Show the strategy, which is invisible: brand-attribute exercises, style explorations, customer profiles (ideally a real customer), business goals that drive site map and features. Tell sensory, detailed stories and combine logic with emotion.
- **Pre-empt the doubt.** In a competitive pitch, identify what the client is thinking ("the big firm is the safe choice"), say it first so rivals have no ammunition, reframe with a strength (tailored attention, a named team, clear capacity commitment). Aim for one obvious choice, not a long list.
- Go in confident; clients hear desperation in voice and tone. For craft of delivery see `public-speaking-persuasion` ch07 and ch12, and `presentation-design` for decks.

## Show, Don't Tell: Free Taste Before the Fee
Let the prospect experience a short, free scoping session instead of a sales call (about an hour: set the scene, 10 min; value articulation with silent individual writing, 15 min; dot voting, about 7 min; the team builds the journey map offline and sends a PDF). Strategy is sold by making the process tangible and letting the client experience a small piece before paying for the whole.

## Outreach That Gets Replies
- **Four-part message**: Greeting (spell the name correctly, match formality), Anchor (one line connecting you: mutual contact, recent post, past event), Intent (the specific request early, with enough detail to avoid ten rounds of back-and-forth), Closing (plain sign-off letting the reader reply on their terms). Short. Offer an email reply as an option, since a call is a heavy ask.
- Cold-call pattern: introduce yourself and what you do, offer something useful for free with no commitment, accept whatever time they choose and let them bring colleagues.
- Aim for the first meeting, not the job. Research the principals, find common ground, lead with their problem rather than your capabilities, call as well as email. When asked for capabilities, ask for their ideas about the problem. If you have no clients, make a spec piece as proof.

## Example Phrasings
- "A firm number needs a short discovery phase; based on what you have told me the build is likely in this range, assuming X and Y."
- "You might be considering a larger firm for safety. Here is what you gain with a team that works directly with you, and here is the capacity I am committing to."

## Common Mistakes
- Bidding fast to seem responsive; a number with no dialogue behind it; days of unpaid proposal work for unqualified leads.
- Sending a big deck of case studies irrelevant to the client.
- Bios and awards padding; vague bios; no references.
- Presenting ideas without linking them to the brief; seeking validation during the pitch.
- Selling features instead of user stories; open-ended timelines.
- Trying to out-size a bigger firm instead of naming a different strength.

## Checklist
- [ ] Qualified and talked first; the document confirms what we agreed?
- [ ] Outcome, deliverables, price on the first page?
- [ ] Scope fixed in quantities with exclusions and change orders?
- [ ] Options or one number, no hours?
- [ ] Next step, validity date, and acceptance method clear?
