# Chapter 11: Packaging, Productised Offers and Retainers

## Core Idea
An offer is irresistible when it delivers the buyer's desired result, in their language, with their fears removed and the tension (price, risk, effort, time) minimised. Packaging is how you stop selling hours and deliverables: you sell a named, bounded outcome at one price, built from components that recur across your projects, in tiers. Good packaging also does pricing work: it prevents itemised haggling, makes comparison happen between your options, and replaces discounts with bonuses.

## 1. Offer Design Sequence
1. **Define the ideal client**: demographics, psychographics, wants, fears.
2. **State the dream outcome** in their words (from conversations and communities).
3. **List what the buyer fears**: wasting time or money, misfit, too many rounds, not knowing what they want, being sold a bill of goods.
4. **Give a concrete answer to each fear**: timeline, mood board or alignment session, an exit clause, fixed revision limits, a clear decision deadline.
5. **Write the offer in results language.** "Something you are proud to show" rather than "identity system"; "discovery call" rather than "kickoff"; "alignment" rather than "mood board". Replace checklists with a roadmap. Do not talk about your process in the offer; talk about what the buyer gets.
6. **Design the process backwards from the outcome.** Ask: "If you were only paid when the outcome was delivered, what would you need in place?"
7. **Package by outcome in tiers**, not by service.

## 2. The Tension Map
Reduce pain while increasing gains. Four tensions:
- **Price tension**: lower it to a no-brainer, or raise perceived value (raising perceived value is preferred; ch04).
- **Risk tension**: raise the perceived likelihood of success: track record, credentials, proof, guarantees, trial periods.
- **Effort and sacrifice**: reduce what the buyer must do or give up (pre-work done for them, one decision at a time, fewer options).
- **Time**: faster delivery of the same quality is a value lever; time to result and certainty of outcome drive price (ch10).
**Ten-times exercise**: list what you would do with 10x the budget; then design the cheaper, scalable version by constraint (a guide, template or productised module instead of custom labour).

## 3. Tiers and Limits
- Three tiers (premium, core, entry) so the client compares your options rather than outside quotes (ch04).
- Limit options to what the client can process: concepts capped at three ("one is too few, four is too many"); a decision required within one business day (one school of thought); two timelines (for example 14 versus 30 days) with a clear extra fee beyond two revision rounds.
- Higher prices reduce competition and attract higher-calibre clients; very low prices invite price shoppers. Price so you can afford better people, tools and time. Pricing a deep, high-touch service like a basic one is a mistake.
- **Replace discounts with bonuses**: low-cost extras (a half-day strategy session) rather than a lower fee.
- Do not put a nice wrapper on a weak product; the packaging must reflect real difference.

### Scarcity by Selection
Limited intake windows with a selection step, or a deliberately capped client list, can create urgency and keep growth manageable. Use it only if the limit is real: invented scarcity damages trust. A stable position helps too: build a situation in which you can say no without anything changing for you, and wait for the right proposal.

## 4. Guarantees and Risk Reversal
- Use a guarantee at the *end of a hesitant sale* to secure the decision, not as a lead hook.
- Tie it to a **measurable milestone within a set period**, written into the contract; promise only what you control.
- Design it so it cannot bankrupt you: limits and conditions. A generous guarantee with no limits is a recurring mistake.
- Alternatives to guarantees: exit clause, trial period (for example 30 days with reporting every two weeks), phased engagement, small upfront payment, instalments, a time-bound money-back clause.
- Address a client's bad past experience by asking what went wrong, listening, then designing a system that answers the fear (for example a 24-hour response promise and a direct phone line). Objections are opportunities to define a concrete guarantee.

## 5. Worked Package: A Multi-Week Sprint (illustrative structure)
Selling a one-week sprint to large clients leaves them discouraged; extend into a fixed-scope programme whose added blocks each solve a stated problem.
- **Prep week**: interview every participant, explain what to expect, pre-draft workshop material so the workshop starts from a filled wall.
- **Sprint week** condensed to about four days; experts attend only the first two.
- **Iteration sprint** on the open questions from the first.
- **Delivery**: prototypes, what worked and did not, answers to the sprint questions, recommended next steps in a shareable deck.
- Total client-expert time about three workshop days, not four weeks of attendance. Every added block has a stated reason. State exclusions (for example development) and offer optional follow-on work. Always end with next steps, or the project drifts. Book starts outside holiday periods. Team: lead/facilitator, prototyping designer, user researcher. Qualitative tests validate a direction; they do not produce statistically significant answers.
This is an example of the *shape* of a productised service; apply the same logic to a brand sprint, a deck system or a website launch.

## 6. Retainers and Minimum Engagements
The material supports only limited rules here; use them cautiously.
- A **minimum engagement** (for example three months) is the answer to "no contract": the work needs time to pay off.
- A **minimum level of engagement fee** per client (ch05) is the retainer's floor logic: a new client must be worth more than the least profitable client you would displace.
- For early-stage clients with potential, ask for a **percentage, royalty or retainer** instead of a discounted one-off fee (ch08), with a cap and clear exit.
- A **trial** (30 days, reporting every two weeks, ability to redirect) works as a reversible start to a longer arrangement.
- **Starter project before a retainer:** for a cold or high-ticket prospect who hesitates, propose a small, priced project first to prove value; the retainer follows the proof. This is a reversible start with a real fee, unlike free work (ch08).
- **Intensive, light, intensive (fractional model):** early-stage clients increasingly buy a designer full-time for about three months on an MVP, redesign or major feature, then scale back to roughly 15 to 20 hours a week or 20 to 40 hours a month for upkeep, then recall the designer for the next intensive piece. Quote each phase separately (the intensive as a fixed fee or output package, the light phase as a monthly hour allowance with a cap and an exit), and say that the light phase is a standing availability, not unlimited requests. These hour figures come from one market description; test them with the client.
- **Low-priced asynchronous retainer:** a few small monthly updates handled by short recorded explanations can sit comfortably beside bigger work and supports travel or side projects; keep the monthly allowance explicit so it does not grow unpriced.
- Reminders: ask for the next project before the current one ends; follow up after delivery with a note and ask for a testimonial.
- Still not covered by the source material: rollover rules and detailed hour-bank mechanics. Do not invent them; define them with the user and ask an accountant or lawyer about terms.

## 7. Onboarding as Part of the Product
Short training videos on how the work runs; a pre-work assignment before kickoff; automated logistics; modules for repeated questions; live coaching kept for individual situations; the share of live to recorded shifts toward recorded over time. Pilot the first version with a small group as a beta before building the full thing.

## 8. Moving from Deliverables to One Strategic Offer
- Reduce the menu to one offer with explicit reasons to buy it; a broad service menu dilutes the strategic message. Expect a messy transition of several months: old clients leave, some return later.
- Price it as a product: a flat fee, with no day rates, team CVs or headcount disclosed. The buyer buys the outcome and your quality control. If a client's procurement process requires a day-rate format, adapt the presentation of the same product instead of abandoning the model (the "no hours in the proposal" rule of ch07 fits when the buyer has no such constraint).
- Sell the scarce part: visual and UX execution are commodities; deciding which bet to make is the value. In sales calls translate a design request into the business problem (engagement, conversion) and its value.
- Say upfront what you do not do. Set a hard line on execution-only work: the premium price or go elsewhere.
- Target clients with budget; very early-stage clients rarely fit a premium process.

## Example Phrasings
- "Here is what you will have at the end: [result in their words]. Here is how we get there, and here is what happens if it does not fit: [exit clause]."
- "If you want more rounds or a faster timeline, here are the two options and their prices."
- "Instead of a lower fee, I can include a half-day strategy session."

## Common Mistakes
- Discounting to a level that makes the business unsustainable.
- Talking about your process rather than the result.
- Open-ended revisions; more options than the client can process.
- A guarantee with no conditions; guarantees used as a hook rather than a closer.
- Launching a full package or course before piloting.
- Multi-call kickoffs with no pre-work.

## Checklist
- [ ] Ideal client, dream outcome, fears written down?
- [ ] Each fear answered by a concrete mechanism?
- [ ] Three tiers, capped options, defined rounds and timelines?
- [ ] Bonus ready instead of a discount?
- [ ] Guarantee conditional, measurable and survivable?
- [ ] Onboarding and pre-work defined?
