# Chapter 12: Raising Rates, Client Mix and Red Flags

## Core Idea
If nearly every proposal is accepted, your price is too low. Rates should rise in regular steps, on new clients first, and the client mix should be actively managed: low-paid, misaligned or difficult work costs more than it earns, because it consumes the scarce capacity that full-price work needs. Underpricing is not a kindness; it produces resentment, over-delivery and bad service. A margin lets you absorb surprises and fix mistakes without resentment.

## 1. When and How to Raise Rates
**Signals**
- **Rule of three:** three yeses in a row means raise the price. Many refusals in a row at the new price: raise your *value* (skills, reputation, proof, packaging) before lowering the price. If everyone says no, the price is too high; if everyone says yes, it is too low. Settle the price over several calls, then hold it.
- Demand exceeds supply (you are booked and turning work away).
- You win jobs only by being cheap: that is a commodity signal (ch10).
- Flat revenue is a slow loss.

**Procedure**
1. Raise by a **meaningful step**: about 2x is acceptable (no need for 10x). Raise in steps, after each step has been paid and felt comfortable (example sequence: 5k, 10k, 20k for strategy). Raising more at once shortens the comfort cycle of raise-get comfortable-repeat.
2. **Raise on every new client**, in regular steps; it is hard to raise prices on existing clients. Apply new minimums to new clients first; expect pushback from existing ones.
3. Explain the price through the **problem the project solves**, not through your costs. Do not justify the number beyond that.
4. **Restrict supply**: be busy, offer rush fees, bring others in. Do not be instantly available: over-responsiveness signals low value and erodes budgets.
5. Uncouple peripheral services (framing, shipping, extras) and do not justify them.
6. **The price itself can be the credential.** You do not need awards or famous clients first; formal education correlates weakly with skill. The first time you charge a number is the hardest.
7. Track **average project value and proposal close rate**; without numbers you cannot see what works.
8. After a refusal, do not cut the price; build value.
- Set price by feel and adjust: raise when too many accept, lower when too few do (one school of thought; compare with the signals above).

## 2. Why a Higher Price Helps You and the Client
- Price carries information: confidence, demand, quality. Higher prices fund better people, tools and time.
- Charging more upfront gives margin to absorb small changes instead of billing each one.
- Higher profitability fixes mistakes early and builds loyalty; clients hire for good service and fire for bad service, and underpricing creates the resentment behind bad service.
- Fees are a share of value created.
- Becoming the person who merits the price matters more than the number itself. Do not feel guilty about earning more.

## 3. Low-Paying Work and Capacity
- **Capacity check**: track demand against capacity over time. If demand for full-price capacity regularly exceeds supply, stop selling the lowest tier.
- Low-paid jobs tie up staff so better work has to be turned down. Cash need is a valid reason only as a short, deliberate exception.
- If a competitor can deliver the same thing cheaper, let the price-only client go. Do not panic and discount when one client objects.
- A new client must be worth more than the least profitable client you would drop for them (ch05 MLE).
- Do not take work just because everyone else does. Do not work for free to build exposure without a price step.
- Sell your creative capacity and intellectual property, not just time.

## 4. Client Red Flags
| Flag | Why it matters | Move |
|---|---|---|
| Asks for price first, collects information for an RFP, no buying intent within 30-60 days | Time sink | Qualify out, or give a bracket and a date |
| Not the decider, literal questions | Proposal will be rewritten upstream | Ask for the decider; equip the champion with something to sell upward (ch07) |
| Treats design as a commodity and says price repeatedly | Will unbundle every line | Hold price; offer tiers; refer out |
| Wants all three of budget, timeline, scope | Impossible triangle | State that fit is weak (ch05) |
| Pushes for discounts as a habit; negotiates call by call | Often trouble later | Hold the price, decline politely |
| Wants a "hands-off" result with no involvement | Contradiction | Name the contradiction neutrally (ch05) |
| Refuses to discuss strategy or discovery fee | Fit test failed | Walk away; they want the old you (ch05) |
| Free work framed as a favour or "exposure" | Commercial client seeking free work | Value-equivalence or decline (ch08) |
| Unrealistic demand (guaranteed outcomes you do not control) | You carry all the risk | Restate, offer a milestone guarantee on what you control (ch11) |
| Poor fit by personality or values | Resentment risk | Decline even when money is tight |
| Asks you to prove you are bigger than you are | Pressure to pretend | Tell the truth about size and experience |

## 5. Triage, Referrals and Letting Go
- Divide prospects into **clients** and **charity** (ch08). Set a paid-to-unpaid ratio and stick to it.
- Refer out when the problem is outside scope or the budget is below your floor. A referral fee (10-20%) can monetise a lead below your minimum.
- Say no briefly and warmly, and offer to help assess alternatives.
- Expect refusals when you change your offer; a no from clients who want the old you confirms the change. Keep clients who accept the expanded definition of you and serve them excellently. Do not wait for old-service clients to follow the change.
- Say no to work that clashes with your values.
- Keep delivery strong: reputation is the next lead (ch10).

## 6. Keeping Good Clients
- Ask for the next project before the current one ends; clients forget quickly.
- Follow up after delivery: a personal note, a small gift within client gift limits, a request for a testimonial tied to a business result. Ask for references at the moment of client happiness.
- Have a documented process; do not freestyle delivery.
- Good, reliable service on small or difficult jobs earns trust that opens bigger, higher-level work and referrals.

## Example Phrasings
- "My rates for new projects have changed. For this scope the investment is X."
- "That is outside what I can do at that level. I can refer you to someone who may fit better."
- "I am booked until [month]; I can fit this in at a rush rate."

## Common Mistakes
- Believing you cannot charge more because you lack a famous school or clients.
- Lowering rates after a refusal instead of building value.
- Discounting repeat clients to win approval.
- Treating a short-term cash gap as a permanent pricing level.
- Under-valuing craft because the output looks easy.
- Keeping clients who buy only the old service.

## Checklist
- [ ] Three yeses in a row? Raise.
- [ ] New minimum applied to new clients first?
- [ ] Close rate and average project value tracked?
- [ ] Red flags screened at qualification (ch05)?
- [ ] A rule for when to refer, decline or accept low work?
