# Chapter 01: Value and Value-Based Pricing

## Core Idea
A price is not a function of your hours or your materials. It is a share of the value the outcome creates for a particular client, discounted for the client's risk and uncertainty. The same logo, site or deck can be worth very different amounts to a one-person shop and to a multi-site company, so the price follows the *client and the outcome*, never the deliverable. The cost-based bid (ch03) sets your floor; value sets the ceiling; the conversation decides where in between you land.

## How It Works
- **Value is client-specific.** Urgent, high-stakes and larger clients value the same work more than small or relaxed ones. Larger clients also carry more risk (errors hurt more, more stakeholders, more iterations), so they pay more. A job priced for a small business is under-priced for a corporation.
- **Clients buy the least risky option, not the cheapest or the most original.** Typical risk checks: case studies, referrals, track record, stability, team size, visible process. Technical skill is roughly equal across designers; what differs is credibility, visible strategy and presentation.
- **Price carries information.** A low price signals low quality; a confident price signals demand and competence. Buyers judge price in context, so you control the reference points they see (ch04).
- **Fees are a share of value created, not a transfer from the client.** Money is not zero-sum; the client keeps most of the gain.
- **Context decides "too expensive".** Absent context, nothing is too cheap or too expensive. Disproportion works in both directions: a large sum for a small goal, or a small sum for a large goal, are both mismatched.
- **A paying client must see value in the outcome.** If a client says the work has no value to them, the honest move is to not do the job, not to cut the fee.

## Procedure: Value-Based Quote in Seven Steps
1. **Find the outcome.** Ask which single result the client wants and why now. Use "why" or "what" questions until the root problem appears (ch05).
2. **Pick the problem with the biggest impact.** List the stated problems; ask which one, if solved, would matter most.
3. **Make it measurable.** Turn qualitative goals into proxies: current metric, target metric, volume, margin. Example chain: extra visitors per day x average sale x days = new revenue per month; a percent lift x revenue x margin = gross value.
4. **Annualise the gain** and put the client's own numbers on the table. Let the client name the value where possible; the figure they name tends to land at the top of the range.
5. **Set the fee as a fraction of that gain, discounted for uncertainty.** Fractions seen in the material: roughly 8-10% of the annual gain (framed as an easy yes); about 10% of gross value; about 20% when uncertainty is high (one school of thought: 20%). Treat these as starting ranges to test, not rules.
6. **Test buy-in before scoping.** "Would this solution at this price make you confident to go ahead?" Listen for hesitation; ask what gives pause and who else must approve.
7. **Only then scope the work, name one price, and take a deposit** (ch03 for terms). Name the price before building the bid; build the bid after agreement.

**Extending steps 3-4 with a value chain:** build the gain from the client's own metrics (for example impressions x conversion rate x revenue per sale), then ask what share of that gain they would invest. A small base fee plus an outcome-linked incentive (ch02) lets you charge more by sharing risk.

**Principles that keep the conversation honest**
- Price is a mutual agreement on the buyer's perceived value of solving the problem; neither your effort nor comparisons with other professions set it. Charge for the outcome and the problem, not for time or the artefact (a short consultation costs little, the surgery that follows costs much more).
- The same deliverable is worth different amounts depending on the business it serves; calibrate to the buyer's gain, not to your own discomfort, and avoid moral judgements about who "deserves" more.
- Four commitments to hold before any pricing conversation: you are good at the work (deliberate practice); you deliver more value than the price, since buyers purchase when perceived value exceeds price; you serve the client's need before your own income; you act with integrity even when it is uncomfortable.
- Diagnose before selling (the "hammer" problem: one solution offered for every need). Do not upsell a small problem; refer it. Sell only what you can deliver well; overreaching costs reputation, refunds and bad reviews.

Cap logic for cheap-to-measure problems: the client's cost of the problem sets the fee ceiling (a recruiting fee near 20% of a salary, the revenue a delayed launch loses per month, the cost of hiring and training an in-house team).

## Sanity Checks on Client Size (use as range checks only)
- A client's revenue is roughly 150-250k per employee (recurring figure ~200k); marketing spend is often around 10% of revenue; a project is priced as a slice of that budget.
- Price the person who has a stake in the outcome. If an agency or intermediary sits between you and that person, price your day rate instead (each intermediary typically marks up about 2x).
- Dropping the intermediary raises your margin but moves estimating, terms and risk onto you.

## Example Phrasings
- "Before we talk about fees: what does this need to do for the business, and how will you know it worked?"
- "If that moves from 40 to 60 enquiries a month, what is that worth over a year?"
- "A fee in the range of a tenth of that gain is typical for this kind of work. Does that feel proportionate?"
- "Which of these three problems would hurt most if we did nothing?"

## Common Mistakes
- Opening with scope and deliverables and only then discussing money; it invites unbundling.
- Starting from your own cost ("I need X per month") instead of the client's gain.
- Quoting a quick number before discovery (a "small" logo job closed on the spot that was worth many times more).
- Selling what you do ("an identity system") instead of what the client gains.
- Promising outcomes that depend on things you do not control (ch11 on guarantees).
- Pricing a corporate job like a small-business job, or a nitpicky client like an easy one (the fix for difficult clients is a higher price, not more patience).

## Checklist
- [ ] Do I know the single outcome and how it is measured?
- [ ] Do I have the client's numbers (current, target, volume, margin)?
- [ ] Is the fee a stated fraction of the gain, not a multiple of my hours?
- [ ] Have I adjusted for client size, risk and urgency?
- [ ] Did I test buy-in and ask who else decides?
- [ ] Is it one number, stated confidently, before scope is detailed?
