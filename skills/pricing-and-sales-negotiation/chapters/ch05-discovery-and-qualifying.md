# Chapter 05: Discovery and Qualifying the Client

## Core Idea
**Diagnose before you prescribe.** The most recurring principle in the material: a doctor, a mechanic or a lawyer asks many questions before naming a treatment and a price, and the person who asks the most questions about your situation is the one you trust most. A client's first request ("I need a new logo", "a redesign", "cheaper") is a self-prescribed solution or a symptom, not the brief. Discovery has two jobs: (1) **qualify** whether this is a client you should serve (budget, timeline, scope, fit) and (2) **measure** the problem so the price can follow from a quantified result (ch01). Money is a legitimate early topic; hidden money talk, not early money talk, spoils projects.

## Part A: Qualifying (Is This Worth My Time?)

### The Three-Variable Test
Budget, timeline, scope. If the client wants all three and you can only meet two, the fit is weak: say so and offer a referral. Sequence on a short qualifying call: **budget first, then timeline, then scope**; if budget or deadline cannot work, end politely. Extra filters before any proposal: do I like them; is there work I can do; can they afford it.

### Early Budget Conversation
- Raise money early and verbally. Learning the budget does not fit now is cheaper than discovering it after weeks of work.
- Ask for the budget **after** a little trust is built and goals are explored; the transactional opener ("what's your budget?") makes you an order-taker and pushes the buyer to the cheapest tier. Relationship-first: ask what success looks like, discuss the problem, then discuss money as part of fit. Both schools agree on early; they differ on the very first sentence. Use the transactional question only on a short screening call with a stated minimum.
- Use "money you have set aside" rather than "budget" (the word budget can sound cheap).
- Offer a range (magnitude-of-scale: ch04) so the client does not stall.
- If far apart, release gracefully: a leaner path, a self-service route or a referral. Act as matchmaker for the right provider.
- Ask what they spent previously and how it turned out, to calibrate expectations.
- **Budget bands for the buyer:** offer broad bands, then ask for the comfortable "sweet spot" and the "stretch limit". It is easier to answer than an open number.
- **Make honesty safe:** say you want the truth so you can find the right fit rather than waste their time; hold the money conversation on a live call, not by direct message or email, so you can read the reaction. When words and body language conflict, trust the body.
- **Qualify the decision-maker in the same call:** "Are you the final decision-maker?" If many people must approve a small budget, decline politely.
- **First call, order of magnitude:** discussing scope, budget and timeline on the first call avoids the long proposal that goes silent. A frame that avoids sounding mercenary: "out of respect for your time and mine, let's cover budget upfront", then a ballpark and a pause. Larger engagements take longer to close because buyers do due diligence, but budget fit can still be checked early; buyers already hold a number, and a price above it after weeks of talk stalls the decision.

### Minimum Level of Engagement (MLE)
1. Set the annual fee target. 2. Divide by the number of clients you can serve at once (the example uses 10; a range of 8-15). 3. The result (about a tenth of the target) is the minimum fee for a new client. Example: 200k target gives 20k; 400k gives 40k.
State it early, or point out the gap and then pause in silence. Frame it as a year of work, not one project ("our minimum is 20k in fees over twelve months"). If the client has a smaller one-off budget, ask whether more work could follow, moving the talk from project to relationship. Treat the minimum as a guideline you may remove: waive it only as a trial close ("if we agree today, I will waive the minimum; can you say yes?"). A "no" is the start of the next step, not the end. Apply it to new clients first; expect pushback from existing ones. A new client must be worth more than the least profitable client you would drop to make room. A strategy or discovery fee also acts as a qualifier: a client who haggles over a small design fee will not pay for discovery.

### Pre-Qualification Before the Call
- State on your site who you serve, what you do and typical cost. Use an intake form (about five questions) or a screener for the first call.
- Ask: deadline, who the decision-maker is, when the decision and award happen. If vague, let the lead wait ("call me in November").
- Do not write proposals for people who are not ready to buy. Disqualify politely when the project is too small or the client does not want a partner.
- Research the person's role and company before the meeting.
- Know your top three likely objections and have calm answers (ch08).

### Client Types and Buyer Types
| Type | Signal | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Fisher | Indecisive, collecting information (often for an RFP), asks price first | Qualify out if no buying intent within 30-60 days |
| Square Pegger | Not the final decider, literal questions | Find the decider; give close matches |
| Cost-conscious | Treats design as a commodity, often burned before | Show risk reduction; do not chase |
| Perfect client | Knows the process, has a business driver | Often pays 20-30% above other bids |
| Price buyer | Lowest price wins | Identify fast; refer out or take a referral fee (10-20%) |
| Indecisive buyer | Lacks context | Say you will probably be the highest, invite competing bids, give decision criteria |
| Know-it-all buyer | Plays art director | Agree and let them lead; watch what happens |
| Value buyer | Buys on emotion, justifies with logic | Do not list features; keep details light; do not talk past the sale |
The two lists overlap (Fisher/indecisive, Cost-conscious/price buyer); classify by behaviour, not by label.

## Part B: Measuring the Problem

### Discovery Frame (Now, Change, Pain, Obstacle, Future, Impact)
1. **Now**: where the client is and what they do.
2. **Change**: what happened recently (years ago, last year, this year, today).
3. **Pain**: how it feels, why urgent, why waiting is not an option.
4. **Obstacle**: what stands in the way and what they have tried.
5. **Desired future**: what they want and what it gives them; make it specific (a three-month outcome, a number, the feeling it brings).
6. **Impact**: how it is measured ("how will we know we got there?").
Dig with the **five whys** (3 to 5 times). Use a hypothetical ("if we removed that worry, would you...?") to lower commitment and build momentum. **No pain means no sale:** find a different problem or say you cannot help.

### Question Craft
- Short, easy questions that break a big problem into small parts; vague opens ("what do you think?") produce random answers.
- "What" and "how" questions are safer than "why" with sensitive prospects (a "why" can sound accusatory). "Why this, why now, why not wait, why me" make the client sell the project to themselves when the relationship can bear it.
- Surface contradictions neutrally: "I am having trouble reconciling these two things" ("cheap" and "high quality"), then let the client resolve it.
- Mirror the client's last words and language; play back what you heard ("what I hear is... is that right?").
- Stakeholders: ask who else is involved, what each would object to, who buys versus who uses, who signs. Pre-interview the main stakeholder to learn other executives' objections. Executives respond to business outcomes (revenue, engagement), not tools.
- Ask the client to rate their confidence or satisfaction on a 1-10 scale; if it is not a 9-10, revisit the outcome.
- Check the client's own numbers (qualified leads, close rate, delivery cost share) so the value is in their terms (ch01).
- See `public-speaking-persuasion` ch12 for the four levels of question (fact, opinion, impact, change).

### Learning From Buyers After the Sale
- Ask clients who bought: "What almost stopped you from buying?" The answer exposes the real obstacles in your process and wording.
- An open post-purchase question (what would you change if you ran this?) can feed offer and product ideas.
- A pitch should answer, in this order: who the offer is for (and who it is not for), why it is different, what benefits it gives, and why the buyer should give it attention. Being in love with the offer without being able to explain it is a common error.

### Paid Diagnosis ("Embrace and Pivot")
Charge for diagnosis, as other professions do. Script: thank the client and acknowledge the request; say you have an approach that helps clients grow and ask if they are curious; ask scaffolding questions (customers, pain points, why they buy from competitors; 20-30 minutes); make the first session free and say so; make the next engagement paid. Strategy can be a value-add for 2-3 iterations and then a separate line item. Do not give away discovery without a case-study or testimonial agreement. If the budget is far below the scope, propose a discovery phase with a smaller fee to define scope and budget together (ch07).

If the problem is outside your scope, refer out. Name the real problem type when evidence supports it ("this sounds like a marketing problem, not a website problem").

## Example Phrasings
- "Before I quote anything, I want to understand what this has to achieve. What happens if it does not get done?"
- "Of the problems you listed, which would have the biggest impact if solved?"
- "Is there anything else we should know, or any question I should be asking?"
- "This may not be the right fit: the timeline and budget may not match. Let me tell you who might be."

## Common Mistakes
- Quoting a website or logo price before any diagnosis; treating the brief as the problem.
- Pitching capabilities, or sending a deck of irrelevant case studies, before listening.
- Asking for budget before trust, or accepting "we have something in mind" and building a large bid.
- Writing long proposals for unqualified leads.
- Using jargon instead of plain language.

## Checklist
- [ ] Budget, timeline, scope tested in that order?
- [ ] Decision-maker and approval path known?
- [ ] Problem quantified in the client's numbers?
- [ ] Self-prescribed solution questioned?
- [ ] Next step agreed, with a date?
