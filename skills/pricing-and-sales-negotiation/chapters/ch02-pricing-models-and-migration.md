# Chapter 02: Pricing Models and the Path Away from Hours

## Core Idea
There are three altitudes of pricing: **input** (your time), **output** (itemised deliverables) and **outcome** (the value created). Each step up takes on more risk and gives more leverage. Time-based billing punishes efficiency, invites micromanagement and line-by-line haggling, and turns every minute into a negotiation. Most of the material converges on one direction: move from hours to day rate to packages to outcome-based fees, as fast as your estimating skill and client mix allow. Hourly billing is the right tool in only a few situations.

## The Models Compared

| Model | Use when | Main risk | Guard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly | Duration is genuinely unknown; temporary or support work | Rewards slowness, clients cut hours, unbilled management and research | Cap it; use only as a fallback |
| Day rate | Booked, defined days; temporary work for a studio | Unpaid overtime; short slots fragment the day | Never sell less than one day; ask what peers are paid and step up with each new engagement |
| Project / output fee | Deliverables are definable | Unlimited revisions, scope creep | Fixed quantity of concepts and rounds, change orders (ch09) |
| Package by outcome | You have repeating components | Pricing the wrong bundle | Build from components that recur across projects |
| Outcome / value-based | You can quantify the result and talk to a stakeholder | Uncertainty, longer sales cycle | ch01 procedure, deposit, defined milestones |
| Base fee plus performance incentive | The client can measure an outcome you influence | Outcome depends on things you do not control; payment lag | Small base covers cost; cap the incentive; define metric and period in writing |
| Percentage / royalty / profit participation | Early-stage client with upside, or licensing | Open-ended risk, unpaid until success | Advanced; keep to one or two partners, cap it, never the core model |
| Time and materials with cap | Scope genuinely open | Client sees every hour | Cap and report regularly |

Seniority as clients see it (useful for day-rate conversations): junior = knows the tools and has a consistent portfolio; mid = fast, hits deadlines, needs light briefing; senior = solves problems and troubleshoots. Clients rank **reliability first (a yes means delivered), then talent, then personality; rate comes last**. Generalists compete with a global pool and are pushed down; specialists with few local competitors command more (ch10).

## Why Hours Fail
- A faster delivery earns less for the same value.
- Clients negotiate both the rate and the hours (two levers to pull).
- Project management, research and thinking go unbilled.
- Itemised estimates invite a cut on each line; a single number hides complexity and reduces nitpicking.
- Hourly work encourages dragging projects out to look busy, which damages the client relationship.

## Procedure: Migration Path
1. **Hourly to day rate.** Sell whole days. When starting with a new studio or client, raise the rate by a step each time.
2. **Day rate to output fee.** Estimate hours per deliverable, add padding, set a fixed number of revision rounds (a typical cap is three; revisions are tweaks, not restarts) and a timeline clause (the bid reopens if the client delays for long). A loose range for output pricing is about 1x to 2x the estimated time cost depending on client appetite (one school of thought).
3. **Output to packages named by outcome** (for example a "sprint" or "launch" package at one price). Build packages from components that repeat across your projects; see ch11.
4. **Extras as one lump sum**, not as new line items.
5. **Outcome / value-based** for clients where a stakeholder owns a measurable result (ch01).
6. **Mix two or three models** instead of relying on one.
7. **Let clients self-select out.** Announce the change at the next booking and allow a few months for old-model clients to leave.

## Fixed Fee: Conditions That Make It Work
- A fixed fee gives certainty to the client and profit upside to you: the margin is the gap between price and cost, and it grows only if the project is managed well.
- Define "done" with objective criteria (number of rounds, approval steps). Subjective "done" breeds endless revisions.
- Write which requests are included and which trigger a change order; keep approvals documented (revisiting approved items is the usual source of disputes).
- Business clients usually accept scope, timeline and change orders more readily; creative clients often treat change as "just part of the process". Adjust tone and documentation accordingly.
- Hybrid for undefined work: fix the price of defined phases (strategy, logo, messaging, applications) and leave undefined parts variable.
- Separate messaging from design so revisions on one do not expand the other.
- Early in a career, absorbing every change at the original fee is common; leave that phase quickly.

## Example Phrasings
- "I sell by project, not by the hour, so the number stays the same whether it takes me two days or six."
- "The fee covers three concepts and two revision rounds. A further round, or a change of direction after approval, is quoted separately."
- "If it is quicker for me, that is good news for you, not a reason for a lower fee."

## Common Mistakes
- Staying hourly after going independent.
- Sending a multi-line estimate that invites a cut on each line.
- Removing scope to meet a lower number (offer a discount or options instead; see ch08).
- Relying on a single model.
- Fixed-fee bids with no definition of done, no change-order rule, no documented approvals.
- Treating profit participation as a core model rather than an advanced add-on.

## Checklist
- [ ] Is the model matched to how predictable the work is?
- [ ] Do I sell whole days at minimum?
- [ ] Are deliverables counted and rounds capped?
- [ ] Does the client see one number, with the breakdown kept internal?
- [ ] Is there a change-order mechanism written into the scope?
