# Chapter 08: Objections, Discounts and Budget Gaps

## Core Idea
When a client says "too expensive", do not justify, do not discount, do not debate. Treat price as a business number, not a verdict on your worth. Justifying concedes the higher ground and invites line-by-line pushback; discounting teaches the client the price is negotiable and attracts bargain hunters. Instead: confirm mutual interest, move the conversation from the fee to the problem and its cost, turn the objection into a scoping conversation, and make at most one explicit, conditional concession. Underlying logic is the same as `public-speaking-persuasion` ch10 (acknowledge, then ask an open question); this chapter adds the pricing-specific moves.

## The Master Sequence for a Price Objection
1. **Breathe; separate emotion.** Price is a number, not your worth. Name your feeling, then the thought, then choose the reaction (see ch09) instead of opening with a comeback such as "you get what you pay for".
2. **Confirm mutual interest.** "It sounds like we both want to work together."
3. **Ask, do not defend.** "Compared to what?" "What is the result worth to you?" "Where does this spend rank against your other priorities?"
4. **Cost of the problem.** Quantify what the unsolved problem costs over time (lost sales, delay, opportunity cost: new business per month x months of delay). Ask how much of that cost they would spend to insure against it. A fee well below their own estimate usually looks small.
5. **Reframe in scope terms.** "I cannot do it at that price. How can we design it so you get what you must have?" Sort must-haves from nice-to-haves, offer options, ask which one or two matter most, then give your recommendation on the best trade-off.
6. **Concede once, conditionally, if at all** (below).
7. **Accept a no gracefully** and offer to help them assess other providers. "No" is information: ask what sits behind it and who referred them.

Symmetry-of-logic test: if the question would be rude in a shop or on a date ("can you do it for half?"), it is not one you have to answer on its own terms.

## Embrace and Pivot (and Raising It First)
- **Embrace**: accept the objection ("you are right, we are expensive / smaller / newer"). **Let the client dig in**: their defence exposes the real criterion. **Pivot** to the value you add and what makes you different: "I help [who] do [what] by [how]; if you do not need that, hire someone else."
- **Raise the objection first.** Open with the three you hear most ("I will probably be more expensive, I may not have your exact experience, and I cannot guarantee results"), then move to the real need. Prepare a calm, specific answer for each.
- **Conditional question**: "If this is important to you, then you probably should not hire us." It makes the client test whether the criterion truly matters.
- People want to stay consistent with what they have said; do not force a public stance.
- Never argue on the client's terms (experience, size against a bigger firm); compete on a specific capability.
- Experts are the least risky choice for a buyer; claim the category.

## Scripted Responses to Frequent Objections
| Objection | Move |
|---|---|
| "I can hire in-house" | You are paying for their problem to be diagnosed, not for a task; compare with hiring and training cost |
| "Why pay for discovery?" | Compare with paying a professional for an assessment before treatment |
| "Why so expensive?" | Ask what the result is worth and what the big problem is |
| "No contract" | A minimum engagement (for example three months) is needed for the work to pay off |
| "Send me the packet" | First confirm agreement on price, scope and deliverables |
| "I just need a logo" | Ask whether they could judge its fit by looking at it alone; reframe as problem type |
| "Same price, more" | Do not add scope silently; ask what exactly they want more of |
| "Can I mix and match specialists?" | You coordinate specialists, as an architect does; you buy the blueprint and the coordination |
| "Drop a step (e.g., testing) / give a discount" | Price is fixed because quality is fixed; a discount would disadvantage other clients |
| "Not enough experience" | Admit it, then pivot to focus, access and process |
| "No guarantees" | Offer a defined milestone guarantee only for what you control (ch11) |
Answer with a question, then confirm the agreement in words. Do not over-explain.

## Low Budget Against Real Value
- Run an effort-versus-result test: (1) single business result; (2) estimate its value (extra visitors x average sale x days); (3) compare with the fee; (4) ask where the spend ranks against their other purchases, to uncover real priorities. Low budget is a priority signal to explore, not a reason to discount. Low-ball inquiries often signal a positioning or marketing problem, not a pricing one.
- Do not accept a fee below the minimum the work needs; if the client refuses, say you are not the right person.
- **Scope versus price: teachers disagree.** One camp: change the scope, not the price, because everything is negotiable and a shorter deliverable at their budget (for example a 30-second video instead of a longer one) keeps your rate intact. The other camp: never cut scope to hit a lower number, because a smaller price for less scope signals a weaker product, the client sees "less for less", and quality suffers; offer a discount or a bonus instead, or hold the price. Reconcile them this way: (a) a *pre-designed* leaner option, offered as a clear choice between two options, is fine, because the price-to-value ratio stays visible; (b) cutting scope and price on the fly, or silently removing scope, signals that the price was arbitrary and is not fine; (c) never remove the parts that make the outcome work (for example testing or strategy) to meet a number. Prepare the leaner option before the call.
- **Do not take all the risk.** A no-fee or percentage-of-revenue deal shifts risk to you. Present fixed fee and revenue share as two alternatives; take a revenue share only with a cap and a financial runway.
- Refer rather than send the client to a bargain marketplace; a referral fee (10-20%) can monetise a lead below your minimum.
- If the budget is far below the implied scope (for example an identity that implies packaging, photography and rollout), realign the scope or refer.

## Discounts: Replace with Bonuses
Discount rules that recur: do not discount to match budget; do not discount repeat clients to win approval; do not concede twice. If you must give something, give a low-cost **bonus** (a half-day strategy session) or a one-time concession tied to something specific.
Concession script: state your range; surface the hidden cost of the cheap option (missed deadlines, management time); position reliability and follow-through; ask for their maximum; concede once to a number between their maximum and your range, explicitly: "this time only; future work is at my normal rate." Decide your final number first; do not negotiate down to it.

## Who Cannot Pay (Non-Profit, Early-Stage, "Exposure")
- Triage: **clients** versus **charity**. Charity has a clear cause and limited means; a commercial business that wants free work is not charity.
- Value-equivalence exercise: name your normal price first, list the non-cash items offered (merchandise, tickets, referrals, lessons, posts), assign each a value, and accept only at a fair equivalent, never at zero.
- Exposure is not currency unless it is measurable (followers, reach, conversions) and written into the contract.
- Ask for a percentage, royalty or retainer from early-stage clients with potential.
- Set a ratio of paid to unpaid work and an explicit audit of who qualifies.

## Budget Gaps During a Project
Raise issues while small: notice the inner signal "I should say something", imagine the best outcome of speaking, and frame it as an early warning with a decision for the client: "We are already over by this much; are you fine with that, or should we scale back?"

## Common Mistakes
- Lowering the price under pressure; apologising for the price; conceding twice.
- Defending with a list of deliverables instead of the value of solving the problem.
- Taking a harsh no as final without asking follow-up questions.
- Arguing aesthetics; business buyers judge outcomes.
- Accepting a percentage deal with no cap; accepting unpaid work framed as a favour.
- Matching the client's combative tone.

## Checklist
- [ ] Did I ask what the result is worth before touching the price?
- [ ] Is my final number fixed before negotiating?
- [ ] Any concession single, explicit and conditional?
- [ ] Scope options ready as an alternative to a lower price?
- [ ] Referral or graceful exit prepared?
