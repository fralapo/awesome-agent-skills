# Chapter 10: Positioning, Niche and Getting Found

## Core Idea
You are interchangeable until your position is narrow enough to have few competitors and broad enough to have prospects. If you win only on price you are a commodity; the test of real positioning is winning *while charging a premium*. Position on a **point of view and an outcome, not a craft**: if you are "the maker", price is the only differentiator. Visibility to the audience that can pay, a narrow lane, and proof turn price shoppers into value buyers. This chapter covers positioning and awareness; for brand-level strategy see `marketing-mba` ch02, and for personal-brand growth see `social-algorithm`.

## 1. Tests for a Position
- **Room test**: imagine everyone who claims your positioning stands up. If almost everyone does, it fails. Aim for roughly 10 up to about 200 people claiming the same thing (one school of thought).
- **Distance test**: most clients should be far away and only a handful within about an hour. Clients willing to travel signal that your expertise is hard to replace.
- **Win-both test**: you need both a sales advantage and a price premium. Winning only on price, or only on awareness, is not differentiation.
- **Narrowing direction**: vertical (one industry) or horizontal (one audience or demographic). Specialise on *what you do and for whom*; "branding" alone is not a position. Too narrow runs out of demand; too broad stays interchangeable.
- Do not add a trendy label that many others also use. Do not stay in a local radius out of habit.

## 2. Why Specialists Earn More
- Clients hire the expert who has seen the problem many times over the generalist; generalists compete with a global pool; specialists with few local rivals set their price.
- Creatives feel a pull toward novelty that dilutes expertise; pick a lane in what the market sees and stay broad in private interests.
- **Halo effect:** visible competence in one area is assumed to carry over; use it to open adjacent work.
- Portfolio: about three pieces aligned with the target work; judge yourself by the weakest piece and remove it; avoid long mixed-discipline portfolios.
- Label portfolio items in plain words people search ("logo", "business card") rather than jargon ("identity system").
- Awareness comes before the sale for an unrecognised niche: educate the market first (see `freelance-designer-ops` ch01).

## 3. Escaping the Commodity
- **Two drivers of price:** *time to result* (faster outcome commands more) and *certainty of outcome* (guaranteed results command more). Ask what convenience, speed or assurance you can add to the same base work. A packaging ladder illustrates it: raw component, then prepared pack, then complete kit, then branded version, each step at a higher price for the same base input.
- **Do not match a competitor's price.** When you drop, they drop; the spiral ends with you paying the client to work. Quality differences that justify a premium are usually modest (about 10%, not 30%); the premium comes mostly from positioning and risk reduction.
- **No true commodity:** find the use case or angle that makes yours different. Do not explain why you are "not a commodity"; package the difference (ch11).
- **Buyer-seen credibility** (five items, in order of what buyers can see): strategy behind the work (invisible, so show it), fit with the target market, reputation (case studies, named clients, testimonials tied to outcomes, press), history (years; borrowable by partnering with experienced people), presentation (website, documented process, copy). A weak site filters out the clients you want and attracts low budgets. Testimonials that say "great work" with no business result add little.
- **Familiar is safe**: buyers pick the least risky option, not the most unique. Be reliably good, not original for its own sake.

## 4. Creating Uncontested Space (Blue Ocean thinking)
Rather than competing in a saturated field, serve non-customers and cut cost while adding value.
- **Four actions (ERRC):** eliminate, reduce, raise, create. Which factors does the industry take for granted that you can eliminate; which to reduce below the standard; which to raise above; which to create.
- **Non-customer analysis:** ask people who avoid the category why; build around their frustrations. Borrow practices from adjacent markets.
- **Category creation:** pick a common offer, list its problems, ask "how might we solve this for a different buyer?" Start with passionate, underserved niches.
- Numbers cited (single source): in one study 16 of 108 firms were blue-ocean and produced 61% of profit. Treat as illustration.
- Use it to design an offer, not as a slogan. Communicate real scarcity (finite capacity, a waitlist), never manufactured urgency. People buy identity and feeling (belonging, accomplishment) as much as the product.

## 5. Getting Found: Leads and Awareness
- **Chain:** lead generation, closing, delivery, follow-up. Delivery is part of sales: reputation drives the next lead.
- **Funnel:** awareness, consideration and validation, a barrier/doubt stage, conversion by micro-commitments (newsletter, call, signup), delight, sharing. Put most effort at the top; word of mouth is powerful but passive and unpredictable.
- **Inform or inspire:** publish knowledge, process and experience that match clients' current problems; find where clients gather information. Social media as advertising reads as spam; no offers before value is given. Being good is no longer enough; the work must be visible.
- **Clients hire people they know, like and trust.** If one of the three is missing (excellent but unlikeable), the hire does not happen. Trust comes from past work or a reliable recommendation.
- **Referrals:** ask existing clients with a specific, low-pressure request; ask at the moment of client happiness; offer referrals to others when a lead does not fit. Build relationships without a transactional aim; visibility creates introductions.
- Speak at conferences, join local boards, publish regularly to stay top of mind. Do not blame the market: clients who value design exist in every market.
- A lead that does not convert is not a loss: you lose only what you had. Keep a consistent professional voice on the channel that matters.
- **Profile writing (attention, interest, desire, action):** a clear headline, supporting detail, evidence that maps to the target need, and an invitation. See `marketing-mba` ch01 for AIDA. The same funnel fits any process where the other side evaluates you (a job search included): at each touchpoint ask what captures attention, what sustains interest, what builds desire, and whether anything makes action hard. Remove friction before the last step (for example a portfolio whose password the evaluator does not have), and think about the evaluator's experience, not only your own.

## Example Phrasings
- "I help [specific audience] with [specific problem] by [how]. If that is not your situation, I am probably not the right fit."
- "I take on six or seven clients at a time. I have an opening in June."

## Common Mistakes
- A trendy label shared by hundreds of others; staying local out of habit.
- Presenting every interest publicly; long mixed portfolios.
- Competing on features and price in a saturated market; matching rivals' prices.
- Posting personal content on professional channels; vague captions nobody searches.
- Networking with a transactional or desperate tone; waiting for merit to be recognised.
- Trying to convince price-sensitive clients to pay premium fees.

## Checklist
- [ ] Specific on both what I do and for whom?
- [ ] Passes the room test and the distance test?
- [ ] Can I win at a premium, not only on price?
- [ ] Is strategy visible (process, cases, testimonials with results)?
- [ ] A repeatable route to the top of the funnel?
