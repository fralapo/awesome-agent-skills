# Chapter 04: Anchoring, Price Brackets and Option Ladders

## Core Idea
Buyers judge a number relative to the numbers they have already seen. Whoever puts the first credible number on the table sets the frame for every concession after it. Three tools control that frame: **name the first number yourself** (anchor), **give a range, higher number first** (bracket) and **present three options instead of one** (ladder). All three exist to stop the client from shopping you against single-price vendors or against their own low figure.

## 1. Anchoring
- The first number sets the frame. Name yours first, high but credible. Do not let the client's number become the reference point; ignore it and restate yours.
- Never anchor near your own floor; the anchor should leave room to settle at or above your target.
- State the value first, then the fee, so the fee looks modest (ch01).
- **Round numbers**, not precise ones. A precise number invites the client to unbundle it and ask for a cheaper formula; a round number is defended with value and size as proxies.
- Say the number once and hold it. Confidence is built by charging it; apologising or over-explaining weakens the anchor.
- Test your anchor: if the client does not react to the top of a range, the price is too low.
- Respond to a lowball by rejecting the anchor and asking for another: "That does not work for this scope. What number would you come back with?" Negotiate against the client's own figures; do not move your final price (ch08).
- One school of thought: ask for double the current rate on the next proposal without justification; if the client declines, move on rather than negotiate.

## 2. Price Bracketing (reading the budget without asking outright)
1. Give a range from round numbers, **higher number first**, then **pause**. Watch the reaction. Do not sell during the bracket.
2. Ask whether the range works, or which end feels comfortable.
3. If the client reacts, ask what they can afford and what would work; do not rebuild the bid on the spot.
4. If the range is too low, ask for their price point and bracket around it. Your floor is your minimum; if the budget is below it, offer a referral or reduced scope.
5. Propose a figure near the **top of their comfortable zone**, not beyond it. Get explicit confirmation that this keeps you in contention, then bid inside that figure. A confirmed number prevents later lowballing; if one happens, say calmly and factually that it breaks the agreement.
6. Do not bid far outside the range the client named; it wastes both sides' time.
- **Magnitude-of-scale question**: "Is this a 50k or a 500k project?" It is a low-friction way to find the order of magnitude before any proposal.
- **When asked "what does it cost?"** answer with a bracket, not a vague reply (a vague answer lets the client stall) and not a single low figure you later cut when challenged. Say the price before showing the work. Stay silent after the number. Never apologise after it.
- **Bracket with a question:** state the range and ask whether it works; if the client needs less, ask for their number instead of bidding against yourself. If resistance appears, restate a higher bracket and ask what works for them, and be ready to walk away with a smaller alternative or a referral rather than accept a budget before scope is explored.
- **Soft close on price**: give a range early, then price within it; the client edits themselves into the range.
- Anchoring high on an option ladder can move a much lower stated budget a long way (one reported case: a 30k budget closed at 300k); treat it as an anecdote, not a rule.
- Illustrative brackets from the material: a typical 10k project quoted as 30-60k to test reaction; strategy starting 30-50k; a small RFP bracketed as floor and ceiling (2k-8k). Scale these to your market; they show the *shape* (a wide range, top first).
- A broad range is explicitly "not a firm commitment" and "based on the assumptions stated"; a firm number comes after discovery.

## 3. The Three-Option Ladder
- Never present a single price; give the client something to compare against, so they compare your options with each other instead of with outside vendors.
- Order: **premium first** (full "white glove": strategy, execution, oversight, training), then **core / most popular** (main deliverables, no extra oversight), then **minimal entry** (one deliverable). Name each option and describe it concretely.
- State the top price, then pause and let it anchor.
- Put the option you most want sold in the **middle**; buyers avoid extremes and tend to choose the middle (compromise effect). Reset the list if needed so your target sits mid-ladder.
- Spacing example used: premium 45k / core 20k / minimal 10k, roughly 2x steps. Treat spacing as one school of thought; the principle is a clear step between tiers.
- Do not over-explain each tier; let the anchor work. For a deeper discussion of the compromise effect see `marketing-mba` ch08.

## Reference Points You Control
- Contrast with the cost of the problem or of a bad outcome (ch08).
- Compare with purchases where cheap is risky (safety- or business-critical).
- Compare with the cost of hiring and training an in-house team, then let the buyer draw the conclusion.
- Higher perceived value is a better lever than a lower price: lower the price tension only as a last resort (ch11).

## Example Phrasings
- "For projects like this, clients typically invest between 60 and 30 thousand. Where does that sit with what you had in mind?" (pause)
- "There are three ways to do this: the full programme, the core build, or a starter. Most clients choose the core build."
- "Is this closer to a 50k or a 500k project for you?"

## Common Mistakes
- Giving one low number; giving the low end first.
- Anchoring near your own number or accepting the client's first figure as the starting point.
- Selling or justifying during the bracket.
- A single price that lets the client shop around.
- Precise numbers that invite unbundling.
- Dropping your number to meet theirs, which anchors you lower each round.

## Checklist
- [ ] Did I name a number or range before they did?
- [ ] High end first, then a pause?
- [ ] Round figures?
- [ ] Three options, target in the middle, premium shown first?
- [ ] Did I get explicit confirmation of the range before bidding?
