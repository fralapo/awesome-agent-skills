# Chapter 10: Judging Design: Levels, Two-Axis Audit and Perception Tests

## Core Idea
"Does it look good?" is not a review question. A direction is judged by what the intended audience will perceive, in order, in the first seconds, and by whether every choice points to the same conclusion. This chapter gives the art director a quality ladder (five levels), a two-axis audit and a set of cheap perception tests that expose weak work without relying on taste. It complements the review routine in ch04 (which says how to brief, route and phrase feedback) and the craft critique method in `graphic-designer` ch07 (which names faults at the file level).

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Five Levels of Design Quality
Each level has a different kind of thinking and its own test. Most work stops at level 1 or 2; a direction should be judged on the highest level the brief actually needs.

| Level | What it delivers | Test |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Aesthetics | Polished, current, "professional" | **Swap test**: cover the logo and imagine another company in the same sector. If the piece still works unchanged, it is only level 1. |
| 2. Organization | Elements have a position and a sequence; hierarchy controls when things are discovered (size, position, contrast, spacing act as timing; empty space acts as punctuation) | **Three-second test**: show it for three seconds, hide it, ask what they saw first, second, third. Inconsistent answers mean it is not organized. |
| 3. Communication | Image and headline express one idea by two different routes | Ask "what will this audience believe this means?", not "what does it say?". |
| 4. Persuasion | A reason to want or need it: reframes the function around something the decision-maker already values, joined to a risk | Check the sequence aspiration, risk, solution, action, and that the call to action is urgent rather than passive ("protect your operations", not "discover"). |
| 5. Memory | Distinctive assets repeated with variation and attached to the brand | Ask what will still exist in someone's mind tomorrow, and whether it is linked to the brand or only to the ad. |

- Attention is earned by aesthetics, guided by organization, understood through communication, desired through persuasion and retained through memory.
- Warning sign of level 1: the strongest feedback anyone can give is "clean, cool, professional".
- Communication helps someone understand an idea; persuasion makes it feel personally consequential.
- Memorability needs two things at once: repeated distinctive elements with enough variation, and the memorable image tied to the brand (the geometry of the mark echoed in the visual). Otherwise people remember the visual and not who made it.
- Persuasion levers and message craft belong to `creative-director` (reference on persuasion and visual communication); this chapter only checks that the look does not undercut them.

### Two-Axis Audit
Every piece is checked on two axes and must pass both.
1. **Technical axis**: focal point, real contrast (not only hue: also size and scale), flow that directs the eye instead of a rigid static grid, hierarchy, balance.
2. **Psychological axis**: fit to the audience's mentality and to the context (what is the job of this piece: a web banner grabs attention and funnels to a link; a magazine cover has impact on a shelf).
Average work usually passes only the technical axis, or part of it.

### Perception Tests (run before approving)
- **Perception-gap test**: show the layout briefly and ask what they remember, in order. Wrong first item, or a missed call to action, means the hierarchy failed. If you must explain a design, it failed. Aesthetics are subjective; confusion is not.
- **Consistency-of-perception rule**: type, imagery, photography, material and tone must lead to the same conclusion. Premium type over low-budget photography breaks the message; the same product can be made to feel cheap or premium through layout, colour and typography alone, but the feeling must match the real audience (a premium look is wrong for a low-budget teenage audience).
- **Attention-thief test**: cover the hero element and look at everything else. What steals attention without having earned it (secondary image, decorative shape, bright colour, texture, badge, extra copy line)? Reduce first (smaller, quieter, lower contrast, further away); delete if it has no job. Fewer entry points means faster understanding; several entry points force the viewer to negotiate before understanding.
- **Emotional-clarity test**: can someone read the intended emotion in under a second without reading a word?
- **Squint or thumbnail test**: shrink the piece or step back. If the path through it is still balanced and easy, the flow works.
- **Viewing-distance question**: what must be recognised from 10 m, 5 m and 1 m? That decides the size of the hero, the headline and the supporting information. Test in the real medium (a platform, a shelf, a feed), not 30 cm from the screen.
- **Logo-removal test**: hide the mark. If typography, colour, composition and imagery no longer feel like one brand, there is a logo but not an identity (see ch12 and ch03).

### The Three Questions and the Why Check
- For any piece ask: what is it trying to make me feel, what is it trying to make me do, which decisions got me there?
- **Why check**: for every element ask why it is there, why this font, why this white space, why this colour. No solid answer means the element is not strong enough. Explain the finished piece aloud to an imaginary client; what you cannot justify is the weakest part.
- **Fresh-eyes review**: export the piece, leave it for a few hours, reopen it on a different device or screen. Looking too long normalises problems. Evaluate midway against the brief and from the audience's position, with your own emotion set aside.
- **Know when to stop**: do not spend half the time on the last 2 percent; be comfortable leaving a piece unfinished and returning with a clear mind.

## Rules and Numbers
- Three sizes are enough to test flow: hook (largest), secondary detail, finisher (smallest: call to action or logo). The most important item is not always the largest (event poster: date and place may be the key while an image is the hook).
- Layered legibility: understood in about three seconds, still rewarding at about fifteen.
- A decision budget of about four (what it is, what to look at, when or where, everything else grouped) keeps a composition decidable.
- Define success first (sign-ups, sales, recall, conversion) and judge backwards from it. A piece that looks only "okay" but converts better is a win; a lovely piece that misses its job is not.
- Clients who ask to "fill the space" or to copy an existing design are asking for level 1; give them reasoning and a levelled alternative rather than refusing.

## Where Opinions Differ
- **Friction**: conventional practice treats friction (tight leading, jarring shape, partial word) as a fault. A more advanced position uses it on purpose, seasoned in small doses and balanced by calm zones. Rule: friction is allowed only if the intent can be named and the piece still passes the three-second test.
- **Simplicity versus dynamism**: depends on message complexity and audience, not on taste. Younger and action-oriented audiences and attention-grabbing pieces tolerate more dynamic compositions; complex or information-heavy messages need a more static one.

## Common Mistakes
- Approving level 1 work because it is clean.
- Testing only on the designer's screen at full size.
- Testing hierarchy by asking "is it clear?" instead of asking what was seen, in order.
- Adding emphasis to fix a hierarchy problem that was caused by too many competing elements.
- Judging against personal taste instead of against the agreed criteria (ch12).

## Checklist
- [ ] Which level does the brief need, and which level does the piece reach?
- [ ] Swap test passed (the piece belongs to this brand and sector only)
- [ ] Three-second test: first, second, third read as intended
- [ ] Attention thieves reduced or removed
- [ ] Emotion readable in under a second
- [ ] Type, image, photography and material all point to one conclusion
- [ ] Checked at thumbnail size and at real viewing distance
- [ ] Every element passes the why check
- [ ] Craft faults routed to `graphic-designer`, idea faults to `creative-director`, usability faults to `ux-ui-expert`
