# Chapter 08: Studying References and Ethical Borrowing

## Core Idea
References are raw material only when they are analysed, not collected. An art director must be able to describe a reference precisely enough that a designer can reproduce one component and change another. Skill grows by faithful copying followed by transformation; the line between influence and plagiarism is intent, process and disclosure. This chapter supplies the analysis method, a search loop for finding references, and the ethics of borrowing. (The mechanics of reference collection into moodboards are in ch02; measuring components is in `graphic-designer` ch01.)

## Procedure 1: Five Ingredients (Analysing a Reference)
1. **Describe what you see, objectively**: no judgement, no interpretation.
2. **Pick one element** (line quality, figures, colour, materials, words, composition), draw it crudely with a blunt tool and write what you see next to it.
3. **Refine the vocabulary** until it is precise: "rendered or not rendered", "irregular, rough line", "flat, no perspective". Vague words (colourful, busy, a centrepiece, violent) mean the observation is not yet specific; name the mark-making that causes the feeling.
4. **Identify the tools and materials**, since they shape the result.
5. **List what you would copy and what you would change.**
Rules: you cannot make what you cannot describe; keep interpretation as a second pass; work on one element at a time; if stuck, return to the five ingredients; do not try to reproduce the whole piece.
Study award collections and analyse why each work succeeds; build a library of recurring formulas.

## Procedure 2: Reference Search Loop (words, then images)
1. Look up the dictionary meaning of the key word (the first meaning is often not the one you need).
2. Search broad, then narrow by adding one word. Typing four or five words too early returns almost nothing.
3. Scan quickly; if nothing fits after about three screens, change the word ("three flicks").
4. Try conceptual synonyms (concept, idea, metaphor) to reach images that interpret rather than illustrate.
5. Read the tags and descriptions of good results to harvest vocabulary, then search with those words.
6. Open related images and the same creator's other work to continue the trail.
7. Save anything that sparks an idea into a bucket; judge later, after enough material is collected.
Curated libraries are less noisy than open web search. Good images combine two unrelated things: look for the common thread. When stuck, change the search terms or the source instead of searching harder.

## Learning Model: Shu, Ha, Ri
- **Shu**: follow the method faithfully for a sustained period.
- **Ha**: break and question the method.
- **Ri**: leave it and build your own.
Copy faithfully, then combine, then transform. A master-copy exercise (closely copying a finished, crafted frame) shows that the skill gap makes an exact copy impossible, so the result changes by itself. Common failures: jumping to your own approach before mastering the method; expecting a formula to remove the need for practice.

## Ethics: Influence or Plagiarism
- Plagiarism is a blatant, unacknowledged copy that is profited from. Influence is judged by **process and disclosure**, not by whether an idea has any precedent.
- Know your references and say where an idea came from when it matters; be ready to name influences. Present the "why" of a choice, not only the "what".
- The rule of borrowing: make the copy better, not worse.
- **Exit test**: ask what you would do to stop copying; if the answer is clear and you have not done it, the work is plagiarism.
- Never sell borrowed work to a client as original, especially when the original owner could object. Do not recycle a famous piece and rely on the audience's ignorance. Ignorance of lineage is no defence once the source is found.
- Distinct from claiming total originality: the honest aim is a distinctive recombination.

## Where Opinions Differ: Boards or Making
- **Collect references first** when the field is unfamiliar: industry conventions, tier, vocabulary.
- **Start making early** because ideas appear in the process, and boards and tear sheets can replace work. Use a bounded collection phase, then analyse components, then make. Do not bring a favourite style to every problem.

## Common Mistakes
- Collecting images without describing them.
- Describing a feeling without naming the cause in the work.
- Judging each result before collecting enough material.
- Assuming the first word you think of is the one the world uses.
- Presenting borrowed work without lineage.

## Checklist
- [ ] Reference described objectively before interpreted
- [ ] One element analysed at a time, with precise vocabulary
- [ ] Tools and materials identified
- [ ] Copy-and-change list written
- [ ] Sources and influences nameable
- [ ] Search terms changed when stuck, not intensified
