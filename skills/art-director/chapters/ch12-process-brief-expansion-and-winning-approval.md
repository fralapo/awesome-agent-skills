# Chapter 12: Process, Brief Expansion and Winning Approval

## Core Idea
Approval is won before the presentation, by the way the brief was expanded, the criteria were agreed and the reasoning was made visible. The art director runs a fixed order (understand, research, derive a visual language, design, test in context, refine, deliver and document), expands a thin brief into a written objective with emotional and cultural layers, and presents work so that the client judges it against agreed words instead of personal liking. This chapter extends ch05 (strategy sheet, taste discovery, decoding vague adjectives) and ch04 (briefing, reviewing, presenting basics); it does not repeat them.

## Procedure 1: Expand the Brief
1. **Three layers**: split the brief into an objective layer (the practical goal), an emotional layer (the response to evoke) and a cultural layer (language anchors, subculture, references the audience uses). Thin briefs are enriched by research and by a drafted visual language, with generated or template suggestions treated as input, never as decisions.
2. **One-sentence objective** that can be said in a five-second conversation ("make people feel an urgent need to sign up now, because otherwise they are missing out"). It drives structure and where the call to action sits. Without it the design fails psychologically from the start.
3. **Define success** in outcomes (sign-ups, sales, recall, conversion) and design backwards from it. Clients pay for outcomes such as clarity, trust and positioning, not for pixels.
4. **Write a real objective in tension form** when the brief has opposites ("appeal to young and old", "modern yet traditional"). A tension names the form: a traditional shield rendered minimally answers both poles.
5. **Four pre-design checks**: canvas size and orientation (a square composition forced onto a wide banner fails), context of use ("context is king": a roadside billboard is seen in seconds at speed, a brochure is read actively, a feed is thumb-scrolled), how dynamic the piece should be, and contextual relevancy (season, industry, brand values, audience).
6. **Constraints list**: platform sizes, dark and light mode, paper and ink, print blacks, viewing distance, accessibility needs handed to `ux-ui-expert`.

## Procedure 2: Research to Moodboard
1. Research four things: the purpose, the competitors in the niche (stand out yet respect conventions, or break them on purpose), the target market, and the industry culture or subculture. Depth scales with budget and time.
2. **Three keyword boxes**: target market, brand, brief requirements. Pull keywords from the brief (modern, sleek, trendy, vibrant) and check every template, reference and result against them to avoid a generic look.
3. **Moodboard contents**: colour swatches with hex codes, imagery of the target person, candidate typefaces, layout references from the same niche and from adjacent media (absorb the feel, do not copy; see ch08), and notes. Add a goals checklist on the first page so the board can be reviewed at later stages.
4. **Emotion-to-element table**: pull the emotions from the brief, list four categories (colour, typography, imagery, layout) and write how each expresses the emotion. Chain: core message in one sentence, tone, emotional goal, colour, typography, symbolism. Keep two or three typefaces.
5. **Sketch before software**: a one-minute paper sketch fixes the focal point, the supporting message and the secondary details; software only executes the decision. Layout is the arrangement that displays information and leads the eye; a poor layout means the message is not conveyed.
6. **Brand-voice alignment for the mark**: before geometry, write five words the brand would never say, five it would overuse, and one tweet-length sentence it would say. A bold voice should not get a timid mark; a calm voice should not get aggressive shapes. Hand the result to `graphic-designer` ch06 together with the brief.
7. **Show a person from the target audience** the direction and ask whether it excites them; it is cheaper than any argument.

## Procedure 3: Set the Judging Criteria First
1. Derive **three words** from research and strategy (for example rare, refined, reserved). Let the visual language of the pitch back them before the mark or hero visual is shown.
2. The client then asks "does this feel rare, refined, reserved?" instead of "do I like it?". Research, strategy, identity and presentation trace back to one central idea.
3. **Adjectives become evidence**: for every claim word ("flexible", "recognisable", "adaptable") show proof in the work (applications on garments, labels, interfaces; recognition without the mark; the system adapting). The viewer's own conclusion is stronger than the designer's claim.
4. Keep a **breadcrumb** (ch05): later feedback is located against the three words, so a disagreement is either "the words were wrong" or "the visuals do not carry them".

## Procedure 4: Present the Work
- **Context is part of the design.** Photography, architecture, materials and clothing around a mark must reinforce the identity: cheap photography makes the work feel cheap, and the same mark next to fluorescent plastic reads differently. Design the world in which the work makes most sense. Never show a bare mark on white and ask "do you like it?".
- **Vary density.** Quiet moments make the busy ones hit harder; making about 70 percent of a composition less dominant often reads as more professional. Maxing out every composition is the beginner instinct.
- **Structure of the presentation**: the client's story and ethos; the objectives (about three, optionally as a checklist ticked off later); the process shown step by step so the concept is not plucked from thin air; the design-led pitch itself with one running theme, one set of fonts and colours and a visible hierarchy; industry-appropriate language (sleek for technology, formal and conservative for finance or law); theming around the client's own colours and typeface when the identity is strong; **close the loop** by returning to the original problem and showing how it is solved; leave an open line for feedback and future work. Never present in a plain word-processing document.
- **Tactile close-ups** (leather, stitching, embossing, metal catching light) let the viewer's memory supply sensations that words cannot.
- **System test in the room**: show the identity on a thumbnail, on dark and light backgrounds and across formats; show it without the mark (logo-removal test, ch10). Different colours, type and images have defined roles; protect powerful elements through scarcity (ch11).
- **Logo delivery kit**: primary mark, secondary lockup, icon-only version, grayscale version and a usage sheet (margins, contrast tips, when to use which). Production details go to `graphic-designer` ch01 and ch12.
- Remain polite, confident and calm; you are selling the reasoning, not defending your taste.

## Procedure 5: Stubborn or Mistaken Feedback
1. Build the strong direction and the client's idea **side by side**, both at comparable finish.
2. Explain each decision in the client's terms (white space aids navigation and comprehension; contrast gets the call to action seen) and name the concrete flaw in the alternative.
3. **Tie the argument to money or the goal**: readability, conversion, recall, cost of reprinting. The designer holds design authority and the name stays attached to the work; being agreeable on a weak concept is the costlier path.
4. Educate with case studies, quick mockups or sketches in real context and jargon-free benefit-based explanations; clients approve more often when they understand.
5. Back-stop with the process agreed up front: number of revision rounds (two is typical for a logo), a price for work after the completion date, deposit before work starts and no final or source files before payment. Money, contract and pricing detail: `freelance-designer-ops` and `pricing-and-sales-negotiation`.

## Procedure 6: Before You Deliver
- Spell-check; print and test on paper; mock up in context (on a shelf next to competitors); get a trusted second opinion; fresh-eyes pass (ch10).
- Keep one asset library with version history, revision notes and a marked final version; centralise client comments and versions on the design itself so vague notes ("make it pop") do not scatter across threads.
- Perceived professionalism is judged instantly through organisation: named files, a structured walkthrough, readable portfolio thumbnails. A stranger should be able to open and finish the project without questions.
- Back up work in more than two places.

## Where Opinions Differ
- **Pipeline length**: models run from four to seven steps; the order is constant (understand, research, derive visual language, design, test, refine, deliver). Pick the shortest that still includes research and testing in context.
- **AI-assisted briefs and palettes**: acceptable as a starting point, never as a final decision; direction and vision must exist before generation.
- **How many routes to show**: see ch05 (wide when taste is unknown, deep when known, single when approved).

## Common Mistakes
- Opening the software before the objective is one sentence.
- Skipping empathy and research and going straight to visuals.
- Presenting without agreed criteria, so the room judges by taste.
- Adjectives in the deck with no proof in the work.
- A bare mark on white, or a pitch deck that does not look designed.
- Accepting a weak concept because the client has a strong vision.
- Scattered files and unclear approved versions.

## Checklist
- [ ] Brief split into objective, emotional and cultural layers
- [ ] One-sentence objective and a defined success measure
- [ ] Four pre-design checks answered
- [ ] Three keyword boxes and a moodboard with a goals checklist
- [ ] Brand-voice alignment written before the mark is drawn
- [ ] Three judging words agreed before the reveal
- [ ] Every claim word has proof in the work
- [ ] Presentation shows context, process, running theme and closes the loop
- [ ] Contested feedback answered side by side with a goal-linked reason
- [ ] Delivery kit, library and backups ready
