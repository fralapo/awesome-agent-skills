# Chapter 09: Product, Material and Finish Direction

## Core Idea
For physical products, packaging-adjacent objects and wearables, the look is decided as much by finish, material, proportion and surface logic as by form. A direction here is a set of **fixed values that define the perimeter**, plus freedom to change anything inside it. Perceived price tier is communicated visually, so a finish or an accessory can move a product up or down a tier without touching its function. Every visible choice must answer a function, a manufacturing reason or an emotion; otherwise it is decoration. The idea comes from the creative director; construction and print production go to the graphic designer (`graphic-designer` ch12); interface and screen behaviour go to `ux-ui-expert`.

## Procedure 1: Direction as a Perimeter (System for Objects)
1. **Write the brand values as a perimeter** (for example performance, craftsmanship, innovation, design). Anything may change if it respects them; this keeps a long-lived range coherent while individual designers stay free (system-rules logic in ch03).
2. **Organise materials and colours into a few collections that map the identity**, for example: a *heritage* collection (the codes people already recognise), an *innovative* collection (materials borrowed from fashion, interiors, nautical or architecture work) and a *performance* collection (technical materials, bold colours). Each collection has its own rules; together they cover the brand's range.
3. **Customisation by reference brief**: for a client-specific variant, translate the colours, livery and details of an iconic model into the contemporary one, so the personal story is recognisable and the product still sits inside the perimeter.
4. **Involve engineering and other departments from the early phases.** Technical needs often produce stylistic unity (a shape dictated by airflow, a seam dictated by assembly); designing late around fixed engineering produces add-ons.
5. **Draw inspiration from any field** (nature, animals, architecture, nautical) and cross-contaminate; aim for a **timeless interpretation of trends**, not trend-chasing.
6. **Expose the sketch process** (annotated sketches) as part of the story when presenting; it shows the reasoning, not only the result.

## Procedure 2: Reading and Setting Perceived Tier
Finish, colour and proportion say the price before any label does.
- **Glossy plastic reads budget; matte or refined finishes with a metal accent read premium.** Check the design beside its price-tier peers, not alone.
- **One-piece, milled or continuous parts** raise perceived quality (and reduce assembly and failure points). Flat glass or panel where a curved, continuous piece could be used wastes a premium material.
- **Reduce the palette to one body colour** and use contrast accents (a transparent part, a chrome or metal detail) for a premium look; remove surface changes that serve no function.
- **Volumes should meet tangentially** so the object reads as one continuous form; hide hinges, seams and screws where possible; keep slots, vents and grilles balanced and aligned on one line. Visible seams, misaligned slots, exposed screws and cut lines read as unfinished.
- **Recessed vs flush details**: a flush (tangent) mesh is cheaper to make and assemble but exposed and easy to dirty on fragile, exposed parts; a recessed grille protects.
- **Finishes must survive wear.** If a protective case is needed to hide a fragile finish (polished metal or clear caps that scratch in a day), change the finish.
- **Status products may carry the brand mark**, placed where it is visible in use; a logo change on a product needs a reason that is explained.
- **Old-fashioned details** (a retro handle, dated lights) make a modern shape look dated; mimicking a brand's surface style without its emotional cues copies the form and loses the point.
- **Signal check**: if most of the audience reads a design as "not on brand", treat it as information; a design too far from expectations may need an intermediate step before the full change.

## Procedure 3: Structure-First and Consistency Checks
- **Ask of every vertex, line and part whether it serves a function, the structure or the manufacturing process**; remove those that do not. When the outer skin is also the structure, remove reinforcing points it does not need; a new internal layout can free the front or another zone to be redesigned.
- **Materials needing fewer machining steps** cut parts and finishing cost (no paint or chrome required). A radical choice is credible when it follows from structure and manufacturing, not from taste.
- **Continuous, flush transitions** (glass to metal) give visual continuity and often aerodynamic or ergonomic benefit.
- **Shape language is a system**: one consistent family of curves (for example super-ellipses rather than plain rounded squares) across surfaces, icons and text. Textured grip patterns should echo the product's existing shapes; symbols should be angled to match button geometry; labels should read correctly from the user's grip position; the grip-change line should follow the hand's contour.
- **Mirrored parts**: identical mirrored parts for the two sides (same layout, same centre) cut cost and confusion.
- **Dead or blocked space** can carry useful information.
- **Visible controls must communicate their function**; frequently used compartments should be visible, not hidden; avoid ambiguous status indicators.
- **Competitor interiors copied with every button and no hierarchy** are a defect: rank frequent controls and keep a few physical ones for the rest.

## Procedure 4: Product Redesign Sequence
1. **Diagnose**: a documented list of usability and aesthetic problems of the existing product, tied to evidence. A redesign without diagnosis is unjustified restyling.
2. **Review** candidate concepts against that list; each change answers one named problem.
3. **Present your version and justify each decision.**
- **Cosmetic-only redesign**: keep the geometry that is functionally tuned; change finish, material, accents and detail. This shifts perceived tier at low risk.
- **Design for both main grip or use positions** and for the orientations users actually hold the product in, not only the marketing one; avoid accessories needed to make the product complete.
- **Core vs accessory**: the core product must work fully on its own; sell add-ons separately to keep the entry price low. Check how an attached accessory changes behaviour, and integrate functional parts (hinges, stoppers) so they read as deliberate detail rather than bolted-on parts. Positioning a device as a computer while keeping its touch-first behaviour unchanged is a contradiction.
- **Wearables**: test the attachment in real use (a magnet placed inside a garment is awkward to remove, set down and re-attach); prefer one always-worn default configuration (strap, clip) over a set of accessories; a wearable on clothing is also a fashion accessory, so give it character of its own; a bold new category needs an emotional story, not a formal tone and a grey palette.
- **Minimalism needs emotional content**; a product that looks like its parent device without its own identity is a missed identity.
- **Cross-sector work**: learn the new domain's problems first, then apply your own visual and functional approach; test ergonomics with real users (an attractive shape that is uncomfortable fails). Separate functional-first (industrial) from emotional-first (automotive) judgement and check both.

## Procedure 5: Justifying Cost and Presenting
- Expensive custom parts are justified only when they solve a specific problem; explain which problem each one solves (optical clarity for sensors, scratch resistance, comfort with fewer seams, one device fitting different heads through modular parts). Name the one deliberate compromise rather than hiding it.
- Social presence is a design requirement for wearables that cover the face or ears: show when the wearer is aware of the surroundings, hide it in full immersion, and transition gradually so others read availability.
- **Launch communication must show the product's features**, not only teaser images; naming the product after the new feature is a valid way to communicate change.
- **Test demos for failure cases**; a public demo that fails damages credibility more than none.

## Interface Principles to Pass On (to `ux-ui-expert`)
Screen and control behaviour is not decided here. When a product brief includes controls or a home screen, hand over these criteria with the direction:
- A control gets one primary and one secondary gesture; stacked gestures (half press, full press, hold, double tap, swipe) with hidden state are hard to learn. Show current state; offer a reset-to-default for settings changed through hidden menus; group by mode and show only what matters for the mode; keep a visible state when other options are active.
- Rotate interfaces with the device orientation so text stays upright; dim inactive areas of a viewfinder.
- Organise a home screen by frequency of use and content type: centre and align the primary navigation, put profile and account top-right with settings beside it, keep power visible, give a dashboard top weight only if it shows live information used daily, use the content's natural shape (vertical cover art, not squares), reduce status indicators to one, show an immersive preview and one primary action on store pages.
- Spatial interfaces borrow physical depth cues: thickness, shadow, sharpness and blur for distance.
- Use feedback animation to explain a state change instead of a loading wait, and give system messages some character; formal technical messages drain emotion.

## Common Mistakes
- Changing finish and form together and calling it a redesign with no diagnosis.
- Glossy plastic or visible seams on a premium-priced object.
- Fragile finishes hidden by accessories.
- Decoration with no function (symbols not matching button geometry, grip patterns unrelated to the form).
- Opening a hardware brief with the interface or screen and leaving engineering to the end.
- Hiding the one compromise instead of stating it.
- Copying a competitor's interior or surface style without its emotional cues.

## Checklist
- [ ] Brand values written as a perimeter; collections mapped to the range
- [ ] Engineering and manufacturing consulted from the start
- [ ] Finish, material and form checked beside price-tier peers
- [ ] Every surface change and line justified by function, structure or emotion
- [ ] Shape language, labels and symbols consistent and oriented to use
- [ ] Redesign: diagnosis written, each change tied to a problem
- [ ] Core works without accessories; attachment tested in real use
- [ ] Cost justified part by part; compromise named; demo failure cases tested
- [ ] Control and screen criteria handed to `ux-ui-expert`
