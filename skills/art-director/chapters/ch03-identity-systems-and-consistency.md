# Chapter 03: Identity Systems and Consistency

## Core Idea
A brand is the perception people hold, shaped by every interaction; a logo, palette or packaging is only the foundation of an ongoing process. The art director owns the system around the mark: rules for colour, type, imagery, iconography and motion, how they shift by medium and audience, and how they stay recognisable over time. Logo construction itself is the graphic designer's craft (`graphic-designer` ch06).

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Visual language over logo**: an organisation should be recognisable even with its mark hidden, through a consistent and distinctive typographic and visual system. Unify an institution with many names or sub-brands under one strong identity so it reads as a single institution.
- **System rules**: define what stays fixed and what flexes by medium and audience (colour roles, type hierarchy, image treatment, icon style, motion principles). Rules are written, not implied.
- **Three-format test**: apply logo, colours, type and icons to three very different formats (social post, slide, product mockup). If it still feels like the same brand it is a system; otherwise it is only a style.
- **Stress test**: can the mark survive animation, embroidery and favicon size? If not, return it to the graphic designer with evidence.
- **Touchpoint priority**: design the identity for the touchpoint that matters most for the business (packaging for a product, menu for a cafe, interface for software), and keep the system flexible enough to grow from one product to a parent brand.
- **Scaling**: typography and illustration style must travel from mobile apps to billboards and social animation; updating only the logo for launch assets leaves the system behind.
- **Future-proofing**: expect the mark to appear in motion, on merchandise, in 3D and possibly AR; plan a refresh every five to ten years, where small refinements of proportion and spacing can go unnoticed while keeping it fresh.
- **Pre-mortem**: ask where the system could fail (logo misread, palette failing on different backgrounds, type unreadable on small screens) and test those cases before launch.
- **Series as a family**: recurring assignments (monthly event posters, seasonal editions) can carry the designer's own concepts and vary by season or theme while belonging to one family.
- **Typographic voice**: letter anatomy carries meaning (a high or low crossbar, stroke weight, letter height shift the era feel); a full weight range in one wordmark can express variety and breadth.

## Rules and Numbers
- Strategy filters decide ties: purpose, mission, vision, audience, non-negotiable values.
- Pair the mark with a restrained typeface and a minimal palette (for example mostly black and white with one small accent); add texture and real product imagery for an organic feel.
- Include a monogram variant and containers (seal-like shapes) in the system when the name is long or the format small.
- Wayfinding and signage can be emotional as well as functional (orientation that also builds a bond with place).

## Common Mistakes
- Delivering assets without rules, so the next designer rebuilds the look from memory.
- A system tested only on the website.
- Changing the logo while typography and imagery stay generic.
- Ignoring the main touchpoint and polishing secondary ones.
