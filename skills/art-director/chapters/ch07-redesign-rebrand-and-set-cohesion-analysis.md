# Chapter 07: Redesign, Rebrand and Set-Cohesion Analysis

## Core Idea
Redesign work is judged by what it keeps as much as by what it changes. Recognition (colour, shelf presence, a signature cue, a legacy element) is equity built over years; a logo or pack change destroys some of it and triggers backlash, so change only as much as the positioning requires and plan the rollout across all touchpoints. The art director owns this direction-level analysis; the construction of the new work goes to the graphic designer.

## Procedure: Analyse an Existing Brand or Pack
1. **List the problems** you can see (too detailed, generic, dated, illegible small, wrong tier, hierarchy confused). Turn each into a candidate fix.
2. **Take an equity inventory**: which elements people recognise at a glance (colour, shape, signature cue, legacy element)? Mark each keep, evolve or drop. Keep brand equity unless the brief asks for a full reset; a full reset loses recognition. Do not drop a legacy element from the front without a reason.
3. **Check the constraints**: fixed logo or colours, fixed copy, shape (changing a bottle or pack shape implies retooling cost), cost of change for the client.
4. **Check positioning**: neither too upstream nor too downstream for the category and the buyer. Compare **perceived price** from design cues with actual price; they should align. Design from the buyer's point of view, then find where it overlaps the brand's attributes.
5. **Rank what must be communicated** (what it is, product name, brand, then the rest) and test the redesign against that ranking (details in `graphic-designer` ch12).
6. **Define the improvement**: a redesign must be significantly better than the original on something named (recognition, clarity, tier, flexibility), not merely different.
7. **Judge against the business goal**, not only taste. Strong reactions to a logo mostly reflect taste; the brief, the audience and the business strategy decide. Judging a mark by first-impression aesthetics alone is like judging a long swim on a diving score.

## Procedure: Decide Whether to Change the Mark
1. Is there a business reason (repositioning, new audience, merger, a mark that fails in use)? "It looks old" alone is a weak reason.
2. If the reason is real, choose the level: refine (small adjustments to proportion and spacing, which may go unnoticed), evolve (keep recognisable cues, change the detail rather than the whole outfit) or reset (accept loss of recognition).
3. **Plan the rollout** across every touchpoint, in priority order (`art-director` ch03); a logo updated while type and imagery stay generic leaves the system behind.
4. Prepare the explanation for stakeholders and audience. **Evolution presentation**: show the step-by-step path from the brand's legacy mark to the new one, so the client sees the logic instead of a leap. Start concrete work early (a weak early version gives feedback to build on) and keep two or three options open; pitching hard in one direction corners you. Give every touchpoint (envelope, card, menu) the same care as the main mark, and check the new mark does not resemble a well-known existing one.
5. Separate **trend** (a lasting shift) from **fad** (short-lived shape or style); ask why you follow either.
6. Check **fatigue**: will it still be pleasant after daily exposure?

## Procedure: Cohesion of a Set (covers, series, ranges)
Scoring order that works for a set of covers or packs:
1. **Concept** with an "aha" moment: something found on closer look.
2. **Theme conveyed**: the real theme of the content, read from analytical sources, not only the plot or product description.
3. **Presence** at the display distance (curiosity from afar, invitation to look closer).
4. **Cohesion**: the set reads as the work of one creator. Give each item its own character and a shared link (colour, grid, motif); a stray default element in one item breaks the family.
Also: a front cover or front panel is the hero; finishes and materials count toward memorability; a brief's constraints (maximum colours, no photography as the core, mandatory logos and barcodes) are part of the design problem.

## Examples (generic)
- A heritage spice brand wants a "fresh look": the inventory shows the deep red and a hand-drawn emblem are what shoppers spot; the redesign keeps both, simplifies the type hierarchy and removes three competing logos. Result: improved clarity, retained recognition.
- A set of six novels: each cover uses a different image device (a double exposure, a silhouette, a hidden shape) with the same palette limit and grid; reviewers ask whether the set reads as one creator.

## Common Mistakes
- Changing logo and colours when the brief forbids it.
- Designing for personal taste rather than the shopper.
- Resetting the brand because the team is tired of it (teams tire before audiences do).
- Reviewing one item of a set instead of the set.
- Evaluating the new mark on a white screen only.
- Buying a mark as a graphic, with no research on competition, history and positioning behind it.

## Checklist
- [ ] Problems listed and tied to fixes
- [ ] Equity inventory: keep, evolve, drop
- [ ] Constraints and cost of change checked
- [ ] Positioning and perceived price checked
- [ ] Named improvement over the original
- [ ] Rollout plan across touchpoints
- [ ] Set judged as a set
