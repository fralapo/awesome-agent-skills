# Chapter 05: Strategy to Direction and Client Discovery

## Core Idea
Visual decisions become objective and defensible when each step is written down and agreed with the client before it is visualised. The art director moves the client through gradual steps (words, attributes, tone, visuals, mark) so that the final design is never a surprise, and discovers the client's taste with concrete examples instead of adjectives. The goal, problem diagnosis and business framing come from the creative director (`creative-director` reference on discovery); this chapter turns that input into a direction the client has already half-approved.

## Procedure 1: Strategy Sheet to Direction
1. **Strategy sheet**: answer a fixed set of questions (goal, customer, challenges, how success is measured). Reduce the answers to about five keywords.
2. **Brand-attribute list and key differentiator**: from the keywords, write the attributes and the one thing that sets the brand apart.
3. **Attributes become voice**: turn each into behaviour. "Secure" becomes "protective, warm, non-judgmental". The voice is what the visuals must carry.
4. **Ask what the client counts as references and competitors**, then show that the result looks like none of them. Clients who say "different from" a competitor often mean "like them in the good ways": ask which.
5. **Scaffold**: get agreement in order: words, attributes, tone, visuals, mark. Each step is signed off before the next.
6. **Fit check with a stylescape** (see ch06) before any logo is drawn: a cheap look-and-feel test.
7. **Keep a breadcrumb**: every later decision references the agreed words, so a revision round can be traced and a disagreement located (the words were wrong, or the visuals do not carry them).
8. **Put the process in the proposal**: the feedback timeline, the number of rounds and a deadline for client input. Silence is a risk to schedule.

## Procedure 2: Discover Taste
Clients rarely articulate taste; abstract words hide different pictures.
1. **Ask what they like before what they need.**
2. **Taste-sorting exercise**: show a large set of past or reference work; each stakeholder marks favourites with one colour of note and dislikes with another.
3. **Compare**: shared picks give a direction. Split picks signal a decision-making problem to resolve before concepting; do not take a split room at face value.
4. **Read competitor language** for envy and aspiration.
5. **Test a described direction** and watch the reactions. Listen hardest when a client reacts strongly, positive or negative, to a specific example.
6. **Think like a shopper buying for someone else**: study what the client actually wears and keeps buying, not what they say. Clients who ask for something new still keep familiar cues; change the detail (the belt) rather than the whole outfit.

## Procedure 3: Decode the Brief
- **Listening sequence**: ask diagnostic questions; stay silent and listen with open ears, not "happy ears" that hear what you want; reflect the brief back and get confirmation ("is that what you are thinking?"); only then design.
- **Translate coded words** (epic, elegant, energetic, celebration) into concrete examples and simple rules. Ask the client to describe the scene; find the **spine**, the recurring element that holds the concept together; identify what is iconic and what must repeat.
- **Define vague adjectives** with a bad-to-good scale (see `graphic-designer` ch07) before they enter a brief or a review.
- Recognise your own assumptions before listening. Avoid questions that feel like a test ("how does it make you feel?" asked as an exam); ask for stories and scenes instead.
- Ask which direction the target lies in rather than demanding the exact answer; questions narrow the options and clients judge expertise by the questions you ask.
- Ask **how the client will decide** (price, results, quality or rapport) and weight the presentation accordingly.
- Be honest early if you are not the right fit; clients usually respond with respect, a changed scope or a referral.

## Where Opinions Differ: How Many Routes
- **About three distinct routes** (ch02, ch04) fits the early stage when the client has no taste anchor and the strategy is still being tested.
- **One or two deeply developed routes ("a mile deep, not a mile wide")** fit once discovery has narrowed the field: the client then faces one decision, not ten, and exploration shrinks with a higher win rate and less burnout.
- **One consolidated direction** fits the step after an approved stylescape: merge feedback from several routes into one, then comp that. Showing several loose comps to a client already past concept stage invites a restart.
Choose by stage: wide when taste is unknown, deep when it is known, single when it is approved.

## Common Mistakes
- Presenting a logo cold, with no process, which triggers silence and endless revisions.
- Relying on "I'll know it when I see it" feedback.
- Accepting suitcase words and exploring everything.
- Treating a strategy deck as read once instead of as a decision filter.
- Claiming total originality; the aim is a distinctive recombination of references the client can recognise as theirs.
- Moving to visuals while stakeholders disagree among themselves.

## Checklist
- [ ] Strategy sheet with about five keywords and a differentiator
- [ ] Attributes turned into a voice
- [ ] Client's references and competitors listed and avoided
- [ ] Taste sorted with concrete examples; split picks resolved
- [ ] Coded words translated into examples and rules
- [ ] Step-by-step sign-off with a breadcrumb back to agreed words
- [ ] Feedback timeline and input deadline in the proposal
