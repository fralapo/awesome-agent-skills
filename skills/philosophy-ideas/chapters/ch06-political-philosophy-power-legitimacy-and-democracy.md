# Chapter 06: Political Philosophy: Power, Legitimacy and Democracy

## Core Idea
In this model, political philosophy asks **who may rule, why anyone should obey, and how power is organised**. Religion and government are the two institutions that most shape ordinary life; changes in one (the Reformation) tend to travel into the other.

## 6.1 Realism in politics
- **Thesis (position)**: a political thinker may analyse how power is won and kept without moralising; the label "Machiavellian" (cunning and unscrupulous) is a one-dimensional reading.
- **Argument**: the Renaissance humanist turn put human beings and their individual experience at the centre, in religion (a Bible translated into a vernacular language made faith personal) and in government. The political realist is a political philosopher only: narrower but not shallower. Judging him by one famous book is like judging an author by one work.
- **Objections**: separating politics from morality may license abuse.
- **Reply**: the realist point is descriptive (what works) and a claim about the conditions of success; whether it should guide action is another question.
- **Limit**: only the framing is covered; details of his doctrine are not.

## 6.2 Natural freedom and the limits of divine right
- **Thesis**: people are born free and equal, not subjects by birth.
- **Argument**: a rival theory derived royal authority from the first man's authority over his descendants. Reply in two steps: (1) the biblical text does not support it: the grant of stewardship was to humankind, not to one man, and the commandment to honour parents is not a patriarchy; honouring parents differs from being their property. (2) Even if it did, scripture is the wrong basis for a system of government; reason is better. Infants are helpless, yet are born free in the way they are born rational: they have the faculty but cannot yet exercise it; so freedom and subjection to parents are consistent. Self-ownership follows.
- **Objections**: if freedom is only a latent capacity, then freedom can be postponed indefinitely; a state need not be limited by natural rights.
- **Reply**: the latent capacity grounds a presumption of freedom that must be overcome by reasons.
- **Limit**: the argument assumes reason can establish political arrangements without revelation, which some deny.

## 6.3 Political obligation
- **Thesis**: a **political obligation** is a special reason to obey the law because it is the law.
- **Argument**: four features: **pro tanto** (applies unless overridden), **comprehensively applicable** (to all laws), **universal** within the territory (to everyone living there) and **content-independent** (the reason has nothing to do with what the law says). To obey is different from to comply: complying means going along for one's own reasons (fear of prison); obeying means acting because of the authority. Not having political obligations does not remove moral obligations: you still must not murder.
- **Objections**: the question matters because the legitimacy of the state itself depends on it; if no one has political obligations, there are no legitimate governments, and relations between states change too. Classical **consent** theories say obligations arise from agreement; the **no-agreement problem**: I never signed; the classical reply: tacit consent by living peacefully in the country.
- **Reply**: tacit consent is weak (one cannot easily leave); alternatives must ground obligation on other things (general knowledge: fair play, gratitude, benefits, natural duty).
- **Limit**: only the structure of the concept and the consent route are covered; other grounds are not.

## 6.4 History as dialectic
- **Thesis (position)**: humans are historical creatures; each generation inherits ideas, institutions, tools, and tries to improve them, and this process of constant change is the dialectic.
- **Argument**: the new design of a car is better because it uses the collective experience of all earlier cars, mistakes included; the same holds for governments. Progress is "two steps forward, one back" and need not occur in every area at once. We should not treat past ages as garbage: every age has strengths and weaknesses, including ours. We are not at an endpoint; change continues. The flux of Heraclitus is the ancestor of the idea.
- **Objections**: progress is not guaranteed; the claim of a "real end" is metaphysical.
- **Reply**: the weaker reading: learn from every era, assume improvement is possible.
- **Limit**: the end towards which history moves is not covered.

## 6.5 Capitalism and its critics
- **Thesis (position)**: a critique from within: capitalism contains internal conflicts that eventually surface.
- **Argument**: the transition from feudalism displaced people from commons (through enclosure laws that turned common land into private property) into factories; nation states competed in trade; when global trade expanded (around 1968 to 1971, depending on who dates it), owners of production moved jobs to places paying less; in the critics' reading, credit and debt became normal for households from the mid-1970s, and graduation now often costs tens of thousands in debt. The tendency of competition to reduce wages is the "internal conflict". Why was it not foreseen at the founding of classical economics? Because the world was different: workers in the West were economically sheltered and could demand good conditions.
- **Objections**: capitalism's defenders point to growth and rising living standards; the debt trend may have other causes.
- **Reply**: the critique targets structure, not individuals.
- **Limit**: this is one political reading; dates and causes are contestable; do not treat as settled history.

## 6.6 Democracy: what it first meant
- **Thesis**: in the original Athenian democracy, offices were filled mostly by lot among volunteers, not by election.
- **Argument**: principle of "anyone who wishes": around 30,000 citizens could attend the assembly, about 6,000 turned up, anyone could speak, propose a law or bring a public suit; a council of 500 set the agenda; juries and magistrates were chosen by **sortition**. Elections were used only for roles needing expertise, such as generals; they were considered aristocratic (rule by the best), not democratic (rule by the many). Term limits and lottery kept classes or parties from forming. Citizenship excluded women, slaves and foreigners, so eligible people were only 10 to 20% of the population.
- **Objections**: some ancient thinkers called it anarchic and run by fools; modern representative democracies reconcile mass rule with the wisdom of experts by electing leaders.
- **Reply**: the contrast shows that "democracy" has been used for very different regimes, which is why the word cannot do argumentative work alone.
- **Limit**: figures are approximate, for the fifth century BC.

## 6.7 Social media and democracy
- **Thesis (position)**: social media affects democracy through exposure, helplessness and coordinated influence more than through isolated false stories (ch02, 2.9).
- **Argument**: online movements can raise money and awareness quickly (hashtag movements changed workplace practices) but can outgrow their capacity to organise and then fizzle or splinter, since they avoid hard questions about leadership, responsibility and delegation. Polarising content for an audience can be paid for covertly.
- **Objections**: effects on political polarisation and authoritarian thinking are unproven.
- **Reply**: study organisational structures, not just content.
- **Limit**: examples are recent and specific.

## 6.8 Tribalism as the common root of group violence
- **Thesis (position)**: religion is not the root of mass violence; the ability to attach to a tribe and exclude others is.
- **Argument**: a philosopher watched a mob murder two political leaders and wanted to post a placard calling it barbarism, but was locked in by his landlord. Religions that teach strict non-violence exist, and violence is committed in the name of country, class or any badge. If the root is tribal attachment, abolishing religion delays but does not solve the problem.
- **Objections**: religion may still amplify tribal division.
- **Reply**: focus on the mechanism that appears across cases.
- **Limit**: a handful of examples; weak as evidence.
