# Chapter 05: Ethics: Consequences, Duty, Virtue, Stoicism and Metaethics

## Core Idea
In this model, ethics has two levels: **normative** (what should be done: consequences, duties, virtues, a way of life) and **metaethical** (what moral claims are and where their authority comes from). Many disputes dissolve once it is clear which level a claim is on.

## 5.1 Utilitarianism and the greatest number
- **Thesis**: the right act is the one that produces the best consequences, usually counted as the most welfare or satisfaction summed over persons; saving five rather than one follows.
- **Argument**: utility means how well something satisfies wants or needs; it differs between people and circumstances; a market gives a rough readout (what you would pay). **Marginal utility** diminishes: the first bottle of water in a desert is worth more than any diamond, but by the tenth bottle a diamond is worth more. Opportunity cost is what you lose by giving up the alternative. This also resolves the water-and-diamonds paradox of value: exchange value and use value differ and both depend on circumstances.
- **Objections**: (a) the "better for whom?" challenge: a state of affairs is better only from someone's point of view; if you are the one, saving the five is worse for you; the five losses are five separate losses, none of which is borne by one person as a five-times loss; the numbers can be raised to billions without any single victim suffering more than one life's loss. (b) Reply: a bird's-eye view says five deaths are worse than one, so the value is "of persons", not "to persons". (c) Counter-reply: that bird's-eye ranking is still a judgment made by someone; a value from the viewpoint of nobody is hard to understand.
- **Reply**: accept the aggregate viewpoint as a moral stance, defended on its own, or accept agent-relative permissions.
- **Limit**: the objector's own position (one may save the one) seems to fail when the numbers become enormous, and intuitions diverge.

## 5.2 Consequentialism versus deontology (general frame)
- **Thesis**: consequentialism judges acts by outcomes; **deontology** judges acts by their conformity to duty or principle, whatever the result.
- **Argument**: the two positions are mapped by the question "where does moral authority come from: outcome or rule?".
- **Objections**: consequentialism can license harming an innocent person for the good of many; deontology can require a disastrous outcome to keep a rule.
- **Reply**: mixed views weigh both.
- **Limit**: the rest of this chapter takes each in turn.

## 5.3 Kant: the autonomy of the moral will
- **Thesis**: a moral code needs a firmer foundation than custom or subjective taste, and it can come only from reason that the agent exercises himself.
- **Argument**: historically the burden of morality was outsourced to a deity or a monarch. But if an agent merely obeys a commanded code, he is never fully the author of his acts: someone else's will intrudes. A slave ordered to kill is not held fully culpable, because the act is not entirely his. Responsibility connects to having a different choice: a lion killing a gazelle is not blameworthy; a person is held accountable when able to choose otherwise (and defendants with mental illness receive reduced sentences for this reason). So a genuinely free decision must rest on one's own reason, and the capacity to reason is the foundation of ethics. (General knowledge: the rule he derived is that one should act only on principles one could will as universal law.)
- **Objections**: the universal-law test is formal and may be satisfied by abhorrent maxims; pure reason may not motivate.
- **Reply**: the claim is about the source of the obligation, not a complete moral rulebook.
- **Limit**: only the first step of this view is covered.

## 5.4 Responsibility, reason and freedom
- **Thesis**: tie blame to the capacity to reason and to respond to reasons.
- **Argument**: reduced sentences for those not in their right mind, exculpation for coerced action, and the idea that "ought implies can" all point to the same capacity (ch03, 3.6).
- **Objections**: if reason is itself determined, the capacity is not freely held.
- **Reply**: the practical point is that blame tracks responsiveness to reasons, which can be tested.
- **Limit**: the metaphysical question stays open.

## 5.5 Virtue ethics: a plan for life
- **Thesis**: living well requires a plan and not merely a set of priorities; Aristotle claimed there is one best plan.
- **Argument**: priorities become a rulebook for living. The unexamined life is not worth living; Aristotle adds that an unplanned life is not worth examining. Wants differ (knowledge versus using one's brain as little as possible), but he thought that analysing them reveals one ultimate end: the argument is that one asks of each aim "for the sake of what?", and the chain ends in one final good. (General knowledge: this end is flourishing, achieved through the exercise of virtues.)
- **Objections**: the modern view is that the question of one final end has no definite answer and each person gives their own meaning; and that "better or worse ends" needs a metric.
- **Reply**: the metric is human nature, what a human being is for.
- **Limit**: depends on a view of nature that is contested.

## 5.6 Virtue in political life: a divergent meaning
- **Thesis**: virtue is not always personal patience, honesty and courage; in political thought it can mean the quality needed to succeed in a political task (see ch06, 6.1).
- **Argument**: the word is unspecific; modern readers assume the personal sense.
- **Objections**: this seems to separate virtue from morality.
- **Reply**: ask which task the virtue serves.
- **Limit**: how far it extends is unverified.

## 5.7 Stoicism
- **Thesis**: the good life is virtue, which is the correct judgement of what is and is not up to us.
- **Argument**: four ideas: (1) the **dichotomy of control**: only one's judgments are fully under one's control; (2) feelings contain judgments ("emotions have cognitive content", like the stone inside a peach), so one can examine and correct the judgment inside an emotion; being insulted is a fact, anger is a judgment about it; (3) **virtue is the only good**: wealth or good news do not help without accurate judgments; (4) **live in accordance with nature**: both align your judgments with reality and recognise that humans are social, preferring the common advantage. Four cardinal virtues: practical wisdom, temperance, justice (fair treatment even of wrongdoers) and courage (also in daily life). A web of causes and effects with a rational structure (logos) is assumed. Ancient philosophy was a design for living, not an academic discipline, a need that ritual-centred religion did not meet.
- **Objections**: the emphasis on inner control can promote passivity or accept injustice; "stoic" in popular use means unemotional, which is a distortion.
- **Reply**: the school claims the opposite: only someone who has cultivated virtue and self-control can improve others; one famous Roman author called for humane treatment of slaves at a time when the law treated them as property; the ideal is to deal with the world as it is rather than imagine an ideal society.
- **Limit**: it is a practice; the claim that "virtue is the only good" is contested; applying it to clinical anxiety is outside the philosophical claim.

## 5.8 The divine-command dilemma
- **Thesis**: morality cannot straightforwardly come from God.
- **Argument**: is something pious (good) because the gods love it, or do the gods love it because it is pious? If the second, goodness is independent of the gods, and the gods only report it; this seems to limit divine omnipotence. If the first, then the gods could tomorrow love injustice and injustice would become good, which makes goodness arbitrary.
- **Objections**: a third option: God's nature is good, and commands flow from it; goodness is neither independent nor arbitrary.
- **Reply**: this moves the question to why that nature is good.
- **Limit**: it targets a particular account of the link, not belief in God.

## 5.9 Metaethics: does objective morality need God?
- **Thesis**: three arguments link morality and religion; only one is meant to prove that God exists.
- **Argument**: (a) a society cannot be good unless enough people believe in God; (b) objective moral values exist, and could not without God, so God exists; (c) religious guidance is needed to know what is right. (a) and (c) at best show that we ought to believe in God or ought to have guidance; they do not show that the belief is true.
- **Objections**: premise (b)'s second claim assumes that duty must be a command from someone; why would it? The wrongness of an act such as rape lies in the harm to the victim, not in a divine ban. Societies' commands are unreliable; a perfect being would be a reliable source, but scripture also endorses things we reject (slavery, killing for belief, killing for same-sex acts), so as a guide it is questionable.
- **Reply**: the debate becomes whether an objective morality can rest on harm and reasons without a commander.
- **Limit**: some argue that without a transcendent source objective morality is unfounded.

## 5.10 The problem of evil
- **Thesis**: an omnipotent, omniscient and perfectly good being would prevent evil; evil exists, so no such being.
- **Argument**: the **logical** version claims a contradiction: if it is sound the being cannot exist (all-or-nothing). The **evidential** version uses probability: given the evils, the probability that God exists is less than one half, and in some versions far lower.
- **Objections**: (a) a **greater good** defence: some evils are needed for goods that outweigh them, even goods outside our knowledge; (b) the **free-will defence**: a world with free beings who can do evil is better than a world of programmed beings. Because of those replies, the logical version is considered by many unsatisfactory; the evidential version is more resilient.
- **Reply**: the evidential argument asks whether the amount and distribution of suffering is what a good being would permit.
- **Limit**: natural evil (disease, disasters) is harder for the free-will defence than moral evil.

## 5.11 Is and ought in ethics
- **Thesis**: claims about "what ought to be" that are derived from "what is" need an explicit bridge (ch01, 1.6, ch02, 2.4).
- **Argument**: in social debates, notice when a description of nature or of present practice is used as a basis for obligation; ask what premise makes the step valid.
- **Objections**: some bridges are plausible (need, harm).
- **Reply**: state the bridge, then argue for it.
- **Limit**: none of the normative theories is fully free of a bridge.
