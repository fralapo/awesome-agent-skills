# Chapter 07: Existentialism, Phenomenology and Language

## Core Idea
In this model, a line of thinkers asks **how experience and language shape what we take to be real**. Phenomenology studies the structure of experience; existentialism takes the absence of given meaning as the starting point; the philosophy of language asks how words limit or enable thought. Their shared move: look at the lens, not only at the object.

## 7.1 Phenomenology: study the lens
- **Thesis**: if objective truth is possible, it must pass through subjective human experience, so the right object of study is consciousness and its structure.
- **Argument**: philosophers repeatedly tried to correct the assumptions of the last guy by looking at the world differently; phenomenology instead looks differently at how we look. Experience filters raw phenomena through categories (space, time, cause and effect); by understanding exactly how experience distorts or shapes the raw phenomena, one hopes to subtract it and reach certainty. The method of a mathematician-turned-philosopher is motivated by certainty, not by questions about how to live.
- **Objections**: no one can step outside experience to subtract it; the current scientific method might have to change to include its own subjective filter.
- **Reply**: the aim is description of structures that every experience shares.
- **Limit**: the further claim that the scientific method would have to be replaced is a speculative extension, not argued here.

## 7.2 Absurdity and the meaning we add
- **Thesis**: much of the sense we find in things is added by habit and conditioning.
- **Argument**: a dinner is a stack of phenomena to which each of us attaches meaning; strip the learned meaning and the scene looks arbitrary. A root, a bench, a chair are seen with no usage attached, "the very paste of things": existence unveils itself as more than an abstract category. A novel of nausea describes this.
- **Objections**: the experience may be a psychological state, not a discovery about being.
- **Reply**: it is used to show that meaning is not given with things but conferred on them.
- **Limit**: only the phenomenological starting point of this author is covered; existential doctrines (freedom, bad faith, rebellion against the absurd) are not covered: omit or mark general knowledge.

## 7.3 The will as the thing in itself
- **Thesis (position)**: the inner nature of the world is not intelligible from outside but accessible from inside: it is will.
- **Argument**: earlier thinkers tried to reach things in themselves by subtracting our experience from external objects. A better route is inward: you are a body animated by a restless striving (for a meal, a car, a job; in a baby, the next bottle); the question is why the default state of a being is constant wanting. An atheist says there is no soul involved; once you get what you wanted you do not rest but want again. If the will is the inner nature of the world, then the world is not a rational plan but a blind striving (general knowledge: this leads to pessimism and to love as an expression of the will).
- **Objections**: why generalise from our own inner states to rocks and trees?
- **Reply**: it is a claim that one "secret passage" lets us look from within.
- **Limit**: speculative; the senses and intellect as products of evolution are mentioned as reasons to doubt that they depict reality.

## 7.4 True-world theories
- **Thesis (position)**: religions and philosophies often posit a "true world" opposed to this one; all share a formula.
- **Argument**: examples: a heaven for which earthly life is a test; a realm of Forms behind appearances; a spirit realm in which the individual self is a wave in an ocean. Each says: there are two realities, one inferior or illusory, one true, and one's task is to transcend appearances. The formula recurs because it answers something we dislike about this world: suffering, death, anxiety, depression. So the model reads such theories as human inventions satisfying psychological needs. Utopias of any political colour have the same structure.
- **Objections**: recurrence could be evidence of a real thing; the genetic fallacy.
- **Reply**: the proper conclusion is to scrutinise the theory; the genealogical argument does not refute it (ch01, 1.5).
- **Limit**: this does not tell which parts of any such doctrine are true.

## 7.5 Philosophy and religion as responses to death
- **Thesis (position)**: both address fear of irreversibility: anything bad that cannot be undone.
- **Argument**: unlike other animals, humans look forward and backward, and worry about losing a limb, a partner or the future. Religion says that death is an illusion or part of a providential plan; the philosopher says that a person can overcome the fear by using only human faculties to look at oneself objectively, "salvation without religion". So the two differ in method; they share the problem.
- **Objections**: the Greek schools were also doctrines with metaphysical commitments; some university curricula omit medieval religious thought altogether; the model holds that Christian ethics made notable contributions.
- **Reply**: keep the categories distinct but read both.
- **Limit**: a general account of motive, not an argument for or against either.

## 7.6 Logic and the limits of what can be said
- **Thesis**: mathematics gives certainty about objects that do not exist physically; so philosophers tried to copy it for things that do.
- **Argument**: the number three is nowhere in the world, yet we can say with certainty that 1 plus 1 is 2. Formal logic does the same for thought: it gives parameters so that thinking is clear. Unlike mathematics, logic can be applied directly to thought and ordinary statements. This is the idea behind a young logician's early project: use logic to find the structure of language and thought. (General knowledge: this led to the view that what cannot be put in logical form cannot be said clearly; this chapter stops earlier.)
- **Objections**: ordinary language is messier than logic.
- **Reply**: the early project is to find what any possible language must share.
- **Limit**: only the starting motivation is covered; later changes of view are not.

## 7.7 Language, thought and power
- **Thesis**: words limit the thoughts that are available.
- **Argument**: in a fictional dictatorship, the official language's dictionary gets thinner, not fatter: removing words shrinks the range of thought, until "thoughtcrime" is impossible for lack of words. A common misreading takes the novel to be about redefining words. So analysing the hidden assumptions behind a concept (race, law, justice, democracy) is not a move of the same kind: it adds scrutiny. Epistemic injustice (ch02, 2.8) has a second kind in which prejudice blocks people from accessing concepts needed to make sense of their own experience.
- **Objections**: critics say that exposing assumptions behind a word is itself an attempt to change its meaning.
- **Reply**: the difference is between adding questions and removing the means of asking.
- **Limit**: a sound analysis can still be wrong; judge it on its premises.

## 7.8 Phenomenological description as a method
- **Thesis (position)**: phenomenological descriptions are useful as a **method** to check what your own concepts add to an experience, and as a **warning** against treating learned meanings as discoveries.
- **Argument**: ask, of any scene: what in this is given and what is conferred by habit?
- **Objections**: this may undermine all meaning.
- **Reply**: the exercise targets unexamined defaults, not meaning as such.
- **Limit**: the exercise is not an argument.
