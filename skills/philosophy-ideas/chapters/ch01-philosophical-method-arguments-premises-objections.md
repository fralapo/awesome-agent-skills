# Chapter 01: Philosophical Method: Arguments, Premises, Objections

## Core Idea
In this model, philosophy is the practice of **making a claim answerable to reasons**: state a thesis, give the argument that supports it, expose the premises, face the strongest objection, and say what the reply costs. Every later chapter uses the same five fields: **Thesis, Argument, Objections, Reply, Limit**. Where a position is contested it is marked **(position)**.

## 1.1 Argument, premise, conclusion
- **Thesis**: an argument is a set of premises offered so that a conclusion follows; what is testable is the link and each premise, not the person arguing.
- **Argument**: validity asks whether the conclusion follows if the premises are true; soundness adds that the premises are true. Formal logic gives "parameters for thinking": if all A are B and some B are C, one cannot conclude that some A are C by form alone; the form itself is certain even when no physical object corresponds to it.
- **Objections**: a valid argument can be useless (circular or tautological); a sound one can be irrelevant to the question asked.
- **Reply**: always attack the weakest premise, not the conclusion; ask what the argument would need to be true.
- **Limit**: certainty reached in logic or arithmetic concerns abstract structures; carrying that certainty to physical things is exactly what is contested (see ch02, ch07).

## 1.2 The dilemma and the regress
- **Thesis**: two forms recur in this material: the **dilemma** (two options, both costly) and the **regress** (each answer demands another).
- **Argument**: the dilemma form asks "is X so because of Y, or is Y so because of X?" and shows that either branch gives up something the opponent wanted (the divine-command dilemma, ch05). The regress form asks what moved the thing that moved the stick and concludes that the chain must stop somewhere (the first-mover argument, ch03) or that it never stops (an alternative, usually unexamined).
- **Objections**: a dilemma may be false (a third option exists); a regress may be harmless if explanation is not required to bottom out.
- **Reply**: before accepting the force of a dilemma, search for the third option; before accepting a regress argument, ask why a stopping point is demanded.
- **Limit**: "obviously the chain must stop" is itself a premise (see also 1.7).

## 1.3 Thought experiments
- **Thesis**: a thought experiment isolates one feature by removing the rest of the world, to test an intuition.
- **Argument**: the floating or "flying man" (a person created fully formed with no sensory input still knowing that he exists) is meant to separate self-awareness from the body (ch04). The colour-scientist case (someone knows all the physics of colour but has never seen it) is meant to show that physical facts leave something out (ch04). A demon that knows all positions and forces is meant to dramatise determinism (ch03). A hotel with infinitely many rooms dramatises counting infinity (ch08, 8.8).
- **Objections**: the intuition may be unreliable outside ordinary conditions; the scenario may smuggle in the conclusion. Example from ch04: we may not know "what it is like" to see red as much as we assume once the experience has passed.
- **Reply**: restate the scenario with different details; check whether the verdict survives.
- **Limit**: an experiment shows what an intuition says, not what is true.

## 1.4 Relativism and the self-undermining test
- **Thesis (position)**: relativism (truth and value are only what a person or society holds) is the classical target for the **self-application test**.
- **Argument**: the sophists treated persuasion as skill and held that "man is the measure of all things", so what is true or right is relative to the judge; arguments have two sides and the better persuader wins.
- **Objections**: if everyone is right, no one can be wiser than another; why would anyone pay to be taught? What of those who think everyone else is wrong: are they right too? The reply from the sophists: they teach not truth but useful knowledge, as in a court.
- **Reply**: the objection forced later thinkers to ground morals on something more stable than a single opinion. The sophist contribution is positive: any serious position must survive their style of attack.
- **Limit**: pointing to a self-defeating form refutes an extreme version, not the milder claim that some judgments depend on context.

## 1.5 Genealogy of a belief (what is the belief for?)
- **Thesis (position)**: asking what psychological need a doctrine satisfies is a legitimate move, but it does not by itself decide truth.
- **Argument**: heaven, the realm of Forms and the "true spirit world" share a formula: this world is inferior or illusory; transcend it to reach the true one. The model reads that recurrence as evidence that such doctrines answer a need rather than report a discovery. The same pattern could be found in political utopias.
- **Objections**: the explanation of why someone believes does not show the belief false (genetic fallacy); many independent people arriving at similar ideas may be evidence for them.
- **Reply**: use genealogy to prompt scrutiny of the premises, then evaluate the premises on their own.
- **Limit**: it is a lever for suspicion, not a refutation.

## 1.6 Is and ought
- **Thesis**: a description of how things are does not by itself license a conclusion about how they ought to be; reasoners often slide from one to the other without saying so.
- **Argument**: examples: "the universe is this way, so it ought to be"; "nature does X, so X is good". Reason can tell us what follows from ends but does not by itself pick the ends (the remark that preferring ruin to a scratched finger is not contrary to reason shows the point).
- **Objections**: hidden bridging premises may be good ones; some thinkers argue that facts about human nature support values.
- **Reply**: demand the bridge premise in plain words, then judge it.
- **Limit**: the gap constrains what science alone can settle (ch08), it does not make moral reasoning arbitrary.

## 1.7 Hidden defaults and the age's alternatives
- **Thesis**: an inference that looks obvious in one age may reflect the lack of alternatives.
- **Argument**: the first-mover argument concludes that something unmoved started motion and then names it God. The model notes that the conclusion is compatible with other readings, for example a physical initial state; the step to "God" is the added premise.
- **Reply**: for each "it follows that", look for what else would also follow.
- **Limit**: noticing a missing alternative does not make the original argument false.

## 1.8 Reasoning under counting and probability
- **Thesis**: intuition fails with nonlinear counting, so some arguments must be checked by calculation.
- **Argument**: the birthday problem: a group of 23 has a 50.73% chance of a shared birthday because the number of pairs grows with the square of the group (253 pairs for 23 people); the trick is to compute the chance of no match and subtract from 100%. The infinite-hotel paradox shows that infinite sets behave differently from finite ones: moving each guest from room n to n+1, or to 2n, frees rooms without adding any (general knowledge: this is the idea of countable infinity).
- **Objections**: the "surprising" result is only surprising under a linear intuition.
- **Reply**: when a probability claim feels wrong, flip the question and count.
- **Limit**: the numbers assume equal likelihoods and no twins; real data may differ.

## 1.9 The Socratic method and the unexamined life
- **Thesis**: the Socratic method asks questions that expose a definition's hidden commitments instead of asserting a thesis.
- **Argument**: asked to define piety, the interlocutor offers examples; the questioner asks for the thing itself, then tests each definition (ch05, 5.8). Plato's dialogues stage this and sometimes put his own later views in the mouth of the teacher.
- **Objections**: the method can leave only aporia (no answer); and it is hard to separate the teacher's views from the pupil's presentation.
- **Reply**: treat each failed definition as progress: it rules out an option.
- **Limit**: a dialogue is a stylised argument, not evidence about what the historical person said.
