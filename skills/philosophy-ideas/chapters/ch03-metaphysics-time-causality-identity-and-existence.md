# Chapter 03: Metaphysics: Time, Causality, Identity and Existence

## Core Idea
In this model, metaphysics asks **what there is and how it is structured**. The recurring questions are what time is, whether the past causes the present necessarily, what makes a person the same person, whether anything could have been nothing, and whether a first cause or a necessary being is needed.

## 3.1 A-theory and B-theory of time
- **Thesis**: there are two main views of time. **A-theory** (tensed): the present is privileged, the future is open, time passes. **B-theory** (tenseless): past, present and future events are all equally real and ordered only by "earlier than" and "later than".
- **Argument**: A-theory matches the ordinary intuition and is a realist reading; B-theory treats the felt "now" as perspective, like the word "here". Each view has echoes in physics and in theology (a God outside time or within time).
- **Objections**: A-theory struggles to say how fast the present moves and conflicts with the physical picture that "now" depends on the observer (general knowledge: relativity of simultaneity); B-theory struggles to explain why the passage of time is so vivid.
- **Reply**: the views are best treated as complementary probes of one concept (philosophy, physics, psychology, neuroscience, theology, and the implications for free will), not a contest that one field wins.
- **Limit**: a map of ontologies of time is a survey, not a solution.

## 3.2 Time in physics: space as an evolving structure
- **Thesis (position)**: time may be a process of rewriting an underlying structure.
- **Argument**: matter was found to be discrete (molecules, photons); by analogy, space may be built from indivisible "atoms of space" known only by their relations, represented as a **hypergraph** (nodes linked by edges that can join more than two nodes). Time is the progressive application of rewriting rules to that structure. Everything in the universe, including particles, would be features of the structure, like an eddy in a fluid.
- **Objections**: a representation is a model; is the model the thing or an approximation? The reply given: here the claim is that this is the whole story, not an approximation to something deeper.
- **Reply**: test it by deriving known physics from it.
- **Limit**: it remains a proposal; flag it as speculative.

## 3.3 Causality and determinism
- **Thesis**: every event has sufficient causes, so given the causes the effect had to occur, and before them it could not.
- **Argument**: a leaf falls only when sap, wind and other factors are in place. A demon that knew all positions and forces could predict everything, including human action. Our brains are physical, so they follow the same laws; each state is fixed by the preceding one back to the start.
- **Objections**: quantum events seem to be uncaused; the reply is that randomness does not equal freedom (3.5). A non-physical mind would escape causation, but then how does it interact? In practice prediction is impossible because it would require knowing boundary conditions at every level (ch08, 8.2).
- **Reply**: determinism is a claim about laws, not about our ability to predict.
- **Limit**: whether the world is deterministic is an empirical matter; the philosophical argument assumes it.

## 3.4 Potentiality, actuality and the first mover
- **Thesis**: change is the realisation of a potential; whatever changes needs a cause that is already actual.
- **Argument**: wood can become fire but does so only when something acts on it; a stick moves only if a hand moves it. Ask what moved the hand, and so on; the chain cannot go on forever, so there is a first mover that is itself unmoved. "Motion" includes any change, not just location. Aquinas argued that this unmoved mover is God, and read Aristotle as compatible with Christian doctrine.
- **Objections**: nothing shows that the first mover is a person, only that something started motion; the modern picture of an early universe with evenly spread matter suggests a physical start without an agent. Also, the argument assumes the regress must end.
- **Reply**: the argument shows a need for a first cause, and any further identification is another step (ch01, 1.7).
- **Limit**: it relies on a physics of motion that was later revised.

## 3.5 Free will: the structure of the problem
- **Thesis**: whether a person acts freely depends on what "free" means and on what the world is like.
- **Argument**: the common meaning is "could have acted otherwise if the clock were rewound": every atom the same, and yet a different jacket chosen. A **dilemma of determination**: any act is either determined or not. If determined, the determiner is inside you or outside you. If outside, you do not control it. If inside, ask whether it was itself determined; follow the chain: it ends outside you or in something undetermined, which is randomness. Either way, you are not the author. Partial determination (80% likely) does not help: ask why this time and not that time.
- **Objections**: this uses a strict notion of control; **compatibilism** says that I am free if nothing coerced me (no gun at my head); **libertarian** views hold that I could have done otherwise.
- **Reply**: free will may not be what matters; two concepts of liberty (freedom from external obstruction and freedom to act on one's own considered will) suggest that an addicted person who turns toward the cigarette shop and misses a flight was not free in the second sense even if not blocked in the first.
- **Limit**: the argument shows one concept fails; other concepts remain.

## 3.6 Why free will matters: ought implies can
- **Thesis**: if you morally ought to do something, you must be able to do it.
- **Argument**: if determinism holds, nobody could have done otherwise, so the practice of moral condemnation loses its footing; punishment might stay as prevention but not as blame.
- **Objections**: compatibilists keep blame by changing what "could" means; a diminished-responsibility practice already tracks degrees of autonomy (mental illness is treated as reduced choice).
- **Reply**: tie responsibility to the capacity to reason (ch05, 5.4).
- **Limit**: it links ch03 to ch05; neither settles the other.

## 3.7 Agency and imagination
- **Thesis (position)**: free will is the capacity to envision futures and to choose among them; agency needs three mental states: a belief in efficacy, optimism and imagination.
- **Argument**: whether those beliefs are conscious depends on a theory of consciousness; free will in turn depends on that theory (ch04). A distinction used: awareness itself (phenomenal) versus the content of awareness (access). Much of what we do is done without phenomenal consciousness; consciousness arises when we get into trouble, like a stone in the shoe.
- **Objections**: if the capacity to imagine is itself caused, then 3.5 reappears.
- **Reply**: the position is that freedom is a capacity of a type of system, not an escape from causation.
- **Limit**: unverified whether the capacity is defended against the determinism argument or only defined.

## 3.8 Identity and the self
- **Thesis**: "what am I?" has several candidate answers: the body only; a mind controlling a body; a pattern; a stage in a cumulative project.
- **Argument**: if a person can choose to alter their own insecurities with a drug, then self-creation is part of what a person does; the "authentic self" is not simply given. A picture of a brain is not a brain; so mapping neurons is not duplicating a mind; if minds are not software, uploading fails (ch04, 4.7).
- **Objections**: a pattern view makes copies equal to originals.
- **Reply**: ask which properties the self needs to keep (memory, character, body continuity).
- **Limit**: the thought experiments conflict; none is decisive.

## 3.9 Why something rather than nothing
- **Thesis (position)**: the deepest question is not whether God exists or what consciousness is, but why there is anything at all.
- **Argument**: the question presupposes that there could have been nothing. Possible answers: (a) a necessary being exists; (b) existence exists by definition (tautological but not trivial); (c) the question is a quirk of language; (d) nothing is unstable and gives rise to something. Aquinas's version: for anything with an essence (a horse), essence and existence are distinct, so its existence comes from something else; there must be a being whose essence is existence. That being is called God.
- **Objections**: the quantum vacuum is not nothing, since empty space still has energy; "nothing" needs levels (no matter, no space and time, no laws, no possibility).
- **Reply**: define the level of nothing you mean before arguing from physics.
- **Limit**: a necessary being could be physical or abstract; "must be" may not equal "God".
