# Chapter 02: Epistemology: Knowledge, Testimony and Misinformation

## Core Idea
In this model, epistemology asks **what entitles a person to a belief**, and how the answer changes for ordinary testimony, experts, the senses, reason and large information systems. The split between reason and experience, the problem of trusting others and the problem of false information are treated as one subject.

## 2.1 Rationalism versus empiricism
- **Thesis**: two premises organise early modern philosophy: knowledge can be reached by reason alone (rationalism) or must come through sense experience (empiricism).
- **Argument**: the continental rationalists are named as Descartes, Spinoza and Leibniz; the British empiricists as Locke, Berkeley and Hume. Kant is placed either as outside the divide or as its climax, depending on the historian, and is where the line of argument arrives either way.
- **Objections**: the tidy labels hide the fact that each thinker used both; the divide is a teaching device.
- **Reply**: use it as a map of premises ("what counts as evidence?"), not as a classification of persons.
- **Limit**: some philosophers belong to neither camp.

## 2.2 Certainty and the radical start
- **Thesis**: an age of conflicting authorities made some thinkers look for a start that could not be doubted.
- **Argument**: most people hold simplified black-and-white convictions to mask uncertainty; the quoted warning is that nothing is so firmly believed as what we least know. Descartes responds by refusing to accept inherited views until tested (general knowledge: the method of doubt and the self-evident "I think").
- **Objections**: doubt may be unlivable; certainty may be bought by narrowing what is asked.
- **Reply**: the aim is a trustworthy base, not permanent doubt.
- **Limit**: even if a first certainty is secured, the route from it to the external world remains contested.

## 2.3 Kant: the mind contributes the frame
- **Thesis**: experience is built from raw sensory phenomena filtered through mental categories such as space, time and cause and effect.
- **Argument**: without those categories, experience would be a chaotic flurry; so what we call the world is already shaped by the knower. The "thing in itself" lies outside what we can access.
- **Objections**: if the thing in itself cannot be known, how can it be said to exist or to cause experience? Two later answers appear in this material: turn inward (the will, ch07) and study the structure of experience itself (phenomenology, ch07).
- **Reply**: the framework is a claim about the limits of knowledge, not a refusal of science.
- **Limit**: it leaves open how much of the frame is universal and how much is learned.

## 2.4 Hume's two limits
- **Thesis**: statements about what is do not entail what ought to be (ch01, 1.6); science, which aims at an increasingly accurate view of what is, inherits that limit.
- **Argument**: even a science that explained nearly everything about the world would still be silent on value; that is a feature of what it is, because everything has limits and the limits give a thing its meaning. (General knowledge: the same author is associated with the problem of induction, which is not developed in this material.)
- **Objections**: perhaps values are natural facts after all (ch05, 5.9).
- **Reply**: the claim is about inference, not about whether anyone should care.
- **Limit**: it does not say the sciences are weak; it says which question they answer.

## 2.5 Testimony and trust
- **Thesis**: most of what a person knows comes from being told.
- **Argument**: the **vulnerability problem**: when you accept an ordinary claim ("the pub is on South Street") you trust that the informant both knows and is honestly reporting. **Reductionists** hold that you need independent evidence that the informant is reliable. **Anti-reductionists** hold that testimony is a basic source: accept what is presented as true and intelligible unless there are stronger reasons not to (an **acceptance principle**). One defence: understanding a rational speaker is a reason to think they are tracking truth. A second: a presumption of truth is built into the practice of testimony, since a society in which all assertions were false could not have testimony at all.
- **Objections**: reason guides what follows from premises, not truth, so rationality alone cannot ground trust; and the reductionist's required evidence (is the informant reliable?) itself mostly arrives by testimony, so the demand is regressive.
- **Reply**: standards vary by context: a casual question needs little, a murder trial more.
- **Limit**: it does not tell you how to detect a lying or confused source; for that see 2.6 and 2.7.

## 2.6 Experts and institutions
- **Thesis (position)**: expert knowledge is real but is produced inside institutions with incentives, politics and naming conventions that can distort it.
- **Argument**: the history of a newly recognised epidemic is used: a name tied to one group (the first label stuck to a sexual minority) shaped who felt at risk and which research was funded; a rushed announcement favoured a national claim to credit; a contaminated sample or a stolen one remained unresolved (which one is unverified). Experts were the best placed to know and still shaped the outcome by labels, nationalism and prestige.
- **Objections**: a few failures do not justify blanket distrust; the way these errors were later corrected was also expert work.
- **Reply**: ask of an expert claim: what is the track record, who benefits, how is it checked by others, what is the name hiding?
- **Limit**: scepticism of authority can become a way to avoid all knowledge; the aim is calibration.

## 2.7 Misconceptions and misinformation
- **Thesis**: a misconception is a belief widely held and largely wrong; it spreads through ordinary mistakes more often than through lies.
- **Argument**: **misinformation** (unintentionally false or misleading) versus **disinformation** (intentionally designed to mislead). Example: a tongue-taste map that divided the tongue into four exclusive zones; the original study found only small intensity differences, in a language few could read, and each retelling and each simplified image distorted it further (a game of telephone).
- **Objections**: if intent cannot be proven, the line between the two is blurry.
- **Reply**: treat the question "what did the original actually say, and who simplified it?" as the first check.
- **Limit**: tracing a source is slow; use it for claims that matter.

## 2.8 Epistemic injustice
- **Thesis (position)**: denying someone the ability to give or receive knowledge on prejudicial grounds is a wrong done to them specifically as a knower.
- **Argument**: picture a pool of shared knowledge. **Testimonial injustice**: blocking someone from adding to the pool (a jury discounts a witness because of prejudice, raising the evidence bar for him). **The other kind** blocks someone from borrowing from the pool: language lacks the concepts to describe their experience (also see ch07, 7.7).
- **Objections**: calibrating credibility is legitimate; it is not always prejudice.
- **Reply**: ask whether the bar was raised only for this person or this group.
- **Limit**: the wrong is separate from its consequences (a wrongful conviction is a further harm).

## 2.9 Information systems and democracy
- **Thesis (position)**: for societies, the main information risk is not isolated false claims but the structure that shapes exposure.
- **Argument**: people sort themselves into communities that fulfil psychological needs (**selective exposure**); flooding the space with contradictory material pushes people to give up judging (**learned helplessness**); coordinated **illicit influence campaigns** need assets (sites, accounts, paid voices, money, coordination), and those are easier to target than lies. One government's staff were estimated to produce hundreds of millions of posts a year, mostly positive and factual, timed to dilute negative sentiment (a figure from a leak, dated and uncertain).
- **Objections**: the long-term effect of selective exposure on polarisation is unproven; the evidence is correlational.
- **Reply**: aim at campaigns at source rather than at individual lies.
- **Limit**: fact-checking does not move people who consume content for belonging, not truth.

## 2.10 Memory as a knower's weakness
- **Thesis**: a memory is a physical trace that is strengthened or weakened, not a recording; treat vivid recall as testimony from your past self.
- **Argument**: experience becomes electrical activity, passes through short-term memory to long-term storage via the hippocampus and several regions; repeated communication between two neurons raises its efficiency (long-term potentiation). Encoding is strongest under attention, deep engagement and meaning; chronic stress, depression and isolation interfere. Synapses weaken with age; one figure given: the hippocampus loses about 5% of its neurons per decade (dated, from a short educational summary).
- **Objections**: loss of detail is not loss of gist.
- **Reply**: for claims that matter, find a second record. (General knowledge: recall is partly reconstructive.)
- **Limit**: the figures are population averages.
