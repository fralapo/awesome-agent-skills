# Chapter 07: Pension Products and Retirement

> Educational concepts, not investment, tax or legal advice. Figures and rules are snapshots (Italy, 2024-2025, year stated), to be re-checked against the rule in force.

## Core Idea
In this model, Italian retirement has three layers: the public contributory pension ("INPS"), supplementary pension products (collective and open pension funds, "fondi pensione"; individual insurance plans, "PIP"), and the accrued end-of-service benefit ("TFR"). Each has its own tax and its own exit rules. The decisions that matter are the **marginal tax rate on the deduction**, the **employer match**, the **cost drag**, and the **exit conditions**, not the product label.

## 7.1 Deduction versus net cost
- **Thesis**: a tax-deductible contribution must be compared with the same pre-tax money invested outside, not with the "refund".
- **Mechanism**: for a marginal rate m, one unit of gross income becomes (1 - m) outside the wrapper and 1 inside. Through payroll the taxable income falls immediately; if paid personally and deducted later, the refund comes later but the net cost is the same. Extra effects exist (employment-income allowances scale with taxable income). The saving is a deferred, reduced tax, not a gift.
- **Worked example**: m = 40%: contributing 5,000 reduces net pay by 3,000 while 5,000 enters the fund; outside, only 3,000 could be invested. Italy, snapshot 2025: marginal rates 23% / 35% / 43%; annual deduction cap 5,164.57 euro including employer contribution.
- **Does not hold when**: the saver has no tax to deduct (flat regime, income fully offset by allowances), so m is effectively zero.

## 7.2 Break-even: fund versus self-managed ETF
- **Thesis**: the fund usually wins at short and medium horizons and with employer matching; a very good self-investor with a long horizon and a low marginal rate can overtake it.
- **Mechanism**: the fund has pre-tax contribution, yearly tax on returns (20%, 12.5% on government bonds) and an exit tax of 15% falling to 9% after 35 years of participation, with no stamp duty. The ETF has post-tax contribution, 26% on gains at sale, equity returns about 7-8% against a fund's 4-5% net. The deduction creates an initial advantage that the ETF's higher return needs years to erase.
- **Worked example (Italy, snapshot 2025)**: marginal rate 35%: ETF overtakes after about 30-35 years; 43%: about 37-39 years; 23%: about 13 years (the fund is not worth it). A very good investor (8% gross, 26% tax on gains) needs about 38 years to beat a fund compounding 4-4.5% net after deduction.
- **Does not hold when**: employer match or TFR accrual exists (the fund then wins almost always), or no income tax can be reduced.

## 7.3 Employer contribution and TFR
- **Thesis**: an employer match is an immediate return no investment can replicate; the TFR fares better in a fund than in the company in many cases.
- **Mechanism**: if the employer adds x% only when the worker contributes y% to the collective fund, the marginal return on the first y% is about 100% instantly. TFR left in the company is revalued at 1.5% + 75% of inflation, with a 17% tax on the revaluation; it is taxed on exit at the average rate of recent years and often paid late. In the fund it follows the fund's return and is taxed at 9-15%.
- **Worked example**: revaluation at 2% inflation: (1.5% + 0.75 x 2%) x 0.83 = about 2.5% net; at 3% inflation about 3.1% net. A 1% match on a 30,000 salary = 300 euro a year.
- **Trade-off**: contributing the accrual ties it to that fund's costs and line choices; some employers may use the match as an argument against raises (position).
- **Does not hold when**: the employer has no accrual (some public or special employers) or the fund is very costly.

## 7.4 Line choice by horizon, not by temperament
- **Thesis**: choose the line by the date money is needed; a guaranteed line is a poor choice beyond a few years.
- **Mechanism**: annualised return = (final/initial)^(1/n) - 1 (not return divided by n; 1.21 over two years is 10% a year, not 10.5%). Over 1-2 years an equity line can lose 10%; over 5 years it is rarely negative; over 20 years equity lines are positive while a guaranteed or money-market line earns about inflation or less. Guarantees trigger only on specific exit events and cost extra in fees.
- **Worked example**: equity line versus guaranteed line over 18 years: about 2.4x versus 1.3x.
- **Does not hold when**: money is needed within 1-3 years or the fund offers no equity line.

## 7.5 Cost drag
- **Thesis**: judge costs relative to expected return, not capital: 2% a year on a 5% expected return takes about 40% of the return.
- **Mechanism**: costs are paid even in loss years. Collective funds often cost close to index funds (0.2-1%); bank-sold funds 2-4%. Fixed per-contribution fees hurt small contributions. Tiny absolute savings (10 euro on 10,000, about 76 euro over 30 years at 7%) rarely justify effort or errors.
- **Does not hold when**: the cost buys something real (a guarantee, access) that you value.

## 7.6 Exit routes and annuitisation
- **Thesis**: a pension fund is not a trap; there are many legal exits, each with its tax and conditions.
- **Mechanism**: benefit at retirement (staying in and continuing to contribute is allowed); transfer after a minimum period (keeping the first-membership date); full withdrawal on loss of requirements (higher tax); partial withdrawal after long unemployment (reduced tax); advances for health costs (reduced tax) or home purchase and for any reason up to 30% (flat higher tax after 8 years). A threshold on annuitisation (about 100,000 per fund; Italy, snapshot 2025): below it the benefit can be taken as capital; above it a minimum share must be annuitised. Because the threshold is per fund, several funds lower each balance; advances and bridge annuity reduce the balance before the test.
- **Worked example (Italy, snapshot 2025)**: 30% advance taxed 23%; health advance up to 75% taxed 9-15%.
- **Does not hold when**: rules change (they do often) or the fund's regulations are stricter.

## 7.7 Bridge annuity ("RITA")
- **Thesis**: a temporary annuity from a pension account bridges the years before public pension age but consumes the account.
- **Mechanism**: monthly payment = (balance x share requested)/(years of annuity x 12), ignoring revaluation; taxed at 15% falling to 9% on the deducted part; non-deducted contributions are not retaxed. Eligibility needs cessation of work and either 5 years to old-age pension (with 20 contribution years and 5 years in the fund) or 24 months of inactivity and 10 years to old-age pension.
- **Worked example**: balance 95,300, 70% requested over 108 months = about 617 gross, 556 net per month.

## 7.8 Life annuity pricing
- **Thesis**: an annuity's value depends on the mortality table, the technical rate, expense loading and options; only the pure single-life, zero-technical-rate, annual-payment version is comparable.
- **Mechanism**: theoretical coefficient = 1/(remaining life expectancy + safety years); with loading L it becomes (1 - L)/expectancy. More frequent payment lowers the coefficient (about -1.3% for semiannual versus annual). Unisex tables transfer value from men to women. Reversibility, guaranteed period and return-of-premium all lower the payment.
- **Worked example**: male at 70: life expectancy 15.8 (+1 = 16.8): coefficient 5.94%; with 1.3% loading 5.875%; a provider paying 5.237% gives up about 11% of value.
- **Does not hold when**: the person has reduced life expectancy or the table is outdated.

## 7.9 The public contributory pension
- **Thesis**: under a contributory system the pension is balance x transformation coefficient, not a share of the last salary.
- **Mechanism**: balance = contributions revalued by average nominal GDP growth (the revaluation cannot be negative: coefficient at least 1, i.e. floor 0%). The coefficient rises with age (about 4.5% at 60 to 6.2% at 70 Italy, snapshot 2024). Gates (Italy, snapshot 2024): old-age pension at 67 with 20 contribution years and an amount at least equal to the social allowance; at 71 with 5 years; early retirement by seniority or contributory early at 64 with 20 years and an amount at least three times the social allowance. Gates are step functions, so being just below one can justify extra contributions.
- **Worked example**: 300,000 x 5.772% (age 68) = 17,316 gross a year.
- **Does not hold when**: pre-1996 contributions exist (earnings-related method).

## 7.10 Buying back study years
Redeeming university years rarely pays under the contributory system; it pays if a threshold is within reach. Three levers: years to retirement (shorter favours it), alternative return (lower favours it), the gap between marginal rate now and rate in retirement (larger favours it). Example: outlay 24,308, 8 years, 35% now, 23% in retirement, alternative 4%: redeeming ~31,900 versus ~33,300 not redeeming.

## 7.11 Insurance-wrapped products
A wrapper stacks costs (entry load, insurance charge, fund fee, surrender penalty): 100 euro a month, 4% gross, 1% fee, 12.5% tax, 1.5% load, 2.7% insurance charge gives net IRR about 2.1%. Life-premium deduction is capped (530 euro, at 19%), so max benefit about 100 euro a year; 2.5% load on 10,000 costs 250 immediately.

## Common mistakes
- Judging the fund by the refund instead of the gross-up.
- Choosing a guaranteed line for a 20-year horizon.
- Ignoring the per-fund annuitisation threshold when splitting.
