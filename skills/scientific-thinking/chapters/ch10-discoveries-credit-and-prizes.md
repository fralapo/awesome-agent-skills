# Chapter 10: Discoveries, Credit and Prizes

## Core Idea
In this model, discoveries are produced by **persistence in a neglected corner, tool-building and removing one obstacle at a time**, inside organisations whose incentives rarely match the value of the result. Credit, money and recognition are allocated unevenly, and powerful inventions are used for purposes their inventors did not choose.

## 10.1 The contrarian bet
- **Thesis**: a major advance can come from working on the unfashionable candidate when the crowd is on the other.
- **Mechanism**: for three decades large companies chased a blue light-emitting diode, without which white LED lighting was impossible. The field concentrated on one material that looked more promising on paper (a smaller crystal-lattice mismatch with its substrate and fewer defects); the other candidate had a large mismatch, high defect density, no known p-type doping and output a hundred times too weak. At a major conference, talks on the favoured material drew hundreds of attendees, the other about five. The person who succeeded chose the neglected material partly because competition was lower, and succeeded.
- **Trade-off**: neglect is usually a signal of real difficulty, and most contrarian bets fail.
- **Does not hold when**: you can find no reason the crowd could be wrong beyond fashion; then following the crowd is wiser.

## 10.2 Remove obstacles one by one
- **Thesis**: breaking a hard problem into three independent obstacles and solving each turns a "dead end" into a path.
- **Mechanism**: the three obstacles were (1) growing a defect-free crystal, (2) making p-type material, (3) raising efficiency by orders of magnitude. Solutions included a second gas flow to pin reactants to the substrate, a buffer layer, heating the doped crystal to drive out hydrogen that was neutralising the p-type dopant (the cause of the earlier failure), and inserting a thin indium-containing "well" with a barrier layer to keep electrons confined. A light output 1,500 microwatts versus the 1,000 needed then made it commercial. Within a few years blue LEDs were produced by the million each month, then white by adding a phosphor.
- **Trade-off**: each fix generates new constraints (the well that traps electrons also leaked them).
- **Does not hold when**: the problems interact so that fixing one breaks another.

## 10.3 Build your own instruments
- **Thesis**: control of the apparatus can be the decisive advantage.
- **Mechanism**: the successful experimenter spent most of a year assembling a growth reactor from scratch, then rebuilt his company's reactor, working daily to modify it and test, which gave a customised tool that rivals could not buy. Brute-force adjustment (pumping more of an element than "should" stick) worked because he could change the machine. Others limited to commercial equipment were stuck on the same step.
- **Trade-off**: bespoke instruments lack standardisation and reproducibility.
- **Does not hold when**: the tool is commodity and the bottleneck is elsewhere.

## 10.4 Ignore the order when your own judgment is better, with caution
- **Thesis**: defying management instructions can be decisive, and is survivorship-biased.
- **Mechanism**: the inventor ignored repeated orders to stop and to turn a prototype into a product. The stories we hear are the ones where that worked. The base rate for ignoring management and being right is low; the policy takeaway is for organisations to protect a small number of high-risk bets with clear stopping rules.
- **Trade-off**: tolerating mavericks costs money.
- **Does not hold when**: safety or legal constraints are involved.

## 10.5 Credit and compensation
- **Thesis**: those who create value are often not those who capture it.
- **Mechanism**: the company's revenue roughly quadrupled over several years on the invention, yet the employee-inventor received a token bonus for the patent and later settled a long legal dispute for a sum that mostly covered his legal costs. The main science prize for this work was shared by three people, even though the result depended on many contributors: a competing group working on the same material had solved the p-type step first, and the winning route combined their idea with a simpler, scalable variant.
- **Trade-off**: simple rules for assigning credit are easy to apply; more accurate ones are expensive.
- **Does not hold when**: contracts specify sharing.

## 10.6 Dual use and unintended consequences
- **Thesis**: making a dangerous thing manageable can multiply its use, including bad uses.
- **Mechanism**: a violently unstable liquid explosive killed many workers. The inventor of a safer form worked step by step: a reliable detonator (a small explosion triggers a large one, a principle later reused in nuclear devices); then a solid carrier (a porous earth that absorbs the liquid so tiny gas bubbles cannot be compressed into hot spots and collapse by cavitation); then a gel made with nitrocellulose that resists leaking and is more powerful; then a smokeless propellant. The same materials built tunnels, canals and railways, and also enabled bombings and acts of terrorism. The stated hope that terrible weapons would make war impossible has a poor track record.
- **Trade-off**: safety work lowers accidents among users while expanding the scale of misuse.
- **Does not hold when**: access to the technology is controlled.

## 10.7 Prizes as legacy management
- **Thesis**: a prize can be an institution for shaping how a person and a field are remembered.
- **Mechanism**: the story told is that a premature obituary labelled the inventor a "merchant of death" (the real obituary language was probably milder), after which he left most of his fortune to fund annual prizes for contributions that "conferred the greatest benefit on mankind". Most people now associate the name with the prize, not the weapon. Whether the motive was redemption, public relations or both is unknowable; as an institution it has created a lasting frame of reference for scientific achievement.
- **Trade-off**: prizes concentrate attention on a few people and on certain fields; areas without prize categories are overlooked.
- **Does not hold when**: you treat prizes as proof of correctness; they reward significance, and past awards have gone to claims that were later challenged.
- **Contested**: the origin story is partly legend and retold with dramatic detail; treat it as illustrative.

## 10.8 Outsider advantage and "impossible" results
- **Thesis**: the belief that something is impossible suppresses attempts; showing it can be done releases a wave.
- **Mechanism**: after the first four-minute mile, others followed within weeks and within a few years many had done it. After the bounded-gap proof, dozens of researchers improved it within months. Conversely, an expert workshop's conclusion that something cannot be done is a reason people do not try. Outsiders without that information, or with different methods, sometimes succeed.
- **Trade-off**: the "impossible" label also saves effort in truly impossible cases (perpetual motion).
- **Does not hold when**: the impossibility is a theorem (see ch09).
