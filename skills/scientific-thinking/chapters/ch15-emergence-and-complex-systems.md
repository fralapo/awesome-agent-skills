# Chapter 15: Emergence and Complex Systems

## Core Idea
In this model, many of the most interesting things (wetness, a heartbeat, an ant colony, a market, a mind, a nation) exist only at a level above their parts: the parts follow simple local rules and the whole shows properties none of them has. Explaining such a thing means working at the right layer, finding the feedback that couples the parts, and being honest about what is unknown (why the layers are so independent, where life or mind begins). Many claims in this area are positions: they are marked.

## 15.1 More is different: layers of reality
- **Thesis**: new properties appear when many parts interact, and each layer follows rules that are not visible in the layer below.
- **Mechanism**: no single water molecule is wet; wetness is a property of a very large number of molecules interacting. Reality can be read as stacked layers (particles, atoms, molecules, cells, tissues, organs, individuals, societies); put many things of one layer together and the next layer gets new traits: one atom handles no information, a group forms a molecule that stores it; molecules are not alive, a particular arrangement is. The layers are also largely independent: to explain how an organ works you do not need quarks, and to explain politics you do not need cells.
- **Trade-off**: working at one layer is efficient but hides what is going on below; descending a layer explains mechanism but rarely predicts the upper behaviour.
- **Does not hold when**: you are at a boundary where a lower-layer effect leaks up (a single mutation, a single trader, a single molecule in a small system).
- **Position (contested)**: some hold that everything reduces in principle to the lowest layer; others hold that emergent properties are real and cannot be derived by working up from particles. Both agree that no one can currently reconstruct the upper layers from the bottom.

## 15.2 Order from local rules and feedback
- **Thesis**: global order can come from agents that know only what they meet.
- **Mechanism**: in an ant colony, each ant carries a chemical signal of its current job and tracks the signals of the ants it recently met. If many gatherers are lost, the remaining ants meet few gatherers, and when the count falls below a threshold some switch to gathering; the balance is restored with no planner, and which individual ant switches is random. Heartbeat pacemaker cells synchronise by exchanging signals with neighbours and aligning with the majority; no cell issues commands.
- **Trade-off**: robust to losing any single agent and cheap to run, but slow to react and hard to steer or debug; the global result cannot be read from a single agent.
- **Does not hold when**: the task needs fast, precise, centralised coordination, or the agents' signals can be spoofed.
- **Use**: to influence such a system, change the local rule or the signal, not the individual.

## 15.3 Layer-appropriate explanation and the free-will question
- **Thesis**: whether a question is well-posed can depend on which layer it is asked at.
- **Mechanism**: a deterministic account runs: particles obey laws; given the state at one time, the later states follow; you are made of particles; so your decisions were fixed long ago. Adding intrinsic randomness changes the picture only by making the cause random, not by giving you control: a decision decided by a random event is not decided by you. A layered reply: the properties relevant to choice (character, deliberation, preferences) exist at the level of the whole brain, just as wetness exists at the level of the whole fluid; asking about free will at the level of particles is a category error, as is explaining a galaxy by digestion. On that layer, you are one of the causes of what happens in your brain.
- **Trade-off**: the layered reply keeps responsibility, but does not prove that the deterministic story is wrong, and it leaves the origin of the higher layer unexplained.
- **Does not hold when**: you take the layered reply as evidence for any specific mechanism; it is an argument about where the question belongs.
- **Position**: unresolved. For practical purposes, whether choice feels free and whether decisions can be explained by prior causes can both be true; the answer changes little about how to act.
- **Note**: the "block universe" reading of time adds another version of the question (ch16).

## 15.4 Entities defined by interaction, not substance
- **Thesis**: a colony, a company or a nation is real in the sense that it acts and is acted upon, although it has no body, brain or fixed membership.
- **Mechanism**: a nation's population, institutions, cities, borders and symbols all change; none is the nation. Yet nations wage wars, change landscapes and cease to exist, because of how a very large number of people interact. The same applies to markets, languages and organisations. These objects raise the same identity puzzles as other composite things (ch13, section 13.6).
- **Trade-off**: treating such entities as agents helps explanation and misleads when pushed (a "nation wants" statement hides disagreement).
- **Does not hold when**: you need to assign blame or duties; then individuals matter.

## 15.5 Life as work against equilibrium
- **Thesis**: a living thing is a structure kept different from its surroundings by continuous energy use, and stops being alive when it stops.
- **Mechanism**: a cell separates itself from its environment and keeps different concentrations of molecules inside and outside by pumping, which costs energy; a universal energy carrier (a small molecule whose breakdown releases chemical energy for work) powers this in every known organism. Photosynthesis captures light energy in chemical form; some cells eat others for the product; a decisive event was one cell taking in another without destroying it, giving the host cell an internal power plant (the origin of mitochondria, which burn sugar with oxygen to regenerate the carrier). The extra energy per cell allowed more complex structures and, much later, animals. The deeper reason is that the universe tends toward more probable, more uniform states (entropy); life locally resists by exporting disorder as heat and waste.
- **Trade-off**: high energy use makes complex life powerful and fragile (loss of oxygen kills the brain first).
- **Does not hold when**: you read "resists entropy" as breaking the second law; the total always increases.
- **Position**: the popular gloss of entropy as "disorder" is criticised as confusing; a more useful account ties it to the unavoidable loss of usable energy in engines (see ch17, section 17.11).
- **Unclear**: how and when the first cell and the first energy carrier arose; unknown.

## 15.6 Evolution: random variation, non-random selection
- **Thesis**: adaptation arises from variation that is random with respect to need, filtered by survival and reproduction.
- **Mechanism**: parents produce more offspring than the environment can support; offspring differ because of recombination (shuffling of parental genes) and mutation (copying errors; only changes that reach reproductive cells are inherited); competition for food, predators, parasites and climate then removes those with unsuitable combinations. The surviving traits spread. On a remote island, a founding group of birds filled different ecological niches by differences in beak shape, avoiding competition. Species are defined as groups that can produce fertile offspring.
- **Trade-off**: selection can act only on existing variation; trade-offs between traits limit what is reachable; most mutations are neutral or harmful.
- **Does not hold when**: variation is not heritable, or when chance dominates in small populations (general knowledge); "survival of the fittest" is then a poor summary.

## 15.7 Intelligence as a toolbox
- **Thesis**: intelligence is a set of problem-solving tools, not one quantity, and where it starts depends on definition.
- **Mechanism**: the basic tools are gathering information (about the world and the body's own state), storing it (memory), and learning (repeatable, adjustable sequences of behaviour). Richer tools include a library of associations, creativity (new connections from existing parts, such as using a tool or tipping a container instead of dropping pebbles), and planning (assembling activities towards a goal and checking events against the plan). A single-celled organism that marks its own trail and avoids it when exploring a maze, and bees that learn an unnatural task and optimise their route, show behaviour that looks like these tools. Whether these count as intelligence is disputed.
- **Trade-off**: a broad definition includes simple organisms; a narrow one excludes them; the choice is about use, not truth.
- **Does not hold when**: you need a single score; this view gives none.
- **Position**: related to consciousness but not identical (15.8).

## 15.8 A gradient of consciousness (hypothesis)
- **Thesis**: if consciousness evolved by small steps, it should be a gradient, not an on-off switch.
- **Mechanism**: a hypothesis in this model lists steps: random movement that slows where food is (no awareness needed); directed movement towards a source that depends on internal state (hunger); perception at a distance (vision); an internal representation that persists when the target is out of sight; memory and object permanence; a sense of time and the ability to wait for a larger reward; modelling what another animal sees or knows (caching food and re-hiding it when watched); and language, which allows hypotheses, plans and thought about oneself. The first step may have been the pressure to find food.
- **Trade-off**: it explains function, not experience: how a representation feels from inside is not addressed.
- **Does not hold when**: you want to locate the exact point where experience begins; unknown, and definitions compete. A claim that even stones have inner lives cannot be tested because they show no behaviour.
- **Position**: all of this is (position) rather than evidence.

## 15.9 Heavy tails: Zipf's law and the 80/20 rule
- **Thesis**: many unrelated quantities follow the same skewed shape, and nobody has a single accepted reason.
- **Mechanism**: in text, the second most common word appears about half as often as the first, the third about a third, and so on: frequency is proportional to one over rank, a straight line on a log-log plot. Estimates from the rank of a word predict its frequency in large collections of text well. Similar shapes appear in city sizes, earthquake sizes, website traffic, surnames, citations, wealth and many others. A related rule of thumb says about 20% of causes produce 80% of effects (the 80/20 rule), found in land ownership, software bugs and customers. One explanation is a compromise between speakers, who want few words, and listeners, who want many specific ones (least effort); others rely on growth processes. The word-frequency case remains without an agreed explanation.
- **Trade-off**: knowing the shape allows rapid estimates and prioritisation; it hides the mechanism, which differs between cases.
- **Does not hold when**: you treat 80/20 as a law (it is a rule of thumb; the exact split varies) or assume one cause behind all appearances.
- **Use**: when a few items dominate, fix the top items first; do not infer a shared mechanism from a shared shape.

## 15.10 Universality: the same pattern in unrelated systems
- **Thesis**: systems whose parts interact and repel one another show the same statistical spacing, whatever the parts are.
- **Mechanism**: events that are independent (arrivals at a stop) follow one distribution; events whose participants influence one another and avoid being too close or too far apart (buses spaced by informal agreements, energy levels in a heavy atomic nucleus, zeros of a prime-number function, cells in a retina) follow another, intermediate between random and regular. A model of a randomly filled table of numbers reproduces the spacing independently of how the numbers are drawn, which is why the same curve shows up across fields. Why this works so broadly has no complete general answer.
- **Trade-off**: transferable models save work; the match can be exact in statistics and say little about each system's mechanism.
- **Does not hold when**: parts really are independent, or the correlation structure differs.

## 15.11 Biological clocks
- **Thesis**: a roughly 24-hour rhythm is built into cells, not only imposed by sunlight.
- **Mechanism**: organisms keep a daily rhythm even in constant darkness, and the clock influences a large share of gene activity (over 40% of human genes by one report), including metabolism, hormones and temperature. Genes whose products feed back on their own production were identified; structural biologists ask how protein-based signals in a cell can measure out about a day.
- **Trade-off**: knowing the genes does not give the mechanism of timing.
- **Does not hold when**: a rhythm is merely a response to light and has no internal timekeeping.
