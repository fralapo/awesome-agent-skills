# Chapter 05: Confidence, Calibration and Expertise

## Core Idea
In this model, confidence and accuracy are different quantities, and the world rewards confidence. **Calibration** (does "80% sure" mean right 80% of the time?) is a skill that is rarely trained. **Expertise** is real only in environments that can be learned; elsewhere, credentials and fluency are the main thing on offer.

## 5.1 Overconfidence is the default
- **Thesis**: people are more certain than their accuracy justifies, in every domain, including experts.
- **Mechanism**: in classic general-knowledge quizzes, people who were 90% certain were right about 75% of the time. In an informal online version of this test with a science-literate audience, those who said they were 91 to 100% sure were right only about half the time. Professional forecasters of economic variables reported about 53% confidence in their predictions of inflation and were right about 23% of the time. A population survey finds most people rate themselves as better than the median driver, which is impossible. Overconfidence has been implicated in many large failures: reckless trading, ignored engineering warnings, test jumps with untested equipment.
- **Trade-off**: confidence helps initiative, persistence and leadership; it is costly when the stakes involve tail risks.
- **Does not hold when**: feedback is fast and clear (a chess player), in which case confidence tracks accuracy better.

## 5.2 Calibration as a practice
- **Thesis**: calibration improves with scoring, not with information alone.
- **Mechanism**: state probabilities ("60% chance I deliver by Friday"), record outcomes, compare. Tracking shows where your 90% really means 70%. Seek disagreement: understanding the best arguments of critics, and what they know that you do not, improves judgment more than collecting more of what you already believe. The best-calibrated people are those who know what they do not know.
- **Trade-off**: probabilistic statements can look weak or evasive to audiences who prefer certainty.
- **Does not hold when**: the decision is one-off and cannot be scored; then use outside-view reference classes and external feedback.

## 5.3 Why confidence persists
- **Thesis**: several mechanisms keep confidence high.
- **Mechanism**:
  - *Limited working memory*: judging your own accuracy requires holding many considerations at once; people with less capacity, or under load, give narrower ranges and are more overconfident.
  - *Substitution*: a hard question is replaced by an easy one (ch03).
  - *Status*: confident people are more often chosen as leaders and keep influence even when their ability is average; listeners literally process confident advice as more rewarding. Interviews and campaigns therefore reward the overconfident, who can talk a good line.
  - *Noisy feedback*: when outcomes are only loosely tied to decisions, nothing corrects a wrong self-image.
- **Trade-off**: any fix costs short-term status.
- **Does not hold when**: social incentives reward accuracy (scored forecasting, betting).

## 5.4 The Dunning-Kruger effect is partly an artifact
- **Thesis**: the popular "confidence peaks at low skill then collapses" graph is a meme, and the original pattern has a statistical explanation.
- **Mechanism**: the original finding is that the worst performers overestimate most and the best slightly underestimate. But if everyone's confidence lies in a narrow middle range while accuracy spans from near 0 to near 100%, then weak performers are bound to appear overconfident and strong ones underconfident; regression to the mean and noise in self-assessment produce much of the pattern. It does not require that incompetence blocks self-awareness.
- **Trade-off**: dismissing the effect entirely would also be wrong; people with little knowledge do often misjudge how much remains to be learned.
- **Does not hold when**: you quote the "Mount Stupid" curve as data; it is a communication device.
- **Contested**: the extent to which the effect is statistical versus psychological is debated in the literature.

## 5.5 Expertise is recognition built from practice
- **Thesis**: experts mostly recognise patterns from large long-term memory, not think harder.
- **Mechanism**: chess masters shown a real mid-game position for five seconds recall many more pieces than amateurs; shown the same pieces placed at random, they do no better than beginners. So their edge is not general memory, IQ or spatial skill, but stored chunks of positions that occur in real play. Recognition produces intuition. The same logic applies to recalling very long digit strings through strong chunking. It takes a long time: "10,000 hours" is a popular rule of thumb, not a law, and the practice must be of a certain kind.
- **Trade-off**: chunks are domain-specific and do not transfer.
- **Does not hold when**: the "expert" is judged outside the domain of their chunks.

## 5.6 Four conditions for real expertise
- **Thesis**: expertise requires a valid environment, many repetitions, timely feedback and deliberate practice.
- **Mechanism**:
  - *Valid environment*: regularities exist to learn. A roulette wheel gives plenty of repetitions and clear feedback but no learnable pattern.
  - *Repetitions with feedback*: tennis players, physicists and chess players get thousands of trials each with a clear result.
  - *Timely feedback*: anaesthetists see an immediate result; radiologists often get delayed or no confirmation, and their accuracy on breast cancer images is moderate. Admissions officers and recruiters rarely learn how the person they chose turned out.
  - *Deliberate practice*: effortful work at the edge of ability, ideally with a coach who identifies weakness. Driving becomes automatic after tens of hours; more hours do not improve you unless you push into harder conditions. Playing the songs you already know does not build skill.
- **Trade-off**: deliberate practice is unpleasant, and most people stop at a comfortable level.
- **Does not hold when**: a practitioner receives feedback by other means (peer review, simulation). Skills can also decay: physicians with two decades of experience did worse on rare diseases they had not seen for years until given a refresher.

## 5.7 Pundits and long-horizon prediction
- **Thesis**: where one-off, low-validity events are being predicted, specialist forecasts are no better than chance-like baselines.
- **Mechanism**: in a long study tracking hundreds of political and economic experts and tens of thousands of predictions, expert forecasts were barely better than chance and were beaten by simple statistical models, with little edge over informed non-specialists even in their own area. Their problem is that elections, treaties and crises are one-offs that never repeat in the same form, so they cannot accumulate feedback.
- **Trade-off**: experts do supply context and explanation that a random guess cannot.
- **Does not hold when**: a forecaster is required to give probabilities and is scored over many comparable questions; some people do improve under those conditions. (The strength of any one pundit "method" is also doubtful: a rule that predicted many past elections can fail on the next.)

## 5.8 Simple rules often beat unaided judgment
- **Thesis**: in noisy domains, a simple fixed formula frequently outperforms trained evaluators.
- **Mechanism**: in one study, counsellors interviewed students for up to an hour with grades, tests and a personal statement; a formula using only high-school grades and a single aptitude test predicted first-year grades better than 11 of the 14 counsellors. Similar results are reported for parole outcomes and pilot success. Humans add noise: mood, inconsistency and over-weighting vivid impressions.
- **Trade-off**: formulas can embed historical bias and are blind to novel factors.
- **Does not hold when**: no stable data exist to build a rule, or when ethical judgment about a case is the point.

## 5.9 Markets as a low-validity environment
- **Thesis**: stock picking is close to a coin flip because short-term price movement is dominated by chance, so clear feedback does not reflect decision quality.
- **Mechanism**: a public wager pitted a cheap broad index fund against a collection of actively managed hedge funds over ten years; the index won by a wide margin. Roughly 80% of actively managed funds fail to beat the market average over ten years, and up to 90% over longer periods. Of those who do beat it, some will do so by luck; random portfolios do about as well. When shown a lamp that lights green 80% of the time and red 20% at random, animals settle on green and win 80%; humans try to predict the red and win fewer (about 68%). We cannot accept average outcomes, so we chase patterns that do not exist.
- **Trade-off**: some managers have real edges (see ch07 for a possible exception that uses large-scale data and models).
- **Does not hold when**: the manager exploits a measurable, repeatable inefficiency and discloses a method that survives out-of-sample testing.
