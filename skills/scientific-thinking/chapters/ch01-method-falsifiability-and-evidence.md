# Chapter 01: Method, Falsifiability and Evidence

## Core Idea
In this model, science is a way of **compressing observations into theories that can be wrong**, and then trying to show that they are. What distinguishes it from other ways of knowing is not certainty but a procedure: precise claims, tests that could fail, independent repetition, and willingness to discard. The rest of this skill describes how that procedure is routinely undermined and how to protect it.

## 1.1 A claim must forbid something
- **Thesis**: a claim is only informative if some imaginable observation would count against it.
- **Mechanism**: a theory is useful when it narrows the possible world. A single short theory that accounts for falling objects, orbits, eclipses and the expansion of the universe is valuable because it makes risky, checkable statements about all of them. A story that fits every outcome (a favourable result confirms it, an unfavourable one is explained away) predicts nothing. Ask: "what result would make you drop this?" If the answer is "none", it is not an empirical claim.
- **Trade-off**: strict falsification is blunt. A single failed test can come from a faulty instrument or an auxiliary assumption, so a theory is rarely dropped on one result; the discipline is to say in advance what pattern of failures would suffice.
- **Does not hold when**: the claim is a definition, a value judgment or a mathematical statement; those are assessed by other standards (see ch09).

## 1.2 Confirmation is cheap, refutation is informative
- **Thesis**: look for the disconfirming case first.
- **Mechanism**: people naturally seek evidence that agrees with what they believe. A deterministic counterexample can settle a universal claim; any number of agreeing instances cannot. This is why a good testing process tries to break the thing rather than demonstrate it works.
- **Trade-off**: constantly attacking your own idea is uncomfortable and slow; the process feels worse than the one that confirms.
- **Does not hold when**: you need to explore (no hypothesis yet). Early exploration is legitimate, as long as exploratory findings are labelled and tested again on fresh data (ch06).

## 1.3 Theories are compressions; randomness is incompressible
- **Thesis**: finding structure means finding a shorter description of the data.
- **Mechanism**: ordered data can be compressed because patterns are predictable (a language is highly redundant; a video changes little between frames). What remains after all regularities are removed is the part that cannot be predicted, which is what we call random. Meaning lives between perfect order (nothing new) and pure noise (nothing to grasp). A scientific law is a compression of the world; its failure to compress some residue is where new science starts.
- **Trade-off**: over-compression, a theory that is too flexible, can fit anything including noise. The test of a real compression is that it predicts new data.
- **Does not hold when**: the underlying process has irreducible chance (quantum events, ch07); then the right target is the distribution, not the exact outcome.

## 1.4 Established knowledge versus speculation
- **Thesis**: separate the settled body of knowledge from live speculation and never present the second as the first.
- **Mechanism**: most speculative ideas are wrong, simply because the space of possible ideas is large and the number of true ones is small. Media and popular books often blur the line: a tentative framework with some successes and some failures is described as "what we have learned about the world". A reader can ask: has this been independently reproduced, by different methods, by people with no stake in it? Is it part of the textbook consensus or a recent preprint?
- **Trade-off**: waiting for consensus is conservative, and early adopters occasionally win; the cost of acting early on a false claim is usually larger than the cost of waiting.
- **Does not hold when**: the stakes of delay are high (a medical emergency, a security flaw); then act on the best current estimate and say how provisional it is.

## 1.5 The best and worst predictions share one theory
- **Thesis**: a theory is judged by its failures as well as its successes, and a huge mismatch is information.
- **Mechanism**: a framework of quantum fields predicts a tiny energy shift in hydrogen to better than one part in a billion, among the most precise predictions in science. The same framework, applied to the energy of empty space, predicts a value around 10^120 times larger than the observed one, among the worst. Both come from the same underlying idea: calculations with virtual particles. The success supports the tool; the failure shows the tool is being applied outside where it is understood. Scientists treat the gap as the most useful clue to what is missing, not as a reason to throw out the theory.
- **Trade-off**: a theory with a spectacular failure can still be the best available; abandoning it without a replacement loses its successes.
- **Does not hold when**: the discrepancy is caused by an error in the calculation or measurement rather than the theory; check that before announcing a crisis. (The estimate behind the 10^120 figure is a rough order-of-magnitude estimate.)

## 1.6 Virtual-particle style entities: indispensable does not mean directly observable
- **Thesis**: a quantity can be required by the calculation without being observed as a particle.
- **Mechanism**: in the field picture, the fundamental objects are fields, and the "particles" used in diagrams are disturbances that never appear as real, detectable particles, yet their combined effect on measurable energy levels is verified. This matters as a general point about theoretical entities: ask what the entity does in the model and what measurable consequence it has, before arguing about whether it "exists".
- **Trade-off**: calling an entity a calculation device is safe but may hide a real physical structure; calling it real invites over-interpretation.
- **Does not hold when**: a direct measurement of the entity becomes possible; then the question is settled by data.

## 1.7 Not knowing is allowed
- **Thesis**: "we do not know" is a valid and often exciting scientific answer.
- **Mechanism**: open problems point to the work still to do. A culture that treats ignorance as failure pushes people to overclaim; a culture that records the size of the gap (an error of many orders of magnitude, an unproven conjecture) directs effort to it.
- **Trade-off**: a stream of "we do not know" messages can undermine public trust if not framed as progress.
- **Does not hold when**: the question has a known answer that the claimant simply has not checked.

## 1.8 Phantom entities: when a new "substance" is invented for an odd reading
- **Thesis**: an unexpected measurement is more often an effect of the apparatus or of a known process than a new substance.
- **Mechanism**: an older theory treated heat as an invisible fluid; a mirror experiment then showed a thermometer dropping near a container of snow, and this led to a proposal that "cold" is a thing of its own, carried by rays from space. The modern account needs no new entity: everything radiates, a thermometer exchanges radiation with all its surroundings, and a mirror replaces a large part of what it receives from the room with radiation from the focal spot. If the focus is cold, it receives less than it emits and cools. Both the heat-fluid and the cold-ray ideas explained some observations, and both were replaced when a kinetic account (heat as motion of particles) accounted for more with less.
- **Trade-off**: new entities sometimes turn out to be real (a missing planet, a new particle); the rule is not "never posit", but "first rule out the apparatus and known processes".
- **Does not hold when**: the new entity predicts something new that is then confirmed.
- **Use**: when a measurement looks like a new substance, list what else exchanges with the sensor.

## 1.9 How well tested: quantity, quality and the limit of a claim
- **Thesis**: "well tested" means many independent tests across varied conditions, with high precision, and a statement of where the theory stops.
- **Mechanism**: a simple generalisation (the sun rises) has been tested an enormous number of times (on the order of a quadrillion human observations, a rough figure) at low precision; the most successful theory of matter has been tested a comparable number of times in a single collider in one year, at very high precision, and in some quantities to precision unmatched in science. Precision grows with better instruments: clocks based on atomic transitions are accurate to about a second in hundreds of millions of years, and optical clocks to about a second over times longer than the age of the universe; they are sensitive enough to test whether time ticks at different rates at different heights. A navigation system would drift by hundreds of metres if its clocks were off by a millionth of a second. The equality of inertial and gravitational mass has also passed tests for centuries. At the same time a successful theory states its own boundary: that theory omits gravity, because gravity is extremely weak at the scale of single particles and no accepted way exists to combine the two frameworks; and it is written in terms of fields, not particles.
- **Trade-off**: a theory can be very well tested within its domain and silent outside it; precision does not imply completeness.
- **Does not hold when**: tests are not independent (the same assumption repeated), or when precision is high and the domain is narrow.
- **Handoff**: details of particle theory and gravity belong to `physics-and-math-ideas`.
