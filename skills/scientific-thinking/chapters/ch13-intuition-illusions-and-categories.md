# Chapter 13: Intuition, Illusions and the Categories We Impose

## Core Idea
In this model, intuition is a fast tool trained on a narrow range of everyday sizes, durations and experiences. Outside that range (very large or very small numbers, many dimensions, long spans of time, other people's inner lives) it keeps producing confident answers that are wrong. A second source of error is naming: many "facts" are the edges of words we invented. The defence is to know which feeling is being relied on, to identify the memory or concept that produced it, and to replace it with a definition, a count or a measurement.

## 13.1 Intuition trained at one scale fails at another
- **Thesis**: a sense of "obviously" is evidence about your training, not about the world.
- **Mechanism**: finite intuitions (the whole is bigger than the part) fail for infinite sets (see ch14). In topology, a space can look flat when you zoom in and still have a different global structure; in four dimensions the intuition does not merely disappear, it remains and points the wrong way, which is why specialists in that area report doubting every result and proceed from definitions, axioms and proved statements only.
- **Trade-off**: ignoring intuition entirely is slow; it is a good generator of candidates and a bad judge.
- **Does not hold when**: the situation is in the trained range (everyday objects, familiar durations), where intuition is often well calibrated.
- **Use**: when a result "feels" wrong, ask whether you are in the trained range; if not, reason from definitions and check with a small computation or example.

## 13.2 Time feels different live and in memory
- **Thesis**: judged while it passes, a duration depends on attention; judged afterwards, it depends on how much was remembered.
- **Mechanism**: an empty activity (monotonous, unimportant) drags while it happens but shrinks in memory because few distinct events were stored; a full activity (novelty, challenge, significance) flies by while it happens but looks long afterwards. The same duration can therefore feel long and short, depending on whether you ask prospectively (during) or retrospectively (after). Retrospective estimates are built from how many distinct things can be recalled; if later years hold fewer new experiences, they are stored as fewer episodes and are judged shorter.
- **Trade-off**: the "each year is a smaller fraction of your life" account is tidy, but it is (position) rather than established: one line of evidence finds little change in how weeks, months or years are remembered, and a speed-up for decades that levels off after about fifty. A memory-count account fits better.
- **Does not hold when**: you need clock time; use a clock, not a feeling.
- **Use**: to make time feel fuller in memory, add distinct episodes; to judge how long something took, count events or use timestamps.

## 13.3 Concepts date events for you
- **Thesis**: when you place events in time without records, you use how similar your concepts of them are, not the calendar.
- **Mechanism**: if two events sit in very different concepts (an era, a style, a technology) you assume much happened between them and judge the gap long; if the concepts are close you judge it short. This works as a heuristic but misorders things whose concepts are poor guides to time. Periodisation (a decade, an age, an "era") is a set of contrived pieces: an era does not begin on a date; the label is chosen after the fact. Many popular "facts that sound false" expose where a concept says too much or too little, not a secret of reality.
- **Trade-off**: concepts reduce cognitive load by dropping detail; the cost is hidden distortions.
- **Does not hold when**: dates are known and checked.
- **Use**: before claiming "that was long ago / recent", check the date.

## 13.4 Other minds may not work like yours
- **Thesis**: people differ in kinds of inner experience, not only in degree; assuming everyone else's mind is like yours is a predictable error.
- **Mechanism**: a minority (about 3% in one often-cited estimate; rough) cannot generate mental pictures at will, while others have unusually vivid imagery; this is called a spectrum, from none to vivid. People often live decades assuming that "picture it in your mind" is a metaphor. Brain-scan work suggests weaker links between memory regions and the visual cortex in people without imagery, whose recollections also carry fewer visual and emotional details; some report fewer intrusive traumatic images. Because the phenomenon is self-reported, researchers use validated questionnaires and indirect measures, and the evidence rests on both.
- **Trade-off**: self-report is the only direct access, and also the least checkable.
- **Does not hold when**: the difference is a clinical loss with a known cause; then standard medical reasoning applies. Variation in imagery is not by itself a disorder.
- **Use**: when two people disagree about what is "obvious" in a task (visualising a design, imagining a user), test whether they use different mental strategies rather than deciding one is wrong.

## 13.5 Colour is built by the brain from light, surfaces and receptors
- **Thesis**: "colour" is not in the light or the object alone; it needs a source, a surface that selects wavelengths, receptors and a brain.
- **Mechanism**: visible light spans roughly 400 to 700 nm. Pigments absorb some wavelengths and reflect the rest; microscopic structures can instead reflect specific wavelengths (many blues are structural); some materials combine both. A pure red pigment would absorb everything except roughly 620 to 700 nm; any leakage muddies the hue, and a pigment that is also durable and safe is hard to find, partly because the link between a molecule's electrons and the colour it gives is still not predictable enough to engineer from scratch. Species differ in what they can see: some insects see ultraviolet and not red.
- **Trade-off**: engineering colour by structure avoids toxic or unstable pigments but is harder to scale.
- **Does not hold when**: you only need a good-enough red; the difficulty is for the ideal one.
- **Position (contested)**: a 2024 review reconstructed evolutionary trees and proposed that colour vision was already present in an ancestor of arthropods about 500 to 550 million years ago, before the conspicuous colours of fruit, flowers, warning signs and mating displays; one suggested reason is that colour vision helped see through flickering light patterns in shallow water. Reconstruction gets weaker the further back it goes, and the conclusion is an inference, not an observation.

## 13.6 Boundaries are conventions: objects, death, decades
- **Thesis**: much of what we treat as discrete things are agreements about where to cut a continuous reality.
- **Mechanism**: a paper and the crane folded from it are not identical (the paper survives unfolding, the crane does not), yet they are not two things in the same place; the relation is called constitution. A table is composed of many particles; when does a collection compose a thing? Answers range from "never" through "only if in contact or fused" to "any collection whatsoever". Peeling parts away one at a time never gives a clear moment when a chair stops being a chair (the sorites problem), and if you cannot say which atoms belong to the chair, billions of near-identical candidates exist (the problem of the many). Replacing every part of a ship over time raises the same issue (which one is the original, the repaired one or the reassembled one). Realists hold that nature has real joints that we find; anti-realists hold that our categories are useful cuts, none more true than another. In this model, the practical conclusion is the same: use categories for what they do (predict, coordinate) and expect them to give odd answers at the edge.
- **Death as a process**: heart stopping is followed by a cascade (brain tissue fails within minutes, other organs persist for much longer on emergency chemistry). "The moment of death" is a legal and medical convention placed somewhere along that cascade.
- **Trade-off**: dropping all categories is useless; keeping them without noticing their edges causes disputes that are only about words.
- **Does not hold when**: a boundary has a physical cause (a membrane, a phase change) rather than a naming choice.
- **Use**: when two people disagree about "is X a Y?", ask what decision depends on the answer and define the term for that purpose.

## 13.7 Randomness is easy to misjudge in both directions
- **Thesis**: random sequences contain patterns, and predictable events are often called random.
- **Mechanism**: with enough items, random processes produce readable words in identifiers and streaks in outcomes; and in everyday speech "random" has drifted to mean "unexpected" or "strange", although the event had causes. Whether a process is random is easier to rule out than to confirm. See ch07 for physics of coins, dice and the difference between ignorance and intrinsic randomness.
- **Trade-off**: calling things random lowers cognitive load but ends the search for a cause.
- **Does not hold when**: a process is built to be random and tested for it.
