# Chapter 11: Wellbeing, Intelligence and Learning

## Core Idea
In this model, three popular topics each illustrate how evidence can be strong in one respect and weak in another: **longitudinal data** tell a stable story about relationships and health but with caveats on sample and causation; **intelligence testing** predicts many outcomes modestly and is routinely misused; and **learning-style matching** is widely believed and lacks support, while the mechanisms that do work are known. Contested points are marked.

## 11.1 Why happiness is hard to study
- **Thesis**: people forecast their own happiness badly and recall it unreliably.
- **Mechanism**: lottery winners often show an initial surge and then are not much happier than others, and some become more isolated. Memory is reconstructive: we store pieces and rebuild, so retrospective accounts distort. The better design follows the same people through life, measuring repeatedly.
- **Trade-off**: long studies are expensive and hard to maintain across generations.
- **Does not hold when**: you only need a snapshot of current mood; then a daily-experience method may suffice.

## 11.2 What a decades-long study found
- **Thesis**: the quality of close relationships is the strongest predictor of later health and happiness.
- **Mechanism**: a study running since 1938 followed the lives of hundreds of men from two very different backgrounds (a university sample and a disadvantaged urban sample), later adding spouses and children, with interviews, medical records and eventually biological samples. Two conclusions: physical health habits matter (exercise, alcohol, smoking, preventive care) and relationship quality matters. At age 50, satisfaction in relationships predicted who would be healthy and happy at 80 better than cholesterol or blood pressure. Aggregated studies report: stronger social connection associated with about a 50% higher chance of survival over the follow-up; loneliness comparable in risk to a modest daily smoking habit; chronic social isolation and subjective loneliness each associated with roughly a quarter more risk of early death; poor relationships associated with higher risk of heart disease and stroke, and lonely older adults showing faster memory decline.
- **Mechanism (proposed)**: relationships regulate stress; without them the body stays longer in a stress state with higher cortisol and chronic inflammation, which wears down many body systems.
- **Trade-off**: the benefit is of quality, not numbers; an unhappy marriage can be worse than none; introverts need fewer but still need some.
- **Does not hold when**: you infer causation from observation alone: healthier people may find it easier to keep friends. The founding samples were not representative (initially young men), and most health-and-loneliness studies focus on people over 50. Treat the effect sizes as associations.
- **Practice**: relationships behave like fitness, a practice to repeat in small acts.

## 11.3 Money and happiness: a contested curve
- **Thesis**: whether income stops raising happiness above some level depends on method and on who you look at.
- **Mechanism**: a widely cited result said that emotional well-being stops improving beyond about 75,000 a year. A later study of many working adults using repeated experience sampling found well-being kept rising with income. A joint re-analysis found both views partly true: for most people, well-being increases with income, but for the least happy fifth, the rise stops at roughly 100,000, while for the happiest it keeps climbing faster. The pattern differs between groups.
- **Trade-off**: aggregate averages hide subgroups with different slopes.
- **Does not hold when**: money is compared across very different places or prices; the figures are US-specific and dated.
- **Position**: relationships, meaningful work and money all contribute; achievements as badges do not by themselves.
- **Contested**: the shape of the income-wellbeing curve is not settled.

## 11.4 What an intelligence test is
- **Thesis**: IQ summarises performance on a battery of tasks, scaled to a population mean, and is meant to estimate a general factor.
- **Mechanism**: scores on different school subjects correlate positively, which led to the idea of a general factor plus task-specific factors. Early tests compared a child's performance to norms for age; modern scores are scaled so the population mean is 100 and the standard deviation 15, so about 68% score between 85 and 115 and around 2% above 130. Typical item types: vocabulary, number sequences (find the pattern by differences or ratios), matrix puzzles with a few rules (shifts, rotations, missing symbols, overlay), all under time limits. Two broad types are distinguished: fluid (new problem solving, peaks in early adulthood) and crystallised (accumulated knowledge, stays stable).
- **Trade-off**: breadth reduces idiosyncratic error but adds testing time.
- **Does not hold when**: a single number is treated as a measure of worth or of every ability.

## 11.5 What IQ predicts, with sizes
- **Thesis**: IQ is a modest-to-strong predictor of school and job performance, and a weak predictor of wealth.
- **Mechanism**: reported associations: brain size about 0.33; exam results five years later about 0.8 in one large cohort (at the high end of reported 0.5 to 0.8 ranges); job performance 0.2 to 0.6, larger for complex jobs; income about 0.2 (so under 5% of variance); net worth even less; survival to old age somewhat higher for higher scores. A military example: recruits below a threshold needed far more remedial training and failed more often. One sample found top earners with IQs below 100; treat that as a single-sample anecdote, not a general rule.
- **Trade-off**: a population-level association does not allow prediction for an individual.
- **Does not hold when**: you generalise from range-restricted samples (for example selective schools).
- **Contested**: sizes vary by study; the largest correlations quoted here are at the upper end of the literature.

## 11.6 Heritability, malleability and the Flynn effect
- **Thesis**: IQ is partly heritable and partly shaped by environment, and average scores have risen over a century.
- **Mechanism**: twin studies give heritability estimates in a broad range, often 40 to 70%, with the caution that heritability describes variation within a population, not how fixed a trait is for an individual. Scores rise by roughly 2 to 3 points per decade (about 30 points over a century), requiring periodic renormalisation. The genes did not change, so explanations are nutrition and health, schooling, and a shift to abstract work. A population a century ago would score about 70 on today's norms, which is absurd as a measure of capacity; this shows the tests measure what culture trains.
- **Trade-off**: statements about average group differences in a single timeframe are easily misread.
- **Does not hold when**: cross-population comparisons ignore that tests are never culture-free; a nominal "culture-fair" test is a marketing term, because shapes and spatial relations differ in cultures.
- **Contested**: both the size of heritability and the causes of the Flynn effect are debated, and the claim of a plateau or reversal in some countries is still under study. Questions about group differences are particularly prone to misuse, and the evidence does not support reading them as innate.

## 11.7 Measurement caveats and misuse
- **Thesis**: scores depend on motivation, coaching and test-taking skill, and a history of misuse should shape how they are used.
- **Mechanism**: paying people to do well on the test raised scores in a meta-analysis, up to about 20 points for large rewards and more for low scorers (contested size); coaching and practice gave up to about 8 points; skipping hard items, time pressure and anxiety also matter. Historically, the idea that scores were inherited and fixed was used to justify forced sterilisation laws affecting tens of thousands of people and later cited by perpetrators of atrocities; the original purpose of the first test was to identify children who needed more help.
- **Trade-off**: ignoring tests removes an objective check; using them naively reinforces bias.
- **Does not hold when**: used to find talented children missed by teacher nomination (more poor and minority students were identified by a standardised test in one comparison), or to make legal decisions about intellectual disability, where embedded validity checks detect faking with good accuracy.
- **Position**: neither "tests are everything" nor "tests are meaningless"; a score does not determine a person's worth.

## 11.8 Learning styles do not hold up
- **Thesis**: the claim that people learn better when taught in their preferred modality has no credible support.
- **Mechanism**: in the common four-way scheme (visual, auditory, reading/writing, kinesthetic), over 90% of surveyed teachers believed in matching. The test of the claim is a randomised experiment: identify learners' styles, randomly assign matched or mismatched teaching, and compare test scores. In controlled studies, the matched groups did no better. Students mostly used study strategies inconsistent with their stated style, with no difference in results. Reviews have concluded there is no credible evidence for the hypothesis. People differ in aptitude (perfect pitch, spatial ability), but the claim that preferences are consistent across tasks fails: geography is learned better with a map for nearly everyone. The belief persists because experiences are interpreted to fit it: a clear diagram helps and gets attributed to being a "visual learner".
- **Trade-off**: teachers spend time and money on style assessment; students may avoid useful formats.
- **Does not hold when**: the task itself requires a modality (music needs listening).
- **Mitigation**: when searching evidence on a belief you hold, search for its refutation as well, not only for confirmation.

## 11.9 What does work
- **Thesis**: learning follows what happens in the learner's head; multimodal, active, effortful formats help everyone.
- **Mechanism**: words and pictures together beat either alone (multimedia effect) when the narration complements the visuals; explicit discussion of misconceptions is important in physics teaching; peer instruction, workshops and frequent questions force active processing; a memory strategy (build a story, rehearse, chunk) explains good recall in an informal list-recall test more than preferred style did. Learning is the process of building bigger chunks in long-term memory through repeated effortful engagement (ch04, ch05).
- **Trade-off**: more effort and less pleasant; students often rate it lower.
- **Does not hold when**: overloaded working memory; too many new items at once, as in some lectures.
