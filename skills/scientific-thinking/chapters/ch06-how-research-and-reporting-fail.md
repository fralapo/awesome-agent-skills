# Chapter 06: How Research and Reporting Fail

## Core Idea
In this model, published science is a **filtered sample**, and the filter selects for novelty, significance and a good story. The result is that a meaningful share of published findings, especially surprising ones, do not hold up. Institutions that use scientific-sounding evidence (courts, news, press offices) inherit the weaknesses without the correction mechanisms.

## 6.1 Why most published findings can be false
- **Thesis**: with realistic power and priors, the proportion of positive published results that are false is far above the nominal 5%.
- **Mechanism**: take 1,000 hypotheses of which 10% are true. With 80% statistical power, 80 true effects are detected. Of 900 false hypotheses, 5% (45) cross p < 0.05. If journals rarely publish null results, the published record contains about 80 true positives and 45 false ones, and more than a third of positives are wrong even before bias. Lower power, a lower share of true hypotheses, or researcher bias make it worse. The point is structural: a threshold on false-positive rate says nothing about the share of false findings among those published.
- **Trade-off**: raising the evidential bar reduces false claims but also discards real ones and slows work.
- **Does not hold when**: the field tests mostly well-motivated hypotheses with high prior probability and large samples.

## 6.2 Replication results
- **Thesis**: when prominent results are re-run, many fail to reproduce, and effects shrink.
- **Mechanism**: a large project repeating 100 psychology studies found about 36% reproduced a significant result, and effect sizes averaged about half the original. An attempt to verify 53 landmark cancer-biology findings reproduced six. These were different fields with different standards.
- **Trade-off**: failing to reproduce does not prove the original wrong; the replication may be underpowered or differ in method.
- **Contested**: how to define "successful replication" (significance, effect size inside an interval, subjective assessment) changes the headline rate, and fields differ greatly; the figures above are illustrative, not universal. Social-science studies that later failed to replicate were cited far more often than those that replicated, so attention goes to the wrong results.

## 6.3 P-hacking and researcher degrees of freedom
- **Thesis**: undisclosed analytic flexibility turns noise into findings.
- **Mechanism**: see ch02 (multiple outcomes, optional stopping, subgroups, dropping conditions). Even in particle physics, where the threshold is five sigma (about one chance in 3.5 million), an exotic five-quark particle was "found" by about a dozen independent experiments over two years, then disappeared when better-powered tests were done. The early analysts were not blind: they knew the expected answer, and cuts to the data were made until a signal appeared.
- **Trade-off**: blinding and pre-registration add procedure and may restrict exploration.
- **Does not hold when**: analysts are blind to the outcome and the plan is registered.

## 6.4 Incentives
- **Thesis**: careers reward publishing, journals reward significance and novelty, so the system selects for false positives.
- **Mechanism**: there is little cost to being wrong and a high cost to not publishing. Replications are hard to publish, and a failed replication can be dismissed as "not doing it right". Rational researchers therefore test novel, unlikely hypotheses (lowering the share of true ones) and choose analyses that clear the bar. Funding depends on attention, so universities and scientists promote results in press releases; journalists want clicks. The chain acts as a telephone game that simplifies and overstates.
- **Trade-off**: reforms add cost and may slow publication.
- **Does not hold when**: incentives are changed: pre-registered studies accepted regardless of outcome, online repositories for null results, public tracking of retractions, and larger replication projects. These have grown in the past decade.

## 6.5 Why headlines mislead
- **Thesis**: surprising results get the most attention and are the most likely to be wrong.
- **Mechanism**: examples of the pattern (2011 to 2023): a claim of a "traversable wormhole" made inside a quantum computer, which was in fact a small calculation that a laptop could do and a sketch of a speculative theory; a claimed room-temperature, ambient-pressure superconductor, whose own data showed resistance not dropping to zero, and a demo showing the sample also sticking to a magnet; an announcement of a net-energy fusion shot, where the gain was net only at the target (the lasers drew far more power than the shot released) and critics argue (position) that there is no near-term route to scale; a measured result of particles faster than light that came from a loose connector; a report of primordial gravitational waves that turned out to be galactic dust. Early reports of breaking science have a high probability of being wrong. Corrections receive far less attention than the original claim.
- **Trade-off**: skepticism can become cynicism, and genuine breakthroughs exist.
- **Does not hold when**: the finding is reproduced independently with different methods.
- **Red flags**: a single group; no replication; a claim much larger than the previous record (here, a claimed critical temperature roughly 260 K above the ambient-pressure record); data in the paper that contradict the headline; a press release stronger than the abstract.

## 6.6 Science in the news is not sport
- **Thesis**: news portrays science as a succession of earth-shattering breaks instead of steady progress.
- **Mechanism**: sport gets a regular roundup of small events; science only appears when it is dramatic. This gives the public a picture of constant crisis ("cosmology is in crisis"), and when hyped discoveries fail, trust falls and funding comes into question. Speaking openly about the risk of hype acts like an inoculation.
- **Trade-off**: dramatic framing attracts attention that supports funding.
- **Does not hold when**: a breakthrough really is sudden.

## 6.7 Forensic techniques adopted before validation
- **Thesis**: courts adopted pattern-matching techniques long before anyone measured their error rates.
- **Mechanism**:
  - *Microscopic hair comparison*: a review of examiners' courtroom testimony in 268 cases handled by a national investigative agency (up to 1999) found erroneous statements in 96% of them; examiners sometimes could not tell human from dog hair. Dozens of defendants had already been sentenced to death and some executed by the time errors were found.
  - *Bite marks*: studies on cadavers found skin is a poor medium (it distorts); none of 89 bite marks matched the models that made them. Yet it was admitted in court as recently as 2025 because the justice system values precedent.
  - *Bloodstain pattern analysis*: widely used from the 1970s, adopted on the basis of a founder's own tests; early methods assumed straight-line trajectories and ignored gravity and drag; the first baseline reliability study was published in 2014; analysts disagreed on the mechanism of a stain about 8% of the time in a large 2021 study.
  - *Fingerprints*: a classification scheme designed to file records, not to prove uniqueness; the human examiner makes the final call after an automated system returns a shortlist. The same examiner given the same pair twice reached a different decision about 10% of the time. A confident cross-checked match was wrong in a high-profile terrorism case, because the verifying examiners knew the first verdict.
  - *DNA*: strongest, but sensitivity now allows transfer of trace DNA onto the wrong object; mixtures of several people are hard to interpret. In a controlled comparison, 69% of laboratories got a four-person mixture wrong and only 21% called it inconclusive.
- **Trade-off**: having forensics is better than not; the issue is overclaiming.
- **Does not hold when**: the technique has been validated with known ground truth, error rates are published, and results are expressed as likelihoods, not as "certain match".

## 6.8 Contextual bias and conformity
- **Thesis**: examiners who know the story reach story-consistent conclusions.
- **Mechanism**: case forms often include the suspect's criminal record, the race of suspect and victim, and the investigators' theory; verifying examiners usually know the first examiner's finding. Conformity and stakes make agreement easy. The remedy is to separate examination from case information, use blind verification, and sequence unmasking.
- **Trade-off**: reduces speed and the examiner's sense of professional judgment.
- **Does not hold when**: the information is genuinely needed (for instance, which surface was touched) and provided late and in a controlled way.

## 6.9 What reliable science looks like
- **Thesis**: reliability is a property of the process, not of any one result.
- **Mechanism**: pre-registration with accepted-regardless-of-outcome publication; larger samples; blind analysis; independent replication; open data; public retraction tracking; and cautious communication that separates established knowledge from tentative claims. In the long run, sound science that has been vigorously tested survives; bold claims, mistakes and dead ends fade.
- **Trade-off**: slower and more expensive.
- **Does not hold when**: not applicable.

## 6.10 Worked case: a famous effect that shrank under replication
- **Thesis**: a viral finding from a small, flexible study can survive in public opinion long after replications fail.
- **Mechanism**: an early study with 42 participants claimed that holding an expansive posture for about a minute changes hormones, risk-taking and the feeling of power; a popular talk spread it. A later study with about 200 participants, blinded experimenters and a longer pose found support for the feeling of power only (hormones and risk did not clear the threshold). It coincided with a large effort to replicate 100 published psychology studies, which reproduced fewer than half (39 by one count). Common problems named in the field: small, underpowered samples; recruiting until the result turns positive; selective reporting of outcomes and analyses; and a bias to publish significant results. A critical blog re-analysed the supporting studies and found no collective effect on the physiological outcomes. The first author later said she did not believe in the effects and listed methodological flaws. Later preregistered studies found a small effect on the self-reported feeling of power and little else. A missing neutral control pose left open whether the effect came from the expansive pose or from the contractive one.
- **Trade-off**: public correction is slow, and personal conflict between camps (accusations of bullying) distorts the discussion.
- **Does not hold when**: a result has been replicated in preregistered, adequately sized studies.
- **Use**: for a "simple trick changes your life" claim, check the sample size, who measured what, whether outcomes were chosen in advance, and whether there is a neutral control.

## 6.11 "In mice": translating animal results to people
- **Thesis**: a result in an animal model has a substantial chance of failing in humans, and reports should say which animal.
- **Mechanism**: animal models resemble a human disease in some respects and differ in others. A mouse engineered with an inherited-Alzheimer's gene variant developed plaques and a drug targeting them looked promising, but failed in people; scientists do not know why, and one candidate reason is the difference in brains beyond the cellular level. A protein thought to help in multiple sclerosis worked in mice and worsened patients because mouse and human immune systems differ; the standard mouse model has symptoms like the human disease but works differently in cells and misses some phases. An antiviral drug that looked safe in several animal models acts on the DNA of cellular power plants (mitochondria) as well as viruses; the animal tests did not show that. Models also cannot capture everything for infectious diseases.
- **Trade-off**: models allow experiments that cannot be done in people and have produced real progress; the issue is overclaiming.
- **Does not hold when**: the pathway is highly conserved across species and the effect has been shown in more than one model and in early human data.
- **Use**: treat "cures X in mice" as the start of a long list of steps, and ask whether the model reproduces the mechanism, not only the symptoms.
