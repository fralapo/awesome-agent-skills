# Chapter 04: Cognition, Belief and Post-Truth

## Core Idea
In this model, belief is driven by **ease, identity and stories** at least as much as by evidence. The same mechanisms that let us navigate daily life (familiarity, pattern completion, group loyalty) are exploited by repetition, media systems and rhetoric. Knowing this does not make you immune, but it tells you which situations call for slow checking.

## 4.1 Cognitive ease and the illusion of truth
- **Thesis**: what is easy to process feels true, familiar and good.
- **Mechanism**: repeated exposure raises liking and credibility, even for nonsense words, unfamiliar characters, yearbook faces or a false statement about a temperature. In a classic experiment, words printed more often in a student newspaper were judged to mean something better. Fluency can also be raised by high contrast, a clear font and easy-to-pronounce names; ease feels like a sign of safety. In evolutionary terms novelty can be a threat, so repeated and harmless exposure becomes comfort. The same mechanism explains fame by familiarity and the strength of advertising.
- **Trade-off**: ease is efficient in familiar settings and gives intuition and creativity.
- **Does not hold when**: the domain is counter-intuitive (physics, statistics) or hostile (marketing, propaganda). There, treat a feeling of obvious truth as a cue to check.
- **Contested**: results showing that a hard-to-read font lowers errors on trick questions (from a large majority wrong to about a third wrong in the original reports) have been difficult to replicate consistently; treat the disfluency trick as unproven for practical use. Repetition effects are more robust.

## 4.2 Two modes of thinking
- **Thesis**: much of thinking is fast, automatic pattern recognition checked, when at all, by a slower effortful process.
- **Mechanism**: a bat and a ball cost 1.10 together, the bat costing 1.00 more than the ball: most people say 10 cents, the correct answer is 5. The fast process offers an answer; the slow process, which is lazy, endorses it without checking. The slow process works in working memory, which holds only about four or five new items, and gets around that limit by chunking: grouping items into larger familiar units through learning. Effort shows physically (pupils widen when people hold and transform digits). Skills become automatic through repeated effortful practice, which then frees working memory.
- **Trade-off**: automation is efficient but rigid. Habits formed in one setting (light switches in the opposite direction, a reversed-steering bicycle) can interfere when the setting changes.
- **Does not hold when**: the two-system picture is treated as literal anatomy; it is a useful model, not two separate organs.

## 4.3 Desirable difficulty
- **Thesis**: things that force the slow process to work are more likely to be understood and remembered.
- **Mechanism**: an advert that deliberately does not make sense gets attention because the fast process cannot dispose of it. In education, passive lectures are easy to tune out and often introduce too much new material for working memory; formats that require answering questions and solving problems force effortful processing (ch11). The pleasant feeling of understanding a smooth explanation is not evidence of learning.
- **Trade-off**: effortful learning is less pleasant, and learners rate it lower.
- **Does not hold when**: difficulty is irrelevant to the target skill, or when it overloads working memory so that nothing is retained.

## 4.4 Stories beat statistics
- **Thesis**: a vivid individual case persuades more than aggregate data.
- **Mechanism**: in a classic helping experiment, a participant heard a fellow participant apparently suffering a seizure over an intercom, and only a minority (about 30%, a handful of a small sample) left their booth to help before the stimulus ended. Told this result, observers still judged two ordinary-looking individuals from the study as likely to have helped; they did not apply the general finding to the particular. But when they first saw the individuals and were told these people did not help, they generalised to the overall rate correctly. The mind works better from cases to rules than from rules to cases. This is why a clear narrative with a villain and a mechanism (a chemical, a company) spreads faster than a probabilistic consensus, and why one cold winter feels more informative than a global trend.
- **Trade-off**: communicators of science face a genuine dilemma: a dramatic single demonstration communicates better than repetitions and statistics, but the repetition and statistics are what establish the fact.
- **Does not hold when**: you are doing the science itself, where repetition and statistical analysis are required. (The original helping study has a small sample, and its familiar narrative has been questioned; treat the pedagogical point as more secure than the exact numbers.)

## 4.5 Motivated numeracy
- **Thesis**: skill does not protect against bias; it can be recruited to defend identity.
- **Mechanism**: participants solved the same proportional-reasoning table framed either as a skin cream trial or as a gun-control study. On the cream, accuracy rose with numeracy for everyone, regardless of politics. On gun control, accuracy rose with numeracy only when the correct answer agreed with the participant's side; when it disagreed, high-numeracy people did no better, and the gap between their own-side and other-side accuracy was larger than for low-numeracy people. Similar patterns appeared with science literacy and with "actively open-minded" self-ratings.
- **Trade-off**: group loyalty is a rational strategy for a social species, so the bias has a function.
- **Does not hold when**: the topic is not identity-laden. Also **contested**: the strength of the effect, and whether it holds across topics and samples, has been debated in later work.
- **Mitigations**: avoid loaded group labels and discuss concrete local policies; foster science curiosity (people who seek out surprising evidence did not polarise as numeracy rose); do not villainise the other side.

## 4.6 How arguments evolve online
- **Thesis**: communities and algorithms select for the version of an argument that provokes most.
- **Mechanism**: the internet lets small, dispersed groups find each other, including groups with extreme or intolerant views that were once too diluted to matter. Each group builds and mutates its picture of the opposing argument, keeping the variants that anger most; these spread; engagement-ranked feeds amplify them further. Material that triggers outrage is shared more than material that informs, producing something like selection pressure on ideas. In one election cycle, fabricated stories were reported to be shared more than real ones.
- **Trade-off**: the same networks let niche communities of learners form, and early signs (broad access to facts) were genuinely positive.
- **Does not hold when**: community members know each other personally and bear a social cost for lying; feedback from friends pulls views back.
- **Position**: ranking on signals of truth (links from reliable sources, track records, citations) rather than on engagement is a plausible direction, though hard to build and subject to constant gaming.

## 4.7 Fact-checks and arguing
- **Thesis**: facts alone rarely change positions; timing and identity matter.
- **Mechanism**: live fact-checks often arrived after statements had spread and did not seem to change behaviour, because each camp had its own account of what is true. Confirmation bias, the tendency to look for agreeing evidence, is a widely documented tendency. Whether public arguing deepens entrenchment is an open question (position: before arguing, consider whether the argument could entrench the other side).
- **Trade-off**: silence leaves a false claim unchallenged.
- **Does not hold when**: the audience is genuinely undecided or the correction offers a replacement explanation.

## 4.8 Treat opinions as swappable
- **Thesis**: hold beliefs like papers in a box: replace one when a better one arrives.
- **Mechanism**: a surprising claim can sound wrong and then check out in a quick search (for example, that manufacturing weighs much more in the lifetime emissions of an electric car than of a petrol one, although the use phase usually still dominates); accepting it means discarding a comfortable belief, which feels unpleasant. Notice the pull to defend the old paper.
- **Trade-off**: constantly updating can look inconsistent.
- **Does not hold when**: the new claim is simply asserted more loudly; the swap rule applies to better evidence, not to repetition.
