# Chapter 12: Evaluating a Claim: Checklist and Worked Cases

## Core Idea
In this model, evaluating a claim is a **short sequence of questions** that catches most errors before any deep analysis: what exactly is claimed, who benefits from the claim, what the comparison is, how much chance could explain it, how many times it was tried, and whether anyone independent has reproduced it. This chapter collects the sequence and applies it to a contested public topic.

## 12.1 The checklist
1. **State the claim in one sentence with a number.** What changes, by how much, compared with what? If the claim cannot be made specific, it cannot be checked. (ch01)
2. **What would falsify it?** If nothing would, treat it as story, not as a finding. (ch01)
3. **Base rate and prior.** How common is the thing before the test or study? Is the claim extraordinary? Compute the posterior with a simple table of 1,000 people. (ch03)
4. **Comparison group.** Was a control selected like the treated group? Could regression to the mean explain the change? (ch02)
5. **Denominator and definitions.** Rates or counts; reported or surveyed; same period and population? (ch02)
6. **Number of tries.** How many outcomes, subgroups, analyses and stopping rules were possible? Was the plan declared in advance? (ch02, ch06)
7. **Effect size and uncertainty.** Variance explained, interval, practical size. A small p-value is not a large effect. (ch02)
8. **Surprise and replication.** The more surprising, the more independent reproductions are needed. Single group, single lab: low weight. (ch06)
9. **Incentives along the chain.** Researcher, institution, press office, outlet, platform, seller. Who gains from this being true? (ch06)
10. **Source of the confidence.** Is the claimant an expert in a valid, feedback-rich environment, or a fluent generalist? Has their record been scored? (ch05)
11. **My own reaction.** Do I like the conclusion? Does it flatter my group? Does it feel obvious because it is repeated? Am I substituting an easier question? (ch04)
12. **Mechanism and alternatives.** Is there a plausible causal pathway, and what third factor could produce the association? Is the vivid story hiding an aggregate that points elsewhere? (ch04, ch02)
13. **Search for refutation.** Search for the claim plus "debunked", "failed to replicate", "criticism", not only for support. (ch11)
14. **Decide with a probability and a next step.** "About 25% likely, would update on a replication in an independent sample." (ch03, ch05)

- **Trade-off**: complete application takes time; apply steps 1 to 4 to everything, the rest in proportion to stakes.
- **Does not hold when**: the claim is a value judgment or a matter of definition rather than empirical fact.

## 12.2 Fast triage by claim type
| Claim type | First questions | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| "New treatment/intervention works" | comparison group, randomisation, regression, sample size | selected extreme, no control, many outcomes |
| "Test says you have X" | base rate, false-positive rate | accuracy mistaken for posterior |
| "Study shows surprising effect" | replication, pre-registration, analytic flexibility | p-hacking, publication bias |
| "Breakthrough announced" | independent confirmation, data in the paper vs headline, size of the jump | press release outrunning data |
| "Expert predicts" | track record, scored probabilities, environment validity | pundit with no feedback |
| "Forensic match" | validation studies, error rate, blind verification, context | overclaiming certainty |
| "Everyone is wrong about Y" | which version of Y; steelman; evidence base | myth that is itself a myth |
| "Rational choice implies Z" | level (individual vs group), one-shot vs repeated | confusing the levels |
| "Math proves democracy/markets/science is impossible/inconsistent" | exact theorem statement and its conditions | stretching a theorem beyond its conditions |
| "It worked in mice" | model fidelity, mechanism, human data | animal result presented as a human one |
| "We are probably living in X / aliens would doom us" | list the assumptions; which are testable | conclusion stated without its conditions |
| "This everyday effect defies physics" | which two quantities are conflated | hidden term; energy not counted |
| "Everyone's mind works like mine" | different strategies, self-report validity | projection of one's own inner experience |

## 12.3 Worked case: common misconceptions about the climate
This case shows how to separate a robust consensus from a rhetorical trick. The claims below are common ones; figures are dated.
- **"It is cold today, so there is no warming."** Weather is local and short-term; "global warming" refers to the global average and the term "climate change" covers the wider set of effects (storms, droughts, floods, acidification). Check scale and time.
- **"Warming stopped in 1995."** A trend line drawn from a hot outlier year and ignoring later data gives a flat slope; most of the warmest years occur in the recent period, and satellite data support the upward trend. Check the start date and the full series. (Cherry-picked window.)
- **"Scientists said it was cooling in the 1970s."** In that decade, papers predicting warming outnumbered those predicting cooling by roughly six to one. A media story is not the literature.
- **"Sea ice rose in the last two years."** A short uptick against a long downward trend. Short windows of a noisy series.
- **"It is the sun."** The sun brightened in the early twentieth century and contributed then, but has dimmed since the 1950s while temperatures rose.
- **"Humans emit a tiny fraction of the CO2."** Natural flows are much larger (about 780 gigatons both into and out of the atmosphere, in rough balance) than human emissions (about 37 gigatons CO2 per year in the early 2020s), but the natural flows balanced; the small unbalanced addition accumulates. The atmosphere's concentration moved from a 180 to 280 ppm band over 800,000 years to above 400 ppm. Isotope ratios of carbon show the added carbon comes from fossil fuels. Flows versus stocks.
- **"Volcanoes emit more."** They emit under 1% of the human amount.
- **"Water vapour is the main greenhouse gas."** True and relevant: it amplifies warming. The atmosphere holds more water vapour only when warmer, so it responds to the CO2-driven change (a feedback), and melting ice lowers reflectivity (another). A doubling of CO2 alone gives about 1 degree C; with feedbacks, evidence points to about 3 degrees C. Check whether the factor is a cause or a feedback.
- **"The predictions failed."** An early model that assumed higher sensitivity overshot; rerun with a sensitivity of about 3 degrees per doubling, it matches observed warming well.
- **"The climate changed before, with CO2 lagging temperature."** Past cycles were triggered by orbital changes; the initial warming released CO2 from oceans, which amplified the warming (most of the rise came after CO2 began to increase). The temporal order does not contradict causation by CO2 now, where the CO2 is added first.
- **"So what if it is warmer?"** Cost and risk comparison: it is probably cheaper to cut emissions early than to pay later.
- **Contested/uncertain**: the exact climate sensitivity (the range around 3 degrees), regional impacts and the policy response remain debated; the figures here are a dated snapshot. Rhetorical pattern across the exchange: shifting from one objection to the next without conceding the previous one.
- **Position**: the existence of a hostile, repetitive set of objections is not evidence about the science; check each against data, and expect that campaigns of doubt have a record in other topics (tobacco, ozone).

## 12.4 Worked case: a "net-energy" or "room-temperature" headline
Apply steps 8, 9, 14: surprising; single group; press release; data in the paper at odds with the headline; a jump bigger than any previous; incentives for funding and attention; independent labs failing to reproduce. Probability assigned: low; next step: wait for replication.

## 12.5 Worked case: a screening test result
Apply step 3 with natural frequencies. Ask the prevalence in people like you, the sensitivity and false-positive rate; compute the expected counts per 1,000; consider a second independent test.

## 12.6 Worked case: a manager says "criticism works"
Apply step 4: was the change measured against a comparison? Regression to the mean explains why praise after a great performance is followed by a worse one. Test with randomised feedback style, or at least track the whole distribution.

## 12.7 Worked case: "democracy is mathematically impossible"
Apply step 1 and the table: what does the theorem state, what conditions? Ranked ballots with three or more options and five conditions. Escape routes: rated ballots, single-peaked preferences. The headline overstates.

## 12.8 Worked case: "we are probably in a simulation"
Apply the far-scenario rules in ch16: write the assumptions (consciousness can be simulated, technology continues, civilisations survive, they choose to simulate, many simulations exist), mark which are testable (none cleanly), and state the conclusion conditionally. The headline drops the conditions.

## 12.9 Worked case: "a posture trick changes your hormones and your life"
Apply steps 3 and 4: sample size, replication, preregistration, control pose. See ch06, section 6.10: the fuller replications supported only a small change in a self-report.

## 12.10 Worked case: "a spool/lever/siphon video shows physics is wrong"
Apply ch17, section 17.14: which two quantities are merged? Is energy conserved? Compute the idealised number, measure it, and chase the difference.
