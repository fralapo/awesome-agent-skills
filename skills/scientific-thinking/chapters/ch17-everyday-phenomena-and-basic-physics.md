# Chapter 17: Explaining Everyday Phenomena with Basic Physics

## Core Idea
In this model, most "paradoxes" of everyday physics (a lighter container that weighs the same, a spool that rolls toward the hand pulling it, a weight that rises when a rope is cut) dissolve when you find the quantity that was being lumped together with another one: force and pressure, mass and weight, springs in series and in parallel, shortest and fastest. The method is: state the surprise precisely, separate the conflated terms, check limiting cases and units, vary one thing at a time, and compare a predicted number with a measured one, then chase the gap. Chapter 15 gives emergent phenomena; this chapter gives mechanisms at the scale of a kitchen.

## 17.1 Pressure is not force: the hydrostatic paradox
- **Thesis**: the force on the bottom of a container depends on the height of liquid above it and the area, not on how much liquid is in the container.
- **Mechanism**: pressure is defined at a point on a surface and equals force divided by area; the force on a base is the sum of the pressure over its area. In a vertical column, the pressure at depth is set by the weight of the column of liquid directly above. A narrow vessel and a wide funnel of equal height therefore press equally on their equal bases; a funnel needs several times more liquid to produce the same reading as a narrow column. The everyday mix-up (a stiletto "applies more force" than a foot) is really about pressure.
- **Trade-off**: pressure is the right variable for fluids; force is right for the total load on a support.
- **Does not hold when**: the walls are not straight and the weight of liquid and force on the base differ; then walls carry some of the load (general knowledge); one tested container shape did not obey the simple rule and is unclear.
- **Use**: before comparing "how heavy", ask whether the quantity is force, pressure or mass.

## 17.2 Mass, weight, apparent weight and "down"
- **Thesis**: mass is intrinsic; weight is a force that depends on surroundings; what a scale shows is a force reading converted to mass units.
- **Mechanism**: a scale responds to any force, not only gravity, so it can be fooled. Weight is mutual: you pull on the Earth as hard as it pulls on you, but the same force moves you far more because your mass is smaller (a dropped pencil moves the planet by a fraction of the width of a proton). An orbiting astronaut has most of their weight but no apparent weight, because they and everything around them are falling together; a helium balloon has weight and negative apparent weight because buoyancy outweighs it. "Down" is not the same everywhere: Earth's spin reduces apparent weight at the equator by about half a percent, and its shape and density variations make the sea-level surface (the surface perpendicular to local down) undulate by up to roughly a hundred metres from a smooth ellipsoid; a person weighs a fraction of a percent less in the places with weaker gravity. A map of such variations can reveal what lies under the surface.
- **Trade-off**: using "weight" for mass is harmless on Earth's surface and misleading elsewhere.
- **Does not hold when**: you leave the surface or use a scale that measures force in an accelerating frame.
- **Related**: inertial mass (resistance to acceleration) and gravitational mass (response to gravity) are different concepts and have been found equal to extreme precision; that equality is why all objects fall alike in a vacuum, while a feather in air falls slowly because of air resistance, not mass.

## 17.3 Weighing what cannot be put on a scale
- **Thesis**: to measure what you cannot touch, find an effect it has on something you can.
- **Mechanism**: a plumb line near a large mountain tilts slightly toward it. Knowing the mountain's volume and rock density from surveys, the tilt compared with the pull of the whole planet gives a ratio of masses, hence the mass of the Earth. Other bodies are weighed by the orbits of things around them.
- **Trade-off**: the answer is only as good as the estimate of density and shape.
- **Does not hold when**: you need local composition; mass alone does not tell you what the planet is made of.

## 17.4 Springs in series and in parallel
- **Thesis**: cutting a tension can raise a weight, because the arrangement changes from series to parallel.
- **Mechanism**: a spring extends in proportion to load; two springs in series (end to end) share the same load and extend by the sum, so a load stretches the pair twice as far; two in parallel share the load, each stretching half as far. With slack ropes bypassing the springs and a short rope joining them, cutting the short rope switches the system from series to parallel, so the weight goes up although "tension was released". Releasing the rope slowly shows the weight dropping first, until the slack ropes tighten, then rising: two regimes. A similar structure appears in traffic networks, where adding a link can make everyone's trip slower (analogy rather than identity).
- **Trade-off**: a tidy rule for ideal springs; real springs deviate from the linear law.
- **Does not hold when**: springs are far from ideal or are compressed to contact.
- **Use**: when a change in constraints surprises you, redraw the system before and after, not just the force.

## 17.5 The spool: rolling and where the force line goes
- **Thesis**: whether a pulled spool rolls toward or away from you depends on the angle of the pull relative to the contact point.
- **Mechanism**: pulling the thread horizontally makes the spool roll toward you and the thread shorten: when a spool rolls a quarter turn away, it unspools less thread than the distance it has travelled, so the thread is pulled out of your hand. Pulling upwards gives the opposite. In between there is an angle at which the line of force passes through the contact point with the table, so it produces no turning effect and the spool just slides if friction is insufficient. As the spool gets nearly full, the mechanical advantage of the arrangement grows without bound; the stated limit assumes enough friction for rolling without slipping.
- **Trade-off**: simple geometry gives the answer; real spools add friction and slipping.
- **Does not hold when**: friction is too low for rolling.

## 17.6 The lever: a chain of reasonable steps that ends in free energy
- **Thesis**: a chain of individually plausible steps can derive a contradiction, and energy accounting shows that at least one step is wrong.
- **Mechanism**: a mass pushed with a constant force over a distance gains the work done as kinetic energy; a lever changes force and distance but not the work, because halving the mass and doubling the arm leaves the turning effect unchanged. Combining these with a tempting assumption about what a lever does to a moving mass yields twice the energy out as went in; the conclusion violates conservation, so the error is in a step. The full resolution is not covered here. In an experiment with colliding balls, the best position for the collision lay far from both the textbook optimum and the faulty one; shock-wave and impedance-matching views (a wave is partly reflected where the medium changes) suggest tuning mass and position to avoid reflection.
- **Trade-off**: conservation checks are a strong filter; they do not tell you which step failed.
- **Does not hold when**: you forget to count a form of energy (heat, vibration, elasticity of the supports).
- **Use**: if a derivation ends in free energy, look for the hidden term; if you cannot find it, assume it exists.

## 17.7 How tall can a siphon be: model, measure, refine
- **Thesis**: a number from an idealised model is a first estimate; a measured gap points to the missing physics.
- **Mechanism**: atmospheric pressure can support a column of water about 10.3 m high; a siphon and a drinking straw are limited by the same balance. A tall siphon operated at about 10 m. Dissolved gas came out as bubbles at about 9 m but was flushed along; the break came at about 10.07 m. At the top, the pressure drops so low that water boils off at room temperature, creating vapour that stops the balance. Subtracting the vapour pressure of water at the temperature used (about 2% of atmospheric pressure) from the pressure that supports the column gives the observed height. Larger claims exist and are unclear.
- **Trade-off**: simplifying assumptions (perfect vacuum, no dissolved gas) make the first answer easy and slightly wrong.
- **Does not hold when**: a different liquid or temperature is used; the vapour pressure changes.

## 17.8 Boiling without heating, and heating by cooling
- **Thesis**: the boiling temperature depends on pressure, so water can be made to boil by lowering the pressure.
- **Mechanism**: boil water in a bottle, cap it, and pour cold water over the bottle. The vapour above the liquid cools and condenses, the pressure falls, and the still hot liquid, now above its boiling point at the lower pressure, boils again. The liquid does not cool much because liquid water holds much more heat per degree than water vapour. At altitude the same dependence makes water boil at a lower temperature (so tea is poor).
- **Trade-off**: the effect is demonstrable and safe only with a sealed container that can withstand the pressure change; take care.
- **Does not hold when**: the container is not sealed, or the liquid is cooled as a whole.

## 17.9 Inverted whirlpool: the heavy liquid rises to the middle
- **Thesis**: centrifuge intuition (heavier goes outward) is correct only for a container that spins with its contents.
- **Mechanism**: stirring a denser liquid under a lighter one in a stationary container makes the denser liquid bulge up in the middle, as tea leaves gather at the centre of a stirred cup. Replacing the lighter liquid with air gives the expected outward flinging. Rotating the whole container on a turntable gives the expected outward behaviour; the bulge appears only when the liquid spins in a stationary container, for example after the turntable stops. The detailed mechanism (pressure gradients balancing the swirling motion) is not covered; only the observation and the controls are described here.
- **Trade-off**: the control experiments (turntable, replacing one fluid) isolate which factor matters.
- **Does not hold when**: the liquids mix into an emulsion; use fluids that separate cleanly.

## 17.10 Exponential decay: twice as fast does not mean twice as long
- **Thesis**: when the rate of loss is proportional to the current amount, a bigger start buys only a fixed extra time per doubling.
- **Mechanism**: a spinner loses speed in proportion to its speed, so its speed falls by half in each fixed interval (exponential decay); the measured speed curve fits this. The spin time until it falls below a threshold then grows with the logarithm of the start speed (derived, general knowledge): doubling the start speed adds one half-life, not twice the time. Therefore effort should go to reducing friction, which changes the decay rate, not to hitting harder.
- **Trade-off**: friction in real bearings has more than one component, and air drag changes the shape.
- **Does not hold when**: the loss is constant per unit time (dry friction alone gives linear decay).
- **Use**: whenever "twice the input" is promised to give "twice the result", check whether the process is proportional or logarithmic.

## 17.11 Resonance and entropy: better descriptions
- **Thesis**: a good physical description says what is being added to what.
- **Mechanism**: resonance is the timing of repeated pushes so that each adds to the energy already in the system: repeated taps on a membrane at a tube's rhythm add to the reflected pulses, and the pulse grows. There are many resonant frequencies, not one, since you can add a push after any whole number of passes. The "favourite frequency" description is incomplete. Entropy was introduced to analyse the efficiency of engines: an engine extracts work from heat flowing from a hot part to a cold one, and some heat must always pass to the cold side, which limits efficiency. A small engine that runs on the temperature difference between a hand and room air shows this. The popular gloss of entropy as "disorder" is criticised as confusing.
- **Trade-off**: formal definitions are exact and opaque; mechanical descriptions are intuitive and partial.
- **Does not hold when**: the system is driven off its resonant condition or has too much damping.

## 17.12 Why shaking brings the big pieces up: granular convection
- **Thesis**: gentle shaking sorts a mixture by size with the largest on top, even if they are denser.
- **Mechanism**: when a big piece rises it leaves a gap that small pieces fill; friction at the walls also drags the outer layer of small pieces down, driving convection cells that carry large pieces up through the middle. This sorting (the Brazil nut effect) is the reason cereal boxes and nut tins are layered after transport; shaking the box makes it worse rather than mixing it. It is a problem for powders in industry.
- **Trade-off**: the same effect helps separate, and hurts mixing.
- **Does not hold when**: the shaking is violent or the pieces have very different shapes and surfaces (general knowledge); the effect described here is for slow, low-speed shaking.

## 17.13 Light and matter in daily life
- **Thesis**: colour effects are often scattering or surface-chemistry effects that look like magic.
- **Mechanism**: the sky is blue because molecules much smaller than the wavelength of light scatter short wavelengths far more strongly (Rayleigh scattering); sunrise and sunset colours come from light passing through more air. The early atmosphere had a different composition, so the sky then looked different; life changed it more than once. A tarnished metal has formed a thin, self-sealing oxidised layer; gold resists this, and a "tarnished gold" ring is usually an alloy; the full explanation involves both electron structure and surface behaviour, and the account here is partial.
- **Trade-off**: short explanations ("because of scattering") are correct and incomplete.
- **Does not hold when**: particles are comparable to the wavelength, which gives whitish scattering (general knowledge).

## 17.14 Debugging an everyday paradox
1. Write the surprise as two numbers or two predictions.
2. List the quantities involved and mark the ones that are being merged.
3. Take a limiting case (zero, infinity, one spring, empty container).
4. Check units and energy; a derivation that creates energy has a hidden term.
5. Vary one variable and run a control (the turntable, the straight-walled container).
6. Compute the idealised number, measure, and look for the missing term in the gap.
7. Accept "unclear" when the explanation cannot be completed.
