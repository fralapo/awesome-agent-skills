# Chapter 09: Copy That Sells

## Core Idea
Copy is the transmission of a clear message. Before touching a sentence, diagnose the offer and the context; then clarify the message from the reader's side, one clear sentence at a time, built backwards from what the reader wants to achieve. Headline rules, benefit extraction, specifics, compressive power and the final gate are in `creative-director` (role `copywriter`); this chapter adds the *diagnosis, the sequence of a sales piece, and the practice habits*.

## Frameworks Introduced

### 1. Diagnose before you write
- Check first whether the product or offer works. If a weak page still sells, the core offer works: optimise that, not the surroundings.
- Many competitors usually signal demand; no competitors and no sales usually signal no demand.
- Review as a **first-time visitor**; teams that live with a page stop seeing its confusion.
- Be specific about what the product does ("a tool that writes blog posts"), not jargon.
- Choose the best medium for the message (text, image, GIF, video) and compress information where possible.
- Market only what you can stand behind; transparent products (free trial, short demo) sell easily.
- Other touchpoints matter; the landing page cannot do all the work.

### 2. Dissect any sales piece with four questions
1. **How did it present the problem?** (Paint the pain before offering the solution.)
2. **How did it frame the benefit?** (Explicit and implicit.)
3. **How did it overcome unspoken objections?**
4. **How did it get you to act?**
A typical persuasion sequence found in effective pieces: reframe the status quo as undesirable → features → felt benefits and tone → a villain (the old way, the big industry) against a hero (the natural, small-batch alternative) → social proof → risk reversal (guarantee) → one simple call to action. Awareness leads to consideration then purchase; bigger purchases need more touchpoints, so do not expect one asset to close a high-value sale.

### 3. Trigger exercise (start with what moved you)
Ask: what caused *you* to act in the last 30 to 90 days? Reverse-engineer the trigger and test whether the piece you are writing pulls the same lever. Be specific about who you talk to; a tight audience responds strongly. A quiz that narrows choice and asks for an email in return for a result builds micro-commitments.

### 4. ADAP structure (copy for the web)
Four moves in order: **Attention** (a self-interest hook) → **Detail** (proof, how it works) → **Desired state** (who the reader becomes) → **Call to action** (as specific as possible). The label is a memory aid for the four moves; the order is what matters. The first line must appeal to self-interest. Persona profile: list the audience's pains and gains and frame the offer as removing the pains.

### 5. AIDA for sequential formats (carousels, emails)
Attention (short headline plus provocative image) → Interest (a reason to keep going: why should I care?) → Desire (paint the changed state) → Action (one specific step). State the benefit early, not features. Put the densest, most valuable point in the middle; readers must get past the third slide. About eight content slides is a practical default and ten the maximum; split a dense slide instead of cramming. Cover with image plus headline outperforms all-type. Close with one action (comment, save, question). Platform specifics → `social-algorithm`.

### 6. Hook formulas
- **Promise formula**: promised result + time frame + the objection it overcomes.
- **Heads or tails**: write about ten headlines, list the key takeaways, then build the piece backwards from them.
- Headline gets the reader to the subhead; do not pitch in it (ch04 layers).

### 7. Testing discipline
- Test the **biggest lever first**: messaging and offer before button colours.
- With small traffic, improve clarity headline by headline rather than running statistical tests.
- For email tests, run an **A/A test** first to measure noise; sender reputation often outweighs subject-line wording.
- Short cold emails tended to outperform long ones in one practitioner's campaigns; long copy is right for a high-consideration page. The two are not in conflict: length follows the reader's stage.
- A one-off viral spike is hard to repeat; distribution through a network is more reliable.
- AI tools are useful for generating many short variants and weak for long-form; humans set strategy and curate. Do not let generated text replace your own thinking.

### 8. Writing practice
- **Copy work**: transcribe a strong author's text by hand to absorb rhythm and structure.
- **Three-stage loop**: intentional consumption (read with a goal, extract a lesson) → distilling and reflection (notes on takeaways, patterns, your state) → creation (apply the patterns).
- Patterns to note: each piece shifts the reader's perspective; a surprising title is answered in the first line; line breaks create pauses.
- Set constraints for the creative phase; they focus attention on the idea.
- Use 30-minute sprints with short breaks rather than long quotas (a long-sprint plan proved exhausting).
- Write in public and accept imperfect output; draft and edit as separate passes.

### 9. Building a repeatable format
Compile what you already share into a fixed-section newsletter. Demonstrate live where possible (editing a page on screen). Short paragraphs, plain words, a conversational tone; replace academic phrasing.

## How to Apply (step by step)
1. Write the offer sentence (ch02) and check that the page says the same.
2. Run the first-visitor review and mark every confusing line.
3. Write the problem paragraph before the solution.
4. Dissect one great sales piece with the four questions; copy the sequence, not the words.
5. Draft with ADAP, then add one proof and one risk reducer.
6. Reduce to one call to action.
7. Test the largest lever; run A/A first for emails.

## Common Mistakes
- Jumping to the solution before the problem.
- Vague audience ("everyone").
- Testing button colours instead of messaging.
- Expecting the landing page to do all the work.
- Insider jargon; vague self-praise ("award-winning", "trust us").
- Stacking calls to action; writing long for length's sake.
- Chasing viral formulas.

## Checklist
- [ ] Offer clear to a first-time visitor in five seconds?
- [ ] Problem before solution?
- [ ] Self-interest hook in the first line?
- [ ] Proof and risk reversal present?
- [ ] One specific call to action?
- [ ] Biggest lever tested first?
