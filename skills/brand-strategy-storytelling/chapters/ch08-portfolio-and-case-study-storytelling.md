# Chapter 08: Portfolio & Case-Study Storytelling

## Core Idea
A portfolio or case study is a story that links your effort to a measurable impact for a client, built so that a skimmer gets the message from the headlines and a deep reader finds the proof underneath. Structure it as an **inverted pyramid**, write the words before the visuals, and make the client the hero and you the guide. UX-specific case-study ingredients (research, validated problem, testing) are in `ux-ui-expert` ch23; slide design for portfolio decks is in `presentation-design` ch09. This chapter is the narrative structure that sits on top.

## Frameworks Introduced

### 1. Inverted pyramid (from journalism)
| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| **Lead** | Headline plus subhead: the takeaway |
| **Body** | Supporting evidence |
| **Tail** | Extra context and proof |
- Every slide or section has a headline stating its takeaway; a reader who reads *only the headlines* should get the story.
- Not a rigid wireframe: not every slide needs a body and a tail.
- Create hierarchy by size and weight; uniform type size means nothing stands out. Chunk text into short paragraphs. Visualise research (charts, quotes) instead of pasting dense documents.

### 2. Three portfolio audiences
1. **Screener**: skims many candidates.
2. **Hiring manager and peers**: read more.
3. **Offer stage**: decides.
Build one portfolio that serves all three through the pyramid, rather than several versions per audience.

### 3. Build order
Career roadmap first: a **one-sentence positioning statement** decides which projects to show (ch03). Then write headlines to form the storyline, then body copy, then visuals. Separate writing from design. Aim for a minimum viable portfolio, submitted for feedback on a fixed deadline; progress over perfection.

### 4. Case-study anatomy
Order: **title, visuals, summary, body**.
- **Title** with an outcome hook, not just the client name.
- **Summary**, three parts for skimmers: (1) the challenge from the client's point of view; (2) the outcome you delivered; (3) the results or impact on their business.
- **Body**: a story where the client is the hero and you are the guide (normal / explosion / new normal, ch05). Not a technical log.
- **Visuals**: process photos, sketches, notes, whiteboards, plus final work in use and isolated on a plain background. Show the *middle* of the process, not only final images.
- **Results**: measured wherever possible; aesthetics alone are judged on taste.
- **Placement**: on the website, and in a short **capabilities document** (a few cases plus services) for early enquiries, before a full proposal.
- Concrete examples of skills in action beat descriptions of skills.

### 5. Pitching business results
Pitch measurable outcomes (percentage sales lift, retention) and state the value; clients repeat the result, not the portfolio. Build a repeatable story clients can retell internally. Test work with the real audience before launch and judge it from the user's side of the table.

### 6. Beliefs and process as proof
Make beliefs visible in the portfolio, not only the work. Depth of process (many iterations) shows passion and judgment. Show how you think, not only the answer ("one plus one plus one").

### 7. Submission checklist (agency or studio)
- Best work first; cut the rest. Quality over quantity.
- State your **role** explicitly on team projects.
- Show process: early frames, versions, short case studies.
- Say specialist or generalist, with evidence; do not claim skills you cannot deliver.
- Keep it current; customise per studio or role.
- Contact details easy to find; respond quickly.
- Hiring-side test: imagine receiving 100 generic emails a day; "to whom it may concern" messages are discarded.
- Be clear about rates and terms (see `pricing-and-sales-negotiation`).

### 8. Presenting design in context
Set the stage before showing the design: who it is for, what problem, what this round covers. Storytelling in a presentation gives feedback in context and reduces unhelpful reactions. Share the direction before the reveal.

### 9. Case-study structure for process-heavy projects
Screenshots alone are not enough; the reader needs context and reasoning to judge the decisions. A sequence that works for design and product projects:
1. **Executive summary on one slide**: what it is, what makes it unique, your role.
2. **Problem and process in one sentence**, repeated at the top of each section so the reader always knows where they are.
3. **Process diagram** with a short label per step: the flow at a glance.
4. **Storyboard or sketches** with a caption per frame, in case drawings are unclear.
5. **Proto-personas** built from the team's existing knowledge: key tasks, emotional state at each stage, needs. Skip invented demographics that add nothing.
6. **Flows and wireframes** that reference the personas, then **high-fidelity screens** with the reason they exist (for example stakeholder feedback).
7. **One deep-dive screen** with numbered annotations, one per design decision.
- Rebuild visuals for the portfolio instead of shrinking working documents; pasted persona sheets become unreadable.
- Keep detail high-level and show the decisions that mattered, not every step. Reading only the headlines should still give the story (section 1).

### 10. Close the imagination gap with real proof
A buyer cannot picture the result of a service they have not bought. Close the gap with documented real experiences: photos, video, testimonials, audio from actual work.
- No proof yet? Create it: offer a first project on friendlier terms (scope and pricing: `pricing-and-sales-negotiation`) and document it.
- Never claim authenticity while showing stock or AI-generated images in place of real evidence.
- Specialise first: proof from one niche persuades better than scattered samples (ch03).

### 11. The portfolio piece as a reusable asset
- Estimate production time honestly, then assume you underestimated it; adding a margin (some practitioners double the estimate) avoids burnout and missed dates.
- Compute return per asset: hours per project, number of projects, leads generated. Example with illustrative numbers: about 16 hours per project x 3 projects, yielding 2 leads, is roughly a day of work per lead.
- Reuse one asset everywhere: portfolio, website, social posts, capabilities deck.
- Do not generalise from one anecdote (a single lead from one post is not a plan); update a portfolio with the full case, not one image per brand.
- Keep private life (pets, food, family) out of the professional showcase; put beliefs and values on the about page (ch06 on the personal/professional line).

## How to Apply (step by step)
1. Write the one-sentence positioning statement; pick 3 to 5 projects that prove it.
2. For each project, write: outcome-hook title, three-part summary, 3 to 6 headlines for the body.
3. Add numbers; if none exist, ask the client for one (even a before/after observation) and say how it was measured.
4. Choose visuals: two process, one in use, one isolated.
5. Cut anything the headlines do not need.
6. Read the page by headlines only; fix breaks in the story.
7. Prepare a capabilities PDF with 2-3 cases and a short service list.

## Short Examples
- Weak title: "Rosa Café". Stronger: "Rosa Café: a menu redesign that raised the average order 14 percent" (the number is illustrative).
- Summary: "Challenge: tourists skipped the lunch menu. Outcome: a bilingual, photo-led menu. Result: lunch covers up, questions to staff down."

## Common Mistakes
- Naming the client as the title and giving no outcome.
- Walls of text; identical font sizes.
- Presenting only the final visuals; no process; no role.
- Several portfolio versions per audience, over-polishing one comma for weeks.
- A portfolio-only site with no content, so nobody finds you.
- Copy-pasted cover letters; outdated reels; contact details buried.
- Creating content for other designers when the target is clients.
- Screenshots with no explanation; paragraphs of text on every slide; one anecdote treated as a repeatable plan.

## Checklist
- [ ] Headlines alone tell the story?
- [ ] Each case has an outcome-hook title and a three-part summary?
- [ ] Client is the hero, you are the guide?
- [ ] At least one measured result per case, or a stated reason?
- [ ] Role and process visible?
- [ ] Positioning sentence decides what is shown?
- [ ] Capabilities document ready for early enquiries?
- [ ] Problem sentence repeated per section, decisions annotated, personas useful rather than decorative?
- [ ] Real proof (not stock) for the claim, and each asset reused in several places?
