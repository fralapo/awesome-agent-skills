# Chapter 05: Brand Story Arcs & Craft

## Core Idea
A product without a story is a commodity; with a story it is a brand, and brand equity is the premium a customer pays over a cheaper alternative. Story tension lives in the **gap** between what a character expects and what reality delivers. A brand story needs one specific person, a want, a concrete obstacle, and one takeaway. Memory is built from emotion plus story, and the emotion can be ordinary and business-relevant.

The skeleton (situation, desire, conflict, change, result), the "but / so" connector, the domino payoff, sensory detail, reliving and the 60-second hook are taught in `public-speaking-persuasion` ch02 and ch03. This chapter adds brand-level arcs and the editing tools that make a written draft land.

## Frameworks Introduced

### 1. Want, obstacle, gap
- Give every character a **want** and an **obstacle**; a want granted at once is no story. "No conflict, no story."
- Subjective expectation meets objective reality; the friction is the story.
- **Beats and gaps**: every beat should open or close a question. For each beat ask "what is the audience now wondering?" A beat that shows without raising a question gives the viewer nothing to wonder about. Use context beats (a darkening sky) to build suspense before the payoff.
- Delay the conclusion and give meaningful detail; reveal the outcome late.

### 2. Normal, explosion, new normal
Three parts: the **normal** (the world as it is), the **explosion** (the event that disrupts it), the **new normal** (the changed world).
- A product launch can frame the old state as the normal ("phones aren't smart") and the new product as the explosion.
- Open with an **establishing shot**: a specific time, place and situation to orient the reader.
- Components in order: setting, explosion, **stakes** (what is at risk if this is the end), sensory detail, dialogue, emotional reaction, lesson.
- Name the stakes explicitly; exaggerate for emotional effect ("best taco of my life" beats "interesting") but never with lies that break trust.
- Put the lesson *after* the tension, not before.

### 3. Brand hero arc
Four parts: **origin** (background and history) → **defining moment** (the catalyst) → **transformation** (the point of no return; the character cannot go back to the old world) → **opposition** (who or what you stand against; characters are defined by allies and enemies alike).
- The hero is defined by the *choice to act*, not by acquiring the ability.
- Take a position: a brand with no opponent attracts fans but no critics, and its story stays shallow.
- Tell the same three-part story repeatedly and consistently, in the audience's language.
- A list of achievements is not a story.
- Fuller version (the full ordinary-world-to-return journey): ordinary world, call, refusal, mentor, threshold, trials, setback and resurrection, return with the lesson. Use it for long-form founder stories.

- **Two journeys, outer and inner**: a brand story has an external journey (what the hero does and the obstacles met) and an internal one (what the hero feels, fears or learns). Brand stories that carry only the outer journey tend to fail to move people; add the inner one. A series that follows the format "I did this, here is the result" is a hero journey in small form.

### 4. Story formula and binary critique
Formula: **personal story + emotional response + one clear takeaway**. Check with four yes/no questions:
1. Is it personal (could someone else have written it)?
2. Does it evoke a feeling (does it leave the reader less than indifferent)?
3. Is there a visible struggle?
4. Is there one takeaway?
In critique, say where the struggle is or what you did not feel; never "I like it / I don't". **Save / share / note test**: if people save, highlight or share, the takeaway is strong; if they do none of these, there is no takeaway. Many takeaways leave zero.

### 5. Editing tools for a flat draft
- **Point-first (start at the end)**: write one sentence stating the point, tell the story back toward it, and cut every detail that does not drive it. Also decide how the story ends and include only the parts that lead there.
- **Top three turning points**: list the three moments that changed the direction and build around them.
- **Emotion audit**: label each paragraph with its emotion (dread, anxiety, excitement, relief). If the answer is "none", rewrite until it lands.
- **Director's zoom**: open wide (time, place, situation), then move to tighter shots.
- **Expand the stakes**: "I needed the job and yet I quit." Hold relief until late; early relief releases the reader.
- **"But" or "instead" instead of "and then"**: forks and reversals create tension (see `public-speaking-persuasion` ch03).
- **Specifics over adjectives**: appearance, numbers, sensory details.
- **Constant editing**: when thin, add; when too much, cut. If you use an AI tool to tidy a draft, feed it the full detailed version first, then trim.
- **Before and after an event**: record expectations, hopes and fears beforehand, lessons afterwards; that is raw material.
- Usable emotions: frustration with an inefficient system, annoyance, agitation while solving a problem, curiosity, wonder, plus despair, courage, fear, love, honour, duty. No hardship backstory is needed (see `public-speaking-persuasion` ch02).

### 6. Customer as the hero
Cast the customer narrowly: wants, needs, fears, aspirations. Position the offer against the *obstacles the customer faces*, in their own words. Design the customer journey so strangers become friends and friends become customers. Describing the audience as "people" is too broad to write for. Count the realistic audience before choosing a channel: a roughly dozen-buyer audience was argued out of a costly website in favour of a hand-delivered presentation.

### 7. Product story before wireframes
Write why the product exists, who it is for, what problem they have, how it affects their life, and how the solution helps. Storyboard the user journey scene by scene (actions, behaviours, environment, people) before any screen or device. A shared story aligns design, research, product and tech; confusing products often start from fuzzy messaging decided early. Storytelling used only at the end to explain an unclear product is a repair, not a strategy. Screens themselves: `ux-ui-expert`.

### 8. Repeatable story, placed where the buyer sees it
- **Repeatable story test**: is the story short, clear and vivid enough that a customer can retell it to someone else and do your marketing for you? If a customer cannot retell it, shorten it and make one detail vivid.
- **Put it where the buyer looks**: on the pack, the product, the label or the first screen, not only on the website or in the mouths of staff. Design speaks one-to-many and is identical every time; a spoken story depends on whoever happens to tell it.
- **Specific, unusual but true details** (an odd origin, a place, a constraint) are remembered; generic claims ("passion for quality") are not.
- **Never fabricate the backstory.** An invented origin can sell better in the short term, but it can be detected and it erodes trust when it is. If the true story is plain, find the true detail inside it.

### 9. Four components and a prepared story set
A story that works in business has four components:
1. An **identifiable character**: a person, not the brand.
2. **Authentic emotion**: any real feeling, not necessarily tragic.
3. A **specific moment** the listener can sit inside, not a summary of a period.
4. A few **specific details**, concrete sensory cues that show you understand the audience.

The change of state is the conflict (normal, explosion, new normal), and the conflict can be small and everyday. The hero need not be heroic; self-deprecation that feels engineered is noticed.

**Story set**: prepare about three stories per product or audience before you need them, and rehearse aloud until the beats come on their own. Memorise beats, not word-for-word scripts, so the telling stays live and you cannot lose the point mid-story. When time is cut, cut bullets and figures first and keep the story; even a short edit keeps one or two details, which signal authenticity. Opening a talk with a story lowers audience resistance (delivery: `public-speaking-persuasion`).

### 10. Story shapes to choose from
Pick the shape that fits the material; all of them reduce to: a character, a strong want (external and superficial), a main obstacle, and a need (internal growth) that differs from the want.
- **Story circle (8 steps)**: comfort zone, want, unfamiliar situation, adapt, pay a heavy price, learn, return to the familiar situation changed. Lighter than the full hero journey (section 3); good for case studies and short founder stories.
- **Seven plot types**: *quest* (reach a goal); *manhunt* (defeat an antagonistic force); *metamorphosis* (rise, fall, regain with growth); *voyage and return*; *comedy* (a barrier keeps lovers apart, then resolves); *tragedy* (a flawed protagonist falls); *rebirth* (redemption).
- Give protagonists flaws; a perfect hero is dull. Make small problems feel large during the ordeal. Antagonists usually stay the same; the hero changes.
- **One-line test**: say who wants what, what blocks it, and what they learn. If any part is missing, there is no story yet.
- Client positioning use: the client is the hero, the designer is the mentor, the offer removes the barrier (ch08).
- A lesson that is stated instead of earned through change will not stick.

## How to Apply (step by step)
1. Pick the customer (ch02 persona) and write their want and obstacle in one line each.
2. Write the point in one sentence.
3. Outline normal / explosion / new normal, and add the stakes.
4. Draft; then run the emotion audit and mark paragraphs with no emotion.
5. Run the four yes/no checks and cut to one takeaway.
6. Start the final draft at the point of greatest conflict, not in chronological order.
7. Test with the save/share/note signal on a small audience.

## Short Examples
- A bakery story: normal = "bread tastes the same everywhere"; explosion = the founder's grandmother's starter is found; new normal = a street where the loaf is still warm at 6 a.m. Takeaway: "you can taste where it came from".
- A freelancer's origin: "I was making logos nobody used" (normal) → a client's shop closed (explosion) → "I now start with the problem, not the logo" (new normal).

## Common Mistakes
- A story without a point, or with five points.
- Telling everything in order, with every detail.
- Giving the lesson before tension exists; leaving the emotion implicit.
- Posting facts and bullet points, which leave no memory.
- Writing a story anyone could sign.
- Avoiding opinions so nobody pushes back, leaving the narrative with no tension.
- A customer-facing story that lives only in the sales talk, or an invented backstory.
- A stated lesson with no change; a perfect hero with no obstacle; a story reported as events with no emotion.
- Polishing until the deadline with no compass for quality.

## Checklist
- [ ] A named person with a want and an obstacle?
- [ ] The gap: what is the reader wondering at each beat?
- [ ] Normal, explosion, new normal, plus stakes?
- [ ] Each paragraph has a nameable emotion?
- [ ] One takeaway, stated in one sentence?
- [ ] Opens near the conflict, not at the beginning of time?
- [ ] An opposition or stance is visible?
- [ ] Could a customer retell it in a sentence, and is it visible where the buyer looks?
- [ ] Is every detail true, and is at least one specific?
- [ ] Three prepared stories, with beats rehearsed aloud?
