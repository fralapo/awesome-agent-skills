# Chapter 02: Discovery & Brand Definition

## Core Idea
Visual and verbal decisions are easier to make and to defend when they rest on a facilitated understanding of goals, customers and the brand, built *before* any design starts. Replace the written questionnaire with a conversational discovery session, find the root problem behind the request, turn the findings into a small set of attributes and one positioning sentence, and use those as the filter for everything that follows.

## Frameworks Introduced

### 1. Discovery session, in order
1. **Goals**: what does "winning" mean for this engagement? Ask for the definition of success early, and put it in numbers where you can.
2. **Challenges**: what blocks those goals right now?
3. **Customers and their pain points** (see persona below).
4. **Brand attributes** (see attribute pair below).

You facilitate: ask, challenge assumptions, mirror back, let the client talk. Do not persuade. Package the insights into a strategy brief and a creative brief.

Why not a questionnaire: it feels like homework, yields generic answers and turns you into an order-taker.

### 2. Symptom vs root cause
Clients often self-diagnose and prescribe ("we need a new logo"). Probe until the underlying issue appears (treat the allergy, not the rash). Probes:
- Why now? Deliverable-only briefs usually come with small budgets and no strategy.
- Ask for an example of every vague adjective ("innovative", "optimistic"): "Show me something that is that."
- "What would have happened in three years' time that would make you happy, professionally and personally?" A look-back question reveals the real goal.
- "What else?" until the list ends.

### 3. Qualify the lead by problem awareness
A request for a price on a deliverable is a **cold** lead. A prospect who describes a problem like one you have solved is **warm**. Do not treat every inbound inquiry as hot; the question you ask next differs.

### 4. Funnel math worked backwards (when the goal is a number)
Target clients → conversations needed → sign-ups needed. Example logic: 5 clients at a value of 1,000 each supports up to 5,000 of spend; at a few replies per hundred contacts, you need hundreds of sign-ups to get a handful of conversations. Put the goal in numbers *before* choosing tactics; widen the top of the funnel; do not judge a campaign on a tiny sample. (Reply-rate figures are rough rules of thumb; check the local benchmark before judging any quoted figure.)

### 5. Customer personas as living hypotheses
Two steps:
1. List behavioural customer types (for example enthusiast, connoisseur, casual fan), then choose the target.
2. Give the target a named profile: age range, life stage, job, income, place, shopping habits, brands they buy; rank their top three needs. Add what they think, hear, do, feel, say and see.

Rules:
- A persona is a hypothesis: refine with market feedback and new team members, re-validate regularly because assumptions go stale.
- Use it as the filter in every review: "are we designing for her, not for us?"
- "Every customer is our ideal customer" is a refusal to choose, not a persona.
- Where several roles use the product (end user, administrator, content creator, partner), write a use case for each and find where their paths cross.

### 6. Brand attributes to a pair, then to a sentence
1. Collect words freely; do not edit during brainstorming, sort and cut at the end.
2. Use **opposing pairs** and accept tensions ("weathered but modern"). When words stall, react to a wall of existing brand examples, then keep the top five descriptors.
3. Group, then reduce to **two** (for example "refined" plus "rustic"). The pair becomes the filter every reference, sketch and sentence must pass.
4. Define the brand in six categories (culture, customer, voice, feel, impact, X-factor), distil to keywords, then write a one-sentence positioning statement *with the client*. Agreement on direction before drawing makes pushback fall.
5. Prefer visual or concrete words to abstract ones; each attribute should map to something you can show.

### 7. One-sentence offer statement (product or service)
"My company is developing **[specific offering]** to help **[specific audience]** solve **[problem]** with **[secret sauce]**."
- Name a type of offering, not "an app".
- Narrow the audience: "college students applying for jobs", not "students".
- Every feature or service must map back to the sentence or be dropped.
- Lead with the problem. Buzzword pitches ("transform healthcare") get rejected; narrow concrete problems ("glasses slide down your nose") get traction.
- Expect several drafts. For a different slot structure (B2B, unworkable/unavoidable/urgent) use `marketing-mba` ch05.

### 8. Competitor audit
Gather the competitive set and look for **clutter and execution gaps**. A cluttered category tells you the target user is not being helped; a clean execution gap is room to stand out. Do the audit before any visual exploration.

## How to Apply (step by step)
1. Run the four-part session with the founder and key stakeholders in one room.
2. Probe the brief: why now, success in numbers, an example for each adjective.
3. Build one or two personas and rank their top needs; keep them on the wall.
4. Reduce attributes to a pair; draft the positioning sentence with the client.
5. Write the strategy brief: goal, audience, attributes, sentence, competitor gaps, what is out of scope.
6. Share the direction *before* presenting (small steps toward the goal: nobody is surprised). Keep scope on a straight line and pull exploration back when it drifts.
7. Hand the attributes and persona to the visual roles as written criteria (`art-director`).

## Preparing yourself
- Build enough business and marketing literacy to ask good questions and to recognise weak answers: conversion, funnels, what a quoted figure means against its benchmark.
- A facilitator with broad knowledge stays unbiased; someone with a stake in one answer does not.
- Bias routine: treat all data equally, argue the opposite side, be most sceptical when everyone agrees.
- Admit when you do not understand the client's jargon and ask for plain words.

## Startup stages (service fit)
Pre-funding: investor narrative and a first brand definition. Funded: help the CEO communicate the vision, prioritise iterations. Mature or legacy: strategic framework, executive facilitation, ROI language for marketing leadership. Across stages the job is to reduce the differences between stakeholders, not to make things pretty; give the client a reusable template so their team can carry on.

## Common Mistakes
- Designing from intuition or personal taste without knowing the audience.
- Accepting the client's numbers and claims unchecked.
- Vague adjectives with no reference.
- Presenting visuals before insights exist.
- Treating the client as a necessary evil or the customer as a nuisance: find the role that makes the customer the hero.
- Treating every inquiry as a hot lead; choosing a comfortable lifestyle business by default without checking the long-term goal.

## Checklist
- [ ] Success defined (numbers where possible) before any work?
- [ ] Root problem separated from the requested deliverable?
- [ ] Each vague adjective backed by an example?
- [ ] Persona named, ranked needs, marked as hypothesis?
- [ ] Attribute pair and positioning sentence agreed with the client?
- [ ] Competitor clutter and gaps noted?
- [ ] Direction shared before the reveal?
