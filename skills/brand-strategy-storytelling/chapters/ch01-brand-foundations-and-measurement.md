# Chapter 01: Brand Foundations & Measurement

## Core Idea
A brand is the gut feeling and reputation that forms in each customer's head; it is the *result* of everything an organisation does, not any single thing it makes. Because it is a result, you cannot declare it, only earn it, and you can measure it. The logo is a container that collects meaning over years; the associations do the work, not the artwork. Know which of the three layers you are actually working on and promise only that.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The three layers
| Layer | What it is | Who owns it |
|---|---|---|
| **Identity design** | Symbols: mark, type, colour, system | Designers |
| **Branding** | Strategy, positioning, messaging, experience, how the promise is articulated across channels | Strategist plus leadership |
| **The brand** | What people feel and say when you are not in the room | Customers and society, shaped by the whole organisation |

- Every customer holds a slightly different version; manage the *dominant* version. When more than half of an audience shares a feeling, that feeling is the brand.
- A designer can influence the brand as a long-term strategic partner, as a founder or owner, or as a senior in-house role with real decision power. A one-off logo-and-site job is identity work, and calling it "branding" over-promises.

### Brand ladder (what to measure)
Four rungs, bottom to top:
1. **Satisfaction**: it did what it promised. A low bar.
2. **Delight**: better than expected; the customer tells others.
3. **Engagement**: love and belonging. Signs: automatic repurchase, buying new launches without evaluating them, emotional attachment, sense of belonging.
4. **Empowerment**: the brand is part of the customer's identity; they would feel a loss without it. A community check: does it help members with personal growth, emotional support, business success, social status, fulfilment?

How to measure: survey customers every year with the same questions, sort them onto the rungs, and track the *share at the top*. Compare year over year. The aim is to move people up, not to keep them satisfied. The business translation: engagement leads to loyalty, loyalty to profit; branding means more people buying more, more often, for more years, at a higher price.

### Brand platform (the plan that comes before a name or a logo)
Why we exist (a one-line "because" statement) · what we stand for · how we differ · how we position · who the customers are and where they spend time · a **verbal toolbox** (words, tone, how we want to be seen). Include sales and marketing in making it, not as a hand-off afterwards.

### Branding vs marketing
Branding defines why, look and feel, and position; marketing carries the message to buyers. They overlap heavily once the organisation is run around customer outcomes. Positioning (occupying a place in the customer's mind) is done by both. Rough starting rule used in practice: about 30 percent of effort on establishing the brand and about 70 percent on getting the word out; treat it as a prompt to check balance, not a law.

### Internal first
A mission, vision and values statement serves staff; the public judges the outcome. Staff who do not live the brand waste external spend. Purpose sets the direction and profit is the outcome. Vague purpose statements without a measure ("transform X") are noise: ask what impact the organisation makes, not only what it sells.

## How to Apply (step by step)
1. **State the layer** you are hired for: identity, branding, or brand-level advice. Write it in the proposal.
2. **Define the target feeling** in one sentence: what should a customer say about this brand when you are not there?
3. **Work backwards**: list the touchpoints that create that feeling (product, messaging, look and feel, service, culture, how employees behave) and mark which you can influence.
4. **Write the brand platform** (above) with the client; keep the verbal toolbox for ch04.
5. **Pick one ladder measure** (a yearly four-question survey is enough) so "the brand works" has evidence.
6. **Check the promise against behaviour**: for each promise, name the behaviour that delivers it. A slogan the company cannot deliver is a false brand.
7. **Translate to business terms** (revenue, retention, new markets) when talking to the client; do not argue taste.

## Short Examples
- A bakery's promise is "always warm". The ladder survey shows 70 percent satisfied, 12 percent engaged. The action is not a new logo: it is the loyalty detail (name remembered, first-batch alert) that creates delight.
- Client says "we want a brand". Reply: "Which feeling do you want customers to have, and which behaviours today produce it?" The answer scopes the whole project.

## Common Mistakes
- Equating brand with logo, tagline, or a checklist of deliverables that is "done" at delivery.
- Claiming "we build brands" after a logo and a website; calling yourself a brand strategist with no strategy work behind it.
- Treating satisfaction scores as proof of brand strength.
- A promise that behaviour contradicts: the gap is more damaging than a modest promise.
- Declaring a "brand mindset" without the customer agreeing. Repeated bad behaviour becomes the identity.
- Arguing aesthetics ("that looks dated") with business clients instead of outcomes.

## Checklist
- [ ] Is the layer I am working on (identity, branding, brand) stated and agreed?
- [ ] Is the target feeling written as a customer sentence?
- [ ] Does every promise map to a behaviour that delivers it?
- [ ] Is there a yearly, repeatable ladder measure?
- [ ] Are sales and marketing in the room for the platform?
- [ ] Is the brand platform free of buzzwords with no measure?
