# Chapter 03: Positioning, Differentiation & Niche

## Core Idea
Positioning is the one claim you own in a market: who the offer is for and what they get. It is a decision to include and to exclude. The claim comes first, proof is built afterwards, and difference (not superiority) is what earns attention and a premium. Narrow enough that a stranger can repeat it in one sentence; narrow enough that you could be number one within a year or two.

Applies to a client brand, a studio, or a person. Perceptual maps, segmentation criteria and white space for large markets are in `marketing-mba` ch02; this chapter adds the *method for choosing and owning* a position, especially for small practices.

## Frameworks Introduced

### 1. The claim formula
"[Offer] for [specific group] so that [result], without [barrier]." Examples of shape: "design for successful CEOs" beats a list of disciplines; "a result for a group, without the barrier that stops them" is the compact form.
- Say it first, then build the proof. A slightly aspirational flag planted early is fine if the skills, capabilities and processes to prove it follow quickly.
- Say it and stick to it; reposition deliberately when the market changes, not on a whim.
- A portfolio with work but no stated claim leaves the visitor to guess what to buy.

### 2. Choose the position: focus = discipline x market
1. **Choose a focus**: what you do and for whom.
2. **Articulate** it as a claim of expertise.
3. **Build** the proof and the method.
4. **Codify** the method into a repeatable process: late-stage buyers fear failure, so a visible method reduces perceived risk. Early in the buying cycle, great work inspires; late in it, process reassures.

### 3. Three-circle fit (for a person or small studio)
Love / good at / pays well. The centre is the position.
- love + good, no money = happy but poor
- good + pays, no love = rich but bored
- love + pays, not good = a dreamer
Add **industry fit**: the same skill sold to a buyer who values and can fund it (a larger, better-funded sector instead of a budget-limited one). Add **access routes**: the places where those buyers spend time (professional circles, trade events, local business lists, suppliers to large companies).

### 4. Wedge
Pick one specialty to claim; adjacent disciplines can sit around it. Specialise externally (what the market can name), diversify internally (curiosity across fields, the T-shape: depth in one thing, breadth for cross-pollination).

### 5. Narrow by forced choice
Keep asking concrete either/or questions until the audience is specific: men or women, older or younger, single or married, employed or founding. Each answer excludes someone; the message gets clearer with each exclusion.
- **Niche test**: can you pick one of them out of a crowd and describe their challenges? If not, the audience is still too broad.
- **Smallest viable audience**: the narrowest group you can picture clearly, with their problems. Start from their problem and design the offer for it, not the reverse.
- **Add one dimension** (topic + audience, or topic + region) to become number one in a sub-niche.
- **Ownership test**: can you become the top name in this lane within one to two years? If not, pick another lane. (Practitioners note that the leading player in a market is often several times the size of the runner-up, so a lane you can lead beats a bigger lane you cannot.)
- **Competition read**: many competitors usually signal demand; no competitors and no sales usually signal no demand.

### 6. Good-and-different matrix
| | Not different | Different |
|---|---|---|
| **Not good** | Knock-off | Gimmick |
| **Good** | Commodity | **Target** |
Good is the entry ticket; difference commands the premium. Methods to find difference:
- **Inversion**: brainstorm the opposite or "dumbest" version of how the category does it; one reframing of a tutoring service around confidence rather than grades rebuilt its whole message.
- **Change about 3 percent, not everything**: to reinvent something, alter one element the category treats as fixed.
- **Play against type**: list what the category does and differ by one degree.
- **Hybrid**: combine two skill sets (for example creative plus business) and keep the strengths of both.
- **Reframe the offer by function, not label**: sell the problem solved, not "a logo" or "branding"; describe what the work does.
- **Commodity ladder**: the same raw commodity moved from raw to prepared to branded packaged product commands step-changes in price. A commodity can be differentiated.
- **Brand assets** to make difference visible: name, voice, identity, packaging. Deliberate appearance signals before words are read.

### 7. Brand core: one word, one enemy, one rallying cry
Three connected elements; the brand sits where they overlap.
1. The **one thing** you want to be known for, ideally one word.
2. The **enemy** you want to eradicate: a practice, a belief, a way of doing things (not a named person).
3. The **rallying cry** that links the two and activates the audience.
Compel and repel: a point of view that repels some attracts the right people. Have the courage to be disliked.

### 8. Attribute slider: plot your brand against two competitors
A fast test that a position is really different.
1. For each brand attribute write two opposite poles ("epic vs intimate", "affordable vs luxury").
2. Force a choice on every slider; no centre marks.
3. Plot your brand and your two main competitors on the same sliders.
4. If your plot matches a competitor's, the position is not yet different: redo it, taking an extreme position on at least one attribute.
- Avoid the middle on every axis; the middle describes the whole category and says nothing.
- If two competitors plot almost identically, the only differentiator left is the packaging or presentation, so make the difference visible there (ch05 section 8).
- "For everyone" is not a position; it is the absence of one.
- Niching with an AI assistant: after the binary narrowing in section 5, ask it to draft an ideal customer profile, telling it to ask clarifying questions first, then to rank that customer's problems by pain and by willingness to pay. Treat the draft as a hypothesis to test with real buyers (ch02).

## Where teachers disagree
- **Narrow specialist vs hybrid/T-shaped.** Narrow works best when the market is large and generalists compete on price; hybrid works when the combination itself is rare and nameable. In both cases the market must be able to *name* you in a few words; "multidisciplinary" with no nameable specialty fails.
- **"Different, not better" vs "good first."** Both agree difference earns the premium; the matrix reconciles them: ignore a gimmick, fix a commodity.
- **Specialising threatens nobody vs specialists draw competitors.** Generalists do not react to a newcomer; specialists attract attention and competitor response. Plan proof accordingly.

## How to Apply (step by step)
1. Run the three-circle fit and list at least three candidate focuses.
2. For each, run the niche test and the ownership test; drop those that fail.
3. Draft the claim formula; write 15 versions, keep the best, then reduce to three short phrases (ch04).
4. Place the claim in the good-and-different matrix; if "commodity", apply inversion or the 3 percent change.
5. Define the brand core (word, enemy, cry) if the brand will carry a stance.
6. List the proof you need in the next 90 days and the method you will codify.
7. Test it: say it aloud to five people in the target group; ask them to repeat it back.

## Common Mistakes
- Trying to do everything for everybody; the message bounces off the market.
- Claiming a title (strategist, storyteller, expert) with no proof behind it.
- Waiting until fully ready before making the claim.
- Positioning as "a bit of everything" because it feels safer; avoiding the decision to exclude.
- Describing what you do (job title, tools) instead of what the buyer gets or who you are.
- Dressing in the generic uniform that signals "I'm like everyone else".
- Copying a competitor's voice or relying on a generated imitation of it.

## Checklist
- [ ] Plotted against two competitors on forced-choice sliders, with at least one extreme and a different shape?
- [ ] Claim states who and what result in one sentence?
- [ ] Passes the niche test (can you spot them in a crowd)?
- [ ] Passes the ownership test (number one in 12-24 months)?
- [ ] Sits in "good and different", not commodity or gimmick?
- [ ] A named proof and a codified method are planned?
- [ ] A stranger repeated the claim back correctly?
