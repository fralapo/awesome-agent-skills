# Chapter 04: Brand Voice & Messaging

## Core Idea
A brand that speaks with one consistent, human point of view connects better than a corporate voice that tries to represent everyone and ends up saying nothing. Voice starts with *words and beliefs*, not visuals; it is organised in layers so each line has exactly one job; and short brand lines work as hooks that start a conversation, not as full descriptions. Headline craft and scoring belong to `creative-director`; this chapter covers the voice, the messaging structure and the short-line techniques that sit around it.

## Frameworks Introduced

### 1. Brand point of view (POV)
- Write as a real person would: state what you think, not what a company should say.
- A stance, even a combative one, is what makes a brand relevant in a category where everyone sounds alike. A **brand persona** (a consistent character with an opinion and a voice) is one way to carry it.
- A **second voice channel** lets a team experiment with tone without diluting the main one; once it has a voice it can carry its own design language.
- Keep a shared perspective across the team; one person's personality excludes the others.
- **Dilution watch**: as the organisation grows, committees turn the voice into safe, correct copy. Audit periodically: does a stranger still recognise this in two lines without the logo?
- A channel with a voice but no perspective becomes a vehicle for messages nobody believes.

### 2. Voice-building sequence
1. Define the target audience (from the client or your research).
2. **Listen**: read what customers say about the brand or the category in comments, forums and social posts, not only in product reviews.
3. Collect words; find the one well-chosen word that is visual, witty or precise.
4. Condense into **one honest paragraph** of beliefs, not buzzwords.
5. **Tone filter**: run dull functional copy (opening hours, a delivery note) through several tones (witty, romantic, sarcastic, understated) and compare. Write one sentence in five or six tones and keep the one that fits the target.
6. Turn the chosen tone of voice into direction for the visual roles (`art-director`).
- Voice consistency matters more than visual consistency across channels.
- Plain words: short, concrete, understandable by a child; translate jargon the buyer never uses.

### 3. Messaging layers (each line has one job)
| Layer | Job |
|---|---|
| Hook / headline | Get the reader to the subhead. Do not pitch in the hook |
| Subhead | Get them to the body |
| Body | Prove and persuade; carries **one** call to action |
| Call to action | One specific step |
- **Three-phrase positioning**: draft the same line about 15 times, choose the best, then reduce it to three short phrases that can be repeated everywhere.
- **Gap framing**: name the two sides of the gap your offer bridges (goals to results, strategy to execution) and write the copy across the bridge.
- Open with the reader's goal, not your history; replace vague self-praise ("award-winning", "trust us") with a question that shows you understand.

### 4. Short brand line techniques (a two-word line is a hook)
- Look for a **noun that is your truth**, not two adjectives. Pair it with a claim of mastery ("world-class" plus an everyday activity no one celebrates).
- The hook should make people ask "tell me more"; do not follow it with a monologue.
- **Known-unknown twist**: take a familiar phrase and change one component (a word, a letter, the word order). "Less is more" becomes "mess is more" for a maximalist brand. A cliche is bankrupt; a cliche with one small change is an asset. Change one thing per attempt.
- **Concept borrowing**: take a well-known film line or concept and change one element so people instantly get the idea ("X whisperer" for a new service).
- Avoid two-adjective positioning ("quietly fierce") and unchanged cliches, which trigger eye-rolls.

### 5. One- or two-word descriptor (people and small brands)
Reduce identity to one memorable word, or a **trait plus a role**. One word is ideal, two the next best. It sets the tone for how people work with you, makes you easy to introduce, and gives the audience something to repeat. Own the quirk that makes you unusual before others label it against you. Add a short **rhythmic catchphrase** (alliteration, repetition, rhyme) for recall. See ch06 for the personal-brand use.

### 6. Naming and domain rules
Name criteria: short, easy to spell, to say and to remember, and something the owner can stand behind. A name can be **self-selecting**: the audience either recognises itself or does not.
Four strategies and trade-offs:
1. **Brand/business name**: professional and scalable; no keyword help.
2. **Personal name**: free if unique, natural for freelancers; common names are taken; can make an agency look small.
3. **Localised keyword** (city + service): helps local search; can read as a cheap service.
4. **Name + new domain extension**: gives context and is often available; people still expect the classic extension; longer addresses.
A workable mix: use one as the primary, register the common variants and redirect them.
Availability check in order: used in your industry? domain parked or in use? newer extensions free?
Avoid dashes, numbers, special characters, deliberate misspellings, acronyms, long names, and words that spell something unintended when run together.
Tests: write the email address four times; say the name to ten people and watch how they type it.

## How to Apply (step by step)
1. Write the one-paragraph belief statement from customer listening.
2. Run the tone filter on three boring sentences; choose the tone; write three do/don't rules.
3. Write the three-phrase positioning from ch03's claim.
4. Draft the hook with a known-unknown twist; give it the "tell me more" test.
5. Lay out the page or post as hook > subhead > body > one CTA.
6. Check names with the typing and speaking tests.
7. Schedule a six-monthly dilution audit.

## Common Mistakes
- Committee-written content: inclusive, correct, and without a point of view.
- Insider jargon the buyer does not use.
- Trying to satisfy the old site and the new direction in one positioning line.
- Pitching in the hook; explaining everything in the first line.
- Several calls to action in one piece.
- Starting from logo or colour; fluffy strategy statements full of jargon.
- A slogan the company cannot deliver.

## Checklist
- [ ] A belief paragraph exists, built from real customer language?
- [ ] Tone chosen by comparison, with do/don't rules?
- [ ] Each layer has one job and the page has one CTA?
- [ ] The brand line makes someone ask "tell me more"?
- [ ] Name passes the spelling, speaking and email tests?
- [ ] Voice would still be recognisable without the logo?
