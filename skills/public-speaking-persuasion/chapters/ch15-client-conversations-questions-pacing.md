# Chapter 15: Client Conversations: Questions, Pacing and Fit

## Core Idea
In a client or sales conversation the strongest move is rarely a clever answer. It is a good question, a deliberate pause and an honest "I do not know yet". Framing the meeting as a mutual check of fit moves the pressure from the speaker to the listener, and makes the speaker calmer and more useful.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Fit-Assessment Frame**: treat the meeting as "are we a good fit for each other?" rather than an audition.
  - When to use: pitches, discovery calls, consultancy and first meetings with a prospect.
  - How: (1) frame the meeting as a fit check at the start; (2) ask about goals, challenges and what success looks like in a year; (3) show value through the quality of your questions, not only through samples of past work; (4) close by agreeing next steps and a follow-up date.
  - Why it works: when you ask and they answer, the pressure shifts to them, and you learn the real need instead of guessing it.
- **PROACT Decision Frame**: five questions to uncover and frame a problem before proposing anything.
  1. **Problem** - what is the real problem, not the stated one?
  2. **Objective** - what result is wanted, and how will success be measured?
  3. **Alternatives** - what else could be done, from cheap to expensive?
  4. **Consequences** - what happens with each alternative (time, money, reputation)?
  5. **Trade-offs** - what do we say no to if we say yes?
  - When to use: discovery, scoping, any decision with several possible routes.
- **3-Lesson Confidence Sequence** (in order): (1) **Slow down**: speak in short bursts without filler words and pause before replying. (2) **You do not need all the answers**: say you are still processing and will come back with a view. (3) **Be genuinely curious**: answer a question with a question that digs into what was just said.

## Rules for a Good Question
- Open-ended, neutral (not leading), clear and direct, concise, one question at a time (no stacked sub-questions).
- Avoid binary questions ("personal or business?") and leading questions: they close the answer.
- A good question frames the problem and does much of the work of the answer.
- Basic questions that pay off: what do you want to accomplish, how will you measure success, what inspired this, what has failed before, what has worked, who else decides.
- In workshops, ask clear questions with many possible answers so people contribute rather than being steered.
- Listen to the answer. Do not run through a checklist while the other person is talking.

## Key Concepts
- **Validation need**: wanting the client's approval makes you weaker; the less you need the job, the more honest and relaxed you can be. Being ready to walk away calms the nerves.
- **Prepare the story, discover the need**: prepare what you want to tell, but let the meeting reveal the real requirements.
- **Count-to-three pause**: wait about three seconds (or more) after someone finishes before replying. The silence lets them finish the thought and often deepens what they say.
- **Transparent processing**: "I would like to collect my thoughts before we move on" is accepted and makes the room safer.
- **Stories over data**: people remember and relate to stories; use data to support them.
- **Remote calls**: body language is less visible, so put the effort into listening.

## Mental Models
- The other person is a thinker to empower, not a bottleneck to answer.
- Nerves often come from impostor feelings and the urge to win approval. Name them, then reframe the meeting as a fit check.
- Curiosity moves the conversation away from you and onto them.

## Anti-patterns
- Answering with ideas before understanding the problem: wastes time and erodes trust.
- Treating the portfolio as a verdict on your worth, and the client as a judge to impress.
- Entering with all the answers and selling one idea hard.
- Filling silence with empty responses ("that's cool") or answering instantly under pressure and regretting it.
- Binary, leading or loaded questions.

## Key Takeaways
1. Reframe the meeting as mutual fit; ask, then listen.
2. Frame the problem with PROACT before proposing alternatives.
3. Good questions are open, neutral, short and single.
4. Pause about three seconds before replying; say plainly when you need time to think.
5. Answer a question with a curious question that digs into the answer just given.
