# Chapter 16: Handling Criticism, Giving Feedback and Reading Influence

## Core Idea
Three situations test a speaker after the talk is prepared: answering a hostile challenge in public, giving someone critical feedback so that they can use it, and noticing when someone else is persuading you by pressure instead of reasons. In this model all three rest on the same skill: slow the exchange down, find what is true in the other position, and move the conversation to a principle or a purpose before reacting. The chapter also holds a seven-step talk structure for a persuasive presentation and a short defensive-literacy catalogue of influence techniques. Everything here is a practitioner's model, not a measured rule; use it ethically and to protect yourself, not to manipulate.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Hostile-challenge sequence**: a reply pattern for a sharp public question or criticism.
  1. **Pause** before answering; do not let the tone set yours.
  2. **Find common ground** with the questioner.
  3. **Concede what is right** in the criticism, explicitly.
  4. **Move to the principle**: start from the experience of the person you serve, not from the technology or from the defence of your own product.
  5. **Admit errors** plainly when they exist.
  6. **Show, do not only tell**: a demonstration or evidence beats a promise.
  7. **Build a coalition**: bring in others who share the goal.
  - When to use: Q&A after a pitch, a client review, an online pile-on.
  - Related: Conversation Aikido (ch10); hostile audiences (ch09).
- **Constructive feedback to a person** (about nine moves in the source; six core ones):
  1. A specific, honest compliment first.
  2. Make the role and its expectations clear.
  3. Describe the behaviour, not the person.
  4. State the aspiration the behaviour is blocking.
  5. Invite the person to analyse themselves, in private.
  6. Pull the opposite lever (what would help more of what you want) instead of only pushing against the fault.
  - Rule: people change only if they want to; feedback offers a reason to want it.
  - Related: leadership communication (ch14).
- **Seven-step persuasive presentation**:
  1. Who I am and whom I help.
  2. The problem the audience already recognises.
  3. "Me too": my story, including failures.
  4. Old way versus new way.
  5. The framework.
  6. Three things to do and three not to do.
  7. A call to action.
  - Alternatives: Problem-Solution-Value; What / So what / Now what; past / present / future.
  - Position: opening with a list of product features weakens the talk; open with the audience's problem.
  - Extras: one message; little text per slide; the rule of three; a surprising anecdote; a prop; some audience participation; finish early.
  - Related: speech structure (ch07), calls to action (ch13).
- **Trust inversion**: instead of "why you should buy from me", ask "what are your priorities, and what would make this worth it for you?". Suspend judgement, give the other person real choice, do not push. People rarely change their mind on facts alone, because social convenience and mental energy weigh on the decision.
  - Related: client conversations (ch15), cognitive biases (ch05).

## Key Concepts
- **Expectation effect**: authority figures' expectations shape the performance of the people they lead. High expectations tend to lift results (the Pygmalion effect), low ones to depress them (the Golem effect). See `ux-ui-expert` ch11.
- **Difficult people**: you do not have to get along; acknowledge your own contribution to the friction, then agree on working rules.
- **Body language and trust**: routine, gaze and breath convey confidence or intimidation; confidence is not rigidity. Control of space and time is part of presence (ch06).
- **Three basic communication errors**: ignoring the audience, complicating (use the bridge technique: connect the new idea to something they already know) and talking only about yourself.
- **Defensive literacy**: knowing the techniques below lets you notice them in others; it does not make them acceptable to use.

## Reading Influence Techniques (defensive catalogue)
**Pressure patterns attributed to cults and charlatans (read as warning signs)**:
- Staged authority and credentials.
- A control architecture around the follower (information, time, people).
- A "loss leader" of shared principles that everyone accepts, followed by larger demands.
- Consistency bias: small commitments used to demand larger ones.
- "Us versus them" framing.
- Exhaustion that lowers critical resistance.

**Rhetorical moves seen in public speech (a case-study set, not a political judgement)**:
- Repetition of slogans; adopting phrases the audience uses.
- Baseline setting: an extreme first claim that makes the next one sound moderate.
- "Many people say": an unnamed crowd as evidence.
- Insinuation: a hint with no claim to defend.
- Discrediting the channel that carries criticism.
- Fear, followed by simple, basic language and an appeal to normality.
- Double speak: words whose meaning slides depending on the audience.
- A benevolent opening and a joke to win goodwill before the harder message.

**Data-driven persuasion**: personal traits can be inferred from small behavioural traces; messages can then be personalised and tested (A/B tests, retargeting). The risk is mass personalisation with little visibility to the target. Distinguish an improper use of data from an infrastructure hack; they are different problems.

**Defence**: slow down, ask for the claim and its source, check who benefits, and separate how a message feels from what it asserts (ch04 fallacies, ch05 biases).

## Mental Models
- Concede first, argue second: agreement on the true part makes the rest hearable.
- Principles outlast points: a reply built on a principle can be repeated; a reply built on a rebuttal cannot.
- Feedback is a gift only if the receiver can use it.
- A technique you can name loses power over you.

## Anti-patterns
- Answering criticism with a counter-attack or a defensive spec list.
- Public feedback for a private problem; feedback about the person rather than the behaviour.
- A talk that opens with features.
- Using the techniques above on an audience; they remain warning signs, not a toolkit.

## Key Takeaways
1. Pause, find common ground, concede the true part, then move to a principle.
2. Give feedback privately, about behaviour, tied to the person's own aspiration.
3. Open a persuasive talk with the audience's problem and your own story, not with features.
4. Ask about priorities before pitching; give real choice.
5. Learn the pressure patterns to protect yourself.
