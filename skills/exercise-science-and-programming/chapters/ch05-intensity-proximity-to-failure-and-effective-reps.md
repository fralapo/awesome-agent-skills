# Chapter 05: Intensity, Proximity to Failure and Effective Reps

> Educational, not medical or coaching advice. Effort advice here is about stimulus and fatigue; if a set causes joint pain, stop and see a qualified professional.

## Core Idea
In this model, two different things get called "intensity": how *heavy* the load is (percentage of a one-rep max) and how *hard* the set is (how close to failure). Growth depends mostly on the second, within a very wide range of the first. Positions differ on how close to failure the optimum is.

## 5.1 Load and rep range: very wide, with edges
- **Consensus**: when sets are taken close to failure, loads from roughly 30% to 85% of a one-rep max (about 5-30+ reps) produce similar average hypertrophy; the traditional "6-12 rep zone" is a convenience, not a physiological boundary. For maximal strength, heavier loads (roughly 65-70% of a max and above) tend to give larger gains; light loads give a smaller strength gain.
- **Edges**: below about 30% of a max, results depend on how the studies are read (three studies are usually cited; one used 15% with and without blood-flow restriction and found 70% better; another matched volume load and found 80% better than 20%, though it is unclear whether set count or load explained it; a third found 30% and 80% equal). Some practitioners think the true lower threshold may be somewhat below 30%, but evidence is thin. Sets of 20% of a max reach an average of dozens of reps, which is impractical.
- **Practical split (position)**: most work in about 6-15 reps, smaller blocks of heavier sets for strength skill and lighter sets for variety. Reasons given: under about five reps, joint strain per unit of muscle stimulus rises and volume is harder to accumulate; over about 12-15, sets are more systemically fatiguing and uncomfortable (position; one data point is that heavy low-rep sets taken away from failure can be less acutely fatiguing than higher-rep sets to failure).
- **Individual differences**: on average 10 and 30 reps give similar growth, but that does not mean everyone responds the same. Within-person comparisons (one limb light, one heavy) show some people gaining much more with one approach than the other, though measurement error exaggerates differences (see [ch01](ch01-reasoning-about-training-evidence.md), 1.2).
- **Does not hold when**: effort is low; then rep range matters, because a set of 30 far from failure is not stimulating.

## 5.2 How close to failure? (contested)
- **Definitions**: *momentary (volitional) failure* is the point where you cannot complete another rep despite your best effort. *Technical failure* is when technique breaks down; it is safer and more reproducible. *Reps in reserve (RIR)* counts how many you could still do; *RPE* rates difficulty from 1 to 10, with 10 = no reps left (so RPE 9 = RIR 1). Both scales descend from category-ratio effort scales used in endurance exercise; they are less accurate with high-rep sets and for beginners (the latter is general knowledge).
- **Position A (close, not necessarily at failure)**: the mainstream view is that sets should usually stop within about 0-3 reps of failure (some say up to 4); more reps in reserve are reasonable when recovering or training for strength.
- **Position B (to failure is better or safer)**: failure guarantees the set was hard, corrects for poorly chosen loads, and might help very advanced trainees who no longer respond to anything less. Reasons people give for going to failure also include culture, ego and "everyone big does it" (position: confounded by genetics, drugs and years of training).
- **Position C (failure is overrated)**: controlled trials in beginners suggest a few reps in reserve and failure are similar, partly because beginners misjudge their reserve (what they call failure may be 3 RIR and what they call 6 RIR may be 3). Costs of failure: more fatigue per set, slower recovery, lower performance in later sets and sessions, more joint irritation in some people; failure on single-joint movements is cheaper than on heavy compound lifts.
- **What research actually shows**: a broad meta-regression that treated proximity to failure as a continuous variable (with subjective estimation of RIR for each study) found closer to failure better for size, more sets better, and a weaker need to be close when loads are heavier. It has been cited by both camps; its authors stress that the estimates are rough and that there is no precise optimum.
- **Practical heuristics**: stay within about 0-3 RIR on most working sets; end isolation and machine sets nearer to failure; keep more reserve on heavy compound lifts and when fatigue is high; use failure sparingly and as a calibration tool, not a default (position; mainstream, not unanimous).
- **Does not hold when**: you are a beginner (use "easy / medium / hard" with a bar-speed or form limit before RPE), or you have specific reasons to test limits (a calibration set with a spotter).

## 5.3 The "effective reps" model (contested)
- **Claim**: only the last few reps (often "about five") before failure count as stimulating, because they combine full recruitment of high-threshold motor units and slow enough bar speed for high tension per fibre; reps earlier in the set are "warm-up" reps for the purpose of growth.
- **Mechanism offered**: size principle plus force-velocity (slow shortening allows more cross-bridges and more tension per fibre); evidence for the recruitment part includes isometric work showing recruitment mostly complete near 80-85% of maximal force, with increases after that from firing rate, and surface EMG studies.
- **Implications**: four sets to failure = about 20 effective reps; four sets one rep short = 16; a set ending 3 reps before failure would need roughly double the sets to match; fatigue is not accounted for.
- **Counter-arguments**: critics of the model stress that it presents a precise, tidy number ("the last five reps count equally") on top of imperfect proxies; that the mechanistic pieces (recruitment, tension) are real but do not by themselves prove the exact cutoff or the equal weighting of the last reps; that studies on proximity to failure rely on estimated reserve, which is subjective; that a broad meta-regression on proximity to failure and size (cited by supporters of both camps) described a dose-response gradient with interacting factors such as load rather than a clean cutoff (details unclear in source); and that the model says nothing about the cost of failure. A softer version ("you need to be fairly close, roughly four or five reps from failure, for it to count") is widely accepted because it matches the consensus in 5.2.
- **Poll check**: practitioners are split on whether the model is valid.
- **Does not hold when**: you are using it to claim that a long set with many reps in reserve is "junk" for all purposes; sets with 5+ RIR can build or maintain strength and technique even if they add little hypertrophy (general knowledge).

## 5.4 Practical pairing of effort and volume
- Harder sets reduce the volume you can recover from; easier sets raise it. This trade is why failure policies and volume targets must be chosen together (see [ch03](ch03-volume.md)).
- A block-level pattern (position): begin at about 3 RIR and move to 0-1 RIR over a mesocycle as volume rises (see [ch08](ch08-periodization-and-deloads.md)).
- A very low-effort, casual programme (several sets of ten-ish reps with plenty left in the tank) is a valid choice if goals are modest or time is scarce; it simply gives less stimulus than the above.

## 5.5 Skill and impact work: leave a buffer (position)
- **Thesis (position)**: when the activity is technical or lands with impact (jumps, vaults, landings, balance and handstand skills), failure is a poor target: leave a reserve and stop while quality is still high.
- **Mechanism**: at failure the technique degrades, and a degraded landing or catch costs more than a degraded curl; a buffer relative to the maximum keeps each rep a clean practice of the skill. A common practitioner framing is a "perceived" recovery check (could I repeat the quality of my best reps?) rather than counting to failure. Strong muscles are said to help protect joints that take repeated impact, which is why athletes in technical sports also do strength work (position; the protective effect is plausible but not shown in the sources).
- **Trade-off**: less stimulus per set than near-failure work; more reliable quality and fewer sessions lost.
- **Does not hold when**: the set is a stable, low-skill movement where failure is safe (supported push-ups, machine work); there, see 5.2.
