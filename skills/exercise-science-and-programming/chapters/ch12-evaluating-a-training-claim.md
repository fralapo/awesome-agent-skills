# Chapter 12: Evaluating a Training Claim (Checklist)

> Educational, not medical or coaching advice. This is a reasoning tool for judging advice; it does not tell any individual what to do. For pain, injury, illness or medication, see a qualified professional (and `biomechanics-and-rehab-reasoning` for mechanics-and-rehab reasoning).

## Core Idea
In this model, a good answer to "should I do X?" states what the claim is, what kind of evidence supports it, how big the effect is, who it was measured in, what it costs, and how you would know if it worked for you. Most disagreements are about the second and fourth questions.

## 12.1 The checklist
1. **What exactly is claimed?** Rewrite it as "for [population], doing [A] instead of [B] changes [outcome] by about [size] over [time]". If you cannot, the claim is vague.
2. **Which outcome?** Muscle size (and how measured), strength (in which lift), performance, health, or "feeling" are different. A result on one does not transfer to another.
3. **Which population?** Untrained students, trained lifters, athletes, older adults? Most trials use untrained or lightly trained people over weeks to a few months ([ch01](ch01-reasoning-about-training-evidence.md)).
4. **What kind of evidence?** Mechanistic, animal, observational, small trial, trials with equated volume, meta-analysis, practitioner experience. State the type and its blind spot.
5. **Equal on what?** Were sets, volume load, time, effort or frequency matched? "Equal volume" removes the benefit that some options exist to deliver (for example, frequency).
6. **Average or individual?** "No difference" is a group mean; did individuals differ ([ch01](ch01-reasoning-about-training-evidence.md), 1.2)?
7. **How was effort defined?** "To failure" and "RIR" are defined differently; estimates may be subjective ([ch05](ch05-intensity-proximity-to-failure-and-effective-reps.md)).
8. **What does it cost?** Time, fatigue, joint stress, complexity, adherence. A theoretically optimal plan you will not follow is not optimal.
9. **Is it a precise number built on proxies?** "Exactly five effective reps", "exactly 12 weekly sets", "exactly one deload every four weeks" deserve suspicion; ask what the confidence range is.
10. **Is the person selling something?** Fear (posture), certainty (a method for everyone) and novelty (a secret) are red flags; also check who benefits.
11. **What would disconfirm it for me?** Define a test: log baseline, run the change for several weeks with controlled variables, compare like with like ([ch07](ch07-rest-tempo-and-progression.md), 7.7).
12. **Is it reversible and low-risk?** If yes, trying it is cheap; if not, demand better evidence and professional advice.

## 12.2 Consensus versus contested (summary, with dated caveats)
| Topic | Consensus | Contested |
|---|---|---|
| Progressive overload | needed; logging helps | which progression scheme is best |
| Volume | roughly 10+ hard sets per muscle per week beats fewer for most people ([ch03](ch03-volume.md)) | whether returns end near 20 sets, whether more can harm |
| Frequency | at least twice weekly is at least as good as once; beyond that small with equal volume ([ch04](ch04-frequency.md)) | whether high frequency helps trained lifters; real-world value |
| Effort | hard sets near failure (about 0-3 RIR) work; very light, far-from-failure sets do not | whether failure is better or worse; the effective-reps model ([ch05](ch05-intensity-proximity-to-failure-and-effective-reps.md)) |
| Load / reps | wide range (about 30-85% of a max) builds muscle near failure | edges below 30%; practical ranges |
| Rest | under about a minute likely costs growth if sets are equal; 2-3 minutes fine ([ch07](ch07-rest-tempo-and-progression.md)) | whether extra sets fully compensate |
| Tempo | wide acceptable range; extremely slow reps are worse | optimal specific tempo |
| Range of motion | full or near-full range most of the time ([ch06](ch06-exercise-selection-specificity-and-range-of-motion.md)) | partial or lengthened-partial techniques |
| Periodization | useful for peaking; some benefit for strength | whether a named method matters for size ([ch08](ch08-periodization-and-deloads.md)) |
| Deloads | fatigue management is useful | scheduled versus reactive |
| Sleep | 7-8+ hours helps ([ch09](ch09-fatigue-recovery-sleep-and-overtraining.md)) | exact amounts for performance |
| "Perfect form" | technique is a skill; progress gradually | whether posture protects from injury ([ch10](ch10-mechanics-of-the-big-lifts-and-when-form-matters.md)) |
| Novice/advanced | beginners progress faster | whether early gains are largely neural (commonly proposed; contested) and whether categories are biological ([ch11](ch11-novices-advanced-trainees-strength-vs-size-and-cardio.md)) |
| Interference | separate sessions reduce it | magnitude in trained people |

## 12.3 Using the checklist in an answer
- State the default, then the exceptions and *why* they exist.
- Present each contested position with its evidence and counter-argument; say when you are giving a position rather than a consensus.
- Offer a test the person can run, not a promise.
- Mark general knowledge that is not from the models in these chapters.
- Stop and refer onward when the question turns to diagnosis, pain, medication or supplements (this skill gives no protocols for those).
