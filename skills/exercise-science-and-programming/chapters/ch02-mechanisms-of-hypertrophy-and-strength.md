# Chapter 02: Mechanisms of Hypertrophy and Strength

> Educational, not medical or coaching advice. Mechanisms are simplified models of physiology; they explain why programming rules exist, they do not predict an individual's response.

## Core Idea
In this model, muscle grows when fibres are recruited and exposed to enough mechanical tension, repeatedly, with enough recovery and food to rebuild; strength is the product of muscle size *and* skill and neural efficiency in a specific movement. Many programming debates (rep range, failure, tempo, volume) are arguments about how to deliver tension to the right fibres without wrecking recovery.

## 2.1 Tension and recruitment
- **Thesis**: the best-characterised pathways that start growth respond to mechanical tension on the fibre.
- **Mechanism**: tension on a fibre pulls on its attachment points to the surrounding connective tissue and activates signalling that raises protein synthesis. Fibres only experience that tension if they are recruited. The **size principle** says motor units are recruited from small to large as the demand rises; high-threshold units (the ones with the most growth potential) are recruited either with heavy loads from the first rep, or with lighter loads only as fatigue forces recruitment near failure. A motor unit is a nerve plus the fibres it controls; it fires all-or-nothing, so a submaximal contraction is produced by recruiting *fewer* units, not by partly contracting each.
- **Trade-off**: heavy loads recruit everything quickly but are hard to accumulate volume with and stress joints; light loads need many reps and fatiguing effort to reach the same recruitment (see [ch05](ch05-intensity-proximity-to-failure-and-effective-reps.md)).
- **Does not hold when**: you are in a state of very low load (below roughly 20-30% of a max, where even sets to failure may be too light to give enough tension) or doing ballistic movement where shortening is fast and little tension per fibre is generated.

## 2.2 Other proposed contributors (less settled)
- **Metabolic stress and the pump** (position): swelling, metabolites and a muscle that feels "worked" are used as practical signs that a muscle was stressed; they probably contribute but not exclusively, and a pump without tension is not a stimulus.
- **Muscle damage and soreness** (position): some disruption seems part of the process, but soreness is a poor predictor of growth and a poor measure of how much to do; sore and not growing is possible, and growing without being sore is normal in experienced lifters.
- **Acute hormone spikes**: short-lived post-set elevations in hormones do not reliably translate to long-term growth; this is why advice that tries to maximise them (for example very short rests) is not well supported (see [ch07](ch07-rest-tempo-and-progression.md)).
- **Muscle protein synthesis (MPS)**: protein synthesis is raised for about a day or two after a session, and training is the main driver, with feeding supplying the building blocks. In this model, MPS is a useful short-term signal but not a growth predictor by itself (acute measurements do not always match long-term hypertrophy). General knowledge: daily total protein matters more than precise timing; this skill does not give supplement or dosing protocols.

## 2.3 Why strength is not just size
- **Thesis**: strength depends on how much force the muscle can produce *and* on how well you coordinate the pattern.
- **Mechanism**: in the first weeks and months of lifting, much of the strength increase is commonly attributed to learning (coordination, technique, confidence, bar path) rather than tissue growth (commonly proposed; contested). Later, tissue growth matters more: for the same skill, a bigger muscle can generate more force, but a smaller person with better leverage, technique and years of practice may out-lift a bigger one. In this model, the training-specific phrase is: *specificity of adaptation to the imposed demand* (SAID). Basic strength is built best in sets of about 3-6; hypertrophy with sets of about 5-30 near failure; the overlap (sets of 5-6) is small, which is why serious strength and size goals eventually get separate emphasis (see [ch11](ch11-novices-advanced-trainees-strength-vs-size-and-cardio.md)). Size built in high-rep work converts to strength only after months of practice at heavier loads.
- **Trade-off**: low-rep work is efficient for skill but limits hypertrophy volume; high-rep work grows muscle but does not train the heavy skill.
- **Does not hold when**: the goal is explicitly a competition lift on a date; then specificity wins progressively as the date nears (see [ch06](ch06-exercise-selection-specificity-and-range-of-motion.md)).

## 2.4 Leverage: why bones matter
- **Thesis (position, from a force-production view)**: a muscle's contraction can shorten it by only a fraction of its length; the skeleton's levers multiply that into a useful range of motion and force at the hand or foot.
- **Mechanism**: the contractile units are all-or-nothing and the distance they can shorten is short; the attachment points on bone (origin, insertion) act as levers, so the same muscle produces different forces and speeds depending on geometry. This is why a person's proportions change which lifts feel easy, and why technique is largely about arranging segments so the bar stays where gravity and the foot make force transmission efficient (see [ch10](ch10-mechanics-of-the-big-lifts-and-when-form-matters.md)).
- **Does not hold when**: you start cataloguing the roles of every small muscle in a movement; this is useful in injury reasoning (route to `biomechanics-and-rehab-reasoning`), but not to plan training of a basic lift.

## 2.5 Detraining and "muscle memory" (conceptual)
- **Thesis**: normal time away from the gym costs less than people fear; severe disuse costs more.
- **Mechanism**: in healthy people simply out of the gym but otherwise active, size and strength decline slowly over weeks (research cited in this domain suggests most people do not lose much until two to three weeks or more without training); the dramatic losses in the literature come from immobilisation or bed rest, where the neuromuscular drive to the limb is also disrupted. Regaining lost size is faster than building it the first time (the "muscle memory" effect), likely through retained adaptation in muscle cells.
- **Trade-off**: knowing this helps you take breaks without panic; it should not be used to justify long lapses.
- **Does not hold when**: you are injured or ill; then decisions belong to a clinician (route: `biomechanics-and-rehab-reasoning`).
