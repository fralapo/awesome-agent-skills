# Chapter 09: Fatigue, Recovery, Sleep and Overtraining

> Educational, not medical or coaching advice. Persistent fatigue, mood changes, injury or unexplained performance decline can have medical causes; this chapter does not diagnose, treat or replace a clinician (see also `biomechanics-and-rehab-reasoning`).

## Core Idea
In this model, training is a stimulus-recovery-adaptation cycle, and progress is made during recovery, not during the session. The practical skill is reading fatigue early enough to adjust, without turning every bad day into a diagnosis.

## 9.1 Stimulus, recovery, adaptation
- **Thesis**: adaptation happens after the stimulus is removed, when the system rebuilds; stacking new stimuli on an unrecovered muscle blunts the response.
- **Mechanism**: a hard session creates disruption and fatigue; recovery restores function; adaptation can overshoot (supercompensation). The old general-adaptation framing (alarm, resistance, exhaustion) says the same: if you keep applying stress, you reach exhaustion. A useful image: new windows are built after the storm, not during it.
- **Fitness-fatigue**: every session adds fitness and fatigue; performance is the difference. A max attempt two days in a row usually shows lower numbers on day two not because fitness vanished but because fatigue masks it.
- **Does not hold when**: the recovery you need depends on a muscle, exercise and the individual; recovery is not one clock (muscle may recover in days, connective tissue slower, general-knowledge).

## 9.2 Functional overreaching, non-functional overreaching, overtraining
- **Thesis**: short-term overload followed by recovery can raise performance; the same load without recovery does not.
- **Mechanism**: *functional overreaching* is a short-term performance drop followed by a long-term gain; *non-functional overreaching* is a longer slump where supercompensation does not occur; *overtraining syndrome* is the extreme, prolonged state.
- **Volume vs intensity**: one review states that overtraining arises more from excessive volume than intensity; a short study in which men did a one-rep-max machine squat six days a week for two weeks showed a drop of about 12% in strength, so very frequent maximal efforts are not safe either; a volume-matched comparison reported more fatigue with heavier, lower-rep sets (while 9.5 shows the opposite when effort rather than volume is matched; the findings answer different questions).
- **Does not hold when**: you are a beginner with small training loads; the risk is low and sleep, food and stress usually matter more.

## 9.3 The "overtraining" word is overloaded (position)
- **Thesis**: the same word is used for at least five different things, which causes confusion about what to do.
- **Mechanism**: it can mean (1) a planned overload used to produce adaptation (overreaching); (2) a dangerous failure state to be avoided; (3) a score from a wearable device built on some algorithm; (4) "I trained a lot and feel bad" in casual speech; and (5) in sports medicine, a *diagnosis of exclusion* made only after ruling out medical causes such as endocrine disorders, anaemia, low energy availability, illness or depression. A review cited in this area found no study that followed healthy people into a documented overtrained state under controlled conditions, so the concept rests on observation and retrospective reports.
- **Practical**: describe what you actually see (performance trend, sleep, mood, appetite, joint pain, life stress) rather than labelling it overtraining; reduce load, fix sleep and food, and if problems persist for weeks or look unusual, see a clinician.
- **Does not hold when**: you are an athlete under medical supervision; use their guidance.

## 9.4 Fatigue indicators
- **Thesis (position)**: look for signals before, during and after fatigue becomes a problem.
- **Leading indicators** (before): poor nutrition over the last days (missed meals, too little food while training hard), stress or poor recovery for several days, lack of sleep, a large step up in load. They predict a fatigue bump even when you feel fine today.
- **Concurrent and lagging indicators** (during and after): performance below plan for several sessions, sets that feel harder than their load suggests, persistent soreness or stiffness at the next session, joint aches, low motivation, poor sleep or appetite, a reading outside your normal on morning resting heart rate (one older rule of thumb used a change above about 10% from a one-week baseline as a sign to cut load for a week or two; this is a heuristic, not validated).
- **Simple check**: four questions: how is sleep, how is energy, are aches worse than usual, and how is strength in the gym.
- **Trade-off**: subjective measures are cheap but biased; wearables are objective-looking but often unvalidated.
- **Does not hold when**: one bad session is read as a trend; require several signals.

## 9.5 The "CNS fatigue" claim
- **Claim**: advanced lifters are limited by accumulated central nervous system (CNS) fatigue and need categorically different programming.
- **Evidence**: a direct test in trained men after ten sets of five back squats at about 80% of a max used nerve stimulation and magnetic brain stimulation to locate fatigue; it lasted up to about 72 hours and was predominantly *peripheral* (muscle and connective tissue) after the first day, although some central fatigue was present immediately after exercise. Limitations: one session, small sample, young men; it does not exclude a chronic central adaptation over months, for which data are absent.
- **Related**: lower-load sets to failure produced more discomfort, more perceived fatigue and more acute force loss than heavy low-rep sets away from failure; so someone who says heavy work "fried my CNS" may just have done more fatiguing work earlier.
- **Position**: before using "CNS fatigue" as an explanation, ask what they feel and check sleep, food, stress and total weekly load.
- **Does not hold when**: very high psychological stress, poor sleep or illness are present; those can be central and real but are not the mechanism claimed.

## 9.6 Load monitoring and its limits
- **Thesis**: monitoring is only as good as how well the measure tracks the *mediating mechanism* for the adaptation.
- **Mechanism**: external load (weight, sets, distance) is easy; internal load (physiological or perceived effort) is closer to what causes adaptation but hard to measure; most settings use external load as a proxy, which may be inaccurate. A popular ratio of recent to long-term load was marketed as predictive of injury, but critics argue it is not a valid metric, that it is a reformulation of standard advice (avoid spikes, progress gradually), and that some large studies found the opposite pattern; people tend to cite the studies that suit them. In this model, gradual increases are sensible because tissue capacity takes weeks to months to adapt (a useful analogy is a bank account: a sudden withdrawal larger than the balance causes trouble), but a single ratio should not be treated as a rule.
- **Trade-off**: simple logs plus a few subjective scores beat complex indices when evidence is weak.
- **Does not hold when**: you work with a clinician or team that validated a specific monitoring tool.

## 9.7 Sleep
- **Thesis**: sleep is a recovery lever with large effects and few downsides.
- **Mechanism**: most adults need about 7-8.5 hours; a small minority (a few percent or fewer) carry genetic variants that make short sleep sufficient, and you cannot train yourself into that group. Studies that restrict sleep to about 5.5-6 hours find worse physical and mental performance and mood, with no benefit. People who are under-slept judge their own impairment poorly: in one study participants felt their performance plateaued after one bad night while measured performance kept declining across three nights. Evidence for more than eight hours of sleep improving athletic performance (for example in sprinters) exists but is limited; long-term trials are rare for ethical reasons.
- **Does not hold when**: sleep problems are persistent or severe; consider medical advice.

## 9.8 Body weight, body composition and appetite (concepts only, position)
- **Thesis (position)**: for the training context, three ideas help: weight is not fat, energy balance is the lever for fat change, and appetite signals can be steered by food structure and attention.
- **Mechanism**: scale weight swings with water and stored carbohydrate, so a fast drop on a very low-carbohydrate regimen is claimed in this model to be mostly fluid; tracking by repeated photos or measures over longer spans is suggested as a counter to scale fixation. Fat loss needs a sustained energy deficit; in this model a very fast loss costs muscle and raises hunger, and the plateau after weight loss is read as a hormonal response (falling leptin raises hunger) and an adaptation, not a failure. Satiety has a mechanical part (stomach distension, bulky and fibrous food) and a hormonal part (fat and protein signals); a meal built on one macronutrient satisfies only one of them. Eating while distracted or stressed is said to blunt hunger and fullness signals. The accumulating behaviour matters more than a single meal.
- **Evidence**: the satiety mechanisms and the weight-versus-fat distinction are mainstream; the claim that cardio mostly burns fat while lifting "keeps burning" at rest, the "no inflammatory foods" stance and the "water only" account of low-carbohydrate results are position and contested.
- **Trade-off**: simple structure (protein, fibre, volume of food, regular meals) is easy to sustain; rigid rules are not.
- **Does not hold when**: a medical condition, eating-disorder history, pregnancy or medication changes the picture; this skill gives no diet, calorie, macro or supplement protocol; see a qualified clinician or dietitian.
