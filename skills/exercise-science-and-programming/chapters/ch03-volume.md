# Chapter 03: Volume

> Educational, not medical or coaching advice. Set counts below are ranges used in the literature and by practitioners, not prescriptions for a specific person.

## Core Idea
In this model, volume is the dose of hard work a muscle receives, and it behaves like a dose-response relationship with diminishing returns and, for each person and each phase, a ceiling set by recovery. The number is less important than whether the sets were *hard sets* and whether you recover from them.

## 3.1 What counts as volume
- **Thesis**: for hypertrophy, the number of hard sets per muscle per week is a better working measure than "sets x reps x load" (volume load or tonnage).
- **Mechanism**: tonnage was the standard way to equate programs for decades, mostly by convention. When moderate-load training (roughly 8-10 reps at around 70-75% of a max) is compared with light-load training (roughly 20-35 reps below 50%) with the *same number of hard sets*, growth is very similar even though tonnage differs a lot. A "hard set" is a working set with good technique, in a rep range of roughly 5-30, taken close to failure (about 0-3 reps in reserve in one popular definition; some sources say "no more than 3-4 reps left"). Warm-ups and sets far from failure are excluded or discounted.
- **Trade-off**: hard sets are easy to count but treat a set of heavy deadlifts and a set of leg curls as equal, which they are not for fatigue (position: count the stimulus honestly, and treat heavy, systemic exercises as more expensive).
- **Does not hold when**: you train for strength with sets of one to three (see [ch11](ch11-novices-advanced-trainees-strength-vs-size-and-cardio.md)); sets of five or fewer need a different counting logic because most sets are not near failure.

## 3.2 The dose-response curve: where is the top? (contested)
- **Thesis**: more weekly sets help up to a point; where the point is, and whether more can *hurt*, is debated.
- **Consensus**: a meta-analysis of about 15 studies (2017) found that 10 or more weekly sets per muscle gave clearly more growth than fewer than 10; the finding survived removing the single longest study. Many beginners grow well at around 10 sets.
- **Position A (inverted-U)**: growth rises with volume, flattens, and eventually falls as recovery is exceeded. In this view roughly 10-20 weekly sets per muscle is a sweet spot for most, and above about 20 you risk plateauing or overreaching. The "maximum recoverable volume" idea was originally proposed as a *ceiling for athletes who share limited recovery with other training*.
- **Position B (no clear ceiling)**: a meta-analysis that included higher-volume studies (as reported, dated) found continued benefit at high weekly volumes (reported as 20-30 sets or more), and no study shows muscle *loss* from adding sets, only plateaus. Counter-argument: few studies exist at very high volume, almost all are short, the participants are not highly trained, and injury risk or lost quality are not captured by cross-sectional-area endpoints.
- **Trade-off**: if you under-dose, growth is left on the table; if you over-dose, you pay in recovery, joint stress and time for small marginal gains.
- **Does not hold when**: a person is stagnating *with room to add sets*; then raising volume is the first thing to test, and when a person is stagnating *while already fatigued*, lowering volume is.

## 3.3 Per-session limits and the cost of tiny sessions
- **Thesis (position)**: volume should be thought of per week *and* per session.
- **Mechanism**: past roughly 5-10 hard sets for one muscle in one session, additional sets tend to be lower in quality (fatigue degrades technique and load), so "junk volume" appears; splitting weekly volume across more sessions raises quality per set. At the other end, one or two sets per muscle per session wastes warm-up time, so clustering sets into fewer sessions can be more efficient. A practical window used by one coaching framework: about 2-4 sets per movement group per session at the low end, and splitting anything beyond about 8.
- **Trade-off**: more sessions means more gym time and warm-up; fewer means longer, more fatiguing sessions.
- **Does not hold when**: sets are short and light (for example, isolation work at low systemic cost); the per-session ceiling then sits higher.

## 3.4 Volume landmarks (a model with a method)
- **Thesis**: each person and muscle has a volume range in which training is productive, bounded by a minimum that produces growth and a maximum from which one still recovers; the useful skill is detecting where you are, not memorising numbers.
- **Mechanism**: the landmarks are **maintenance volume (MV)**, the dose that holds size; **minimum effective volume (MEV)**, the lowest dose that yields growth; **maximum adaptive volume (MAV)**, the productive middle band; **maximum recoverable volume (MRV)**, the most you can recover from. They are *not* constants: they differ by muscle (for example, a trainee may find hamstring work caps at roughly 10-15 weekly sets including hip hinges), by exercise, by phase (capacity is lower at the start of a block and rises as you adapt), by diet, sleep and stress, and with training age.
- **Estimating them**: the formal method needs several training blocks (maintenance: lower volume until performance is stable; MEV: start low and raise until performance improves; MAV: try neighbouring volumes and compare progress; MRV: raise volume until performance stalls or falls across repeated high-volume weeks). The estimates have error; the benefit is a range (for example, over about six months) rather than a number.
- **Using them in a block** (position): start near MEV at a conservative effort (around 3 reps in reserve), add load, reps or sets slowly each week while keeping effort within roughly one rep of the previous week, and finish the block near MRV before a deload (see [ch08](ch08-periodization-and-deloads.md)).
- **Trade-off**: a model is easy to over-apply; it is tidy but built from few controlled data and many practitioner observations. Treat as a hypothesis you check against your own log.
- **Does not hold when**: your performance cannot be measured reliably (changing exercises every week, large life stress), or you are a beginner whose progress is not volume-limited.

## 3.5 Autoregulating volume from feedback
- **Thesis (position)**: when you cannot know your landmark in advance, adjust sets from short-term signals rather than from a fixed plan.
- **Mechanism**: three proxies are used together. *Pump*: none suggests too little work, decent and at least as good as last week suggests you are on track, a huge pump is fine unless disruption follows. *Disruption (soreness, joint stiffness)*: none suggests too little; fully healed before the next session is the target; still sore at the next session means too much. *Performance*: hitting planned load and reps with a sensible challenge is right; too easy means more is available; failing planned numbers because of accumulated fatigue means too much. A simple rule uses a one-to-four score for soreness and for performance after each session to decide whether to add, hold or cut sets the next time.
- **Trade-off**: more responsive than a fixed plan, but subjective; soreness is a poor guide at low frequencies and for exercises you have not done in a while, and a pump can be shaped by diet and hydration.
- **Does not hold when**: sets are not hard, technique is poor, or frequency is so low (once a week) that soreness dominates the signal. Fix those first (see [ch04](ch04-frequency.md)).

## 3.6 Quick rules
- First get quality (technique, rep range, near failure) and frequency in order; then tune the number of sets. Start tuning from a frequency you already recover from (two to four sessions per muscle per week in one framework), not from an extreme.
- If you stall and have room: add a few sets for the lagging muscle; if you stall and feel run down: reduce.
- Specialisation phases (position): raise one muscle to a high volume (for example near 30 weekly sets) while holding others at 10-20, then rotate.
