# Chapter 11: Novices, Advanced Trainees, Strength versus Size, and Cardio

> Educational, not medical or coaching advice. Training status categories and cardio-lifting trade-offs here are general models; cardiovascular health, medication or medical conditions are outside this chapter and belong with a clinician.

## Core Idea
In this model, how a person should train depends less on a label (novice, intermediate, advanced) than on the *time it takes for their work to show up* in performance and tissue, on their goal (strength, size, or both) and on what else competes for recovery (such as endurance training).

## 11.1 The novice effect
- **Thesis**: almost anything works at first; early results do not prove a method.
- **Mechanism**: a person with no training history improves from any new, harder-than-before stimulus (learning, coordination, confidence), so beginner programmes seem to work regardless of design; this is also why many university trials on untrained students cannot distinguish methods.
- **Trade-off**: beginners progress fast with simple programmes; but "it worked for me when I began" is weak evidence for advanced training.
- **Does not hold when**: a beginner has a clear limitation (injury, anatomy) that requires a tailored start.

## 11.2 Is novice / intermediate / advanced a biological category? (contested)
- **Claims of the framework**: (1) the groups have different adaptation rates; (2) so they need different progression models (add weight every session, every week, every month); (3) so programme design should differ by category.
- **Evidence against clean categories**: a large dataset of drug-tested, raw competition powerlifters (as reported, dated; thousands of lifters followed for years) showed a smooth, decelerating curve of strength gain (about 7.5-12.5% above baseline in year one, around 20% above baseline after ten years), with no visible inflection point where a lifter crosses from novice to intermediate. The dataset describes people who compete, so it may not represent recreational lifters, and the category is not directly measured.
- **Alternative model (position)**: the labels describe a *measurement window* (how long you wait for work to show on the bar). Different tissues adapt at different speeds: neural and skill changes in weeks, muscle size in weeks to months, tendon stiffness in at least 6-8 weeks and size changes longer, bone remodelling in months. A fast early strength gain can outpace tendon adaptation, which is a reason to progress sensibly and watch for symptoms.
- **Practical consequence**: use a reactive approach: let the demonstrated response set the next increment, and do not leave a programme that is working just because you have a new label. The same logic applies at year one and year ten: only the size of the increments and the frequency change.
- **Counter-argument**: labels are a fast way to estimate how many years someone has trained, which correlates with recovery capacity and with how quickly fatigue accumulates (advanced lifters hit their volume ceiling in fewer weeks than novices, [ch08](ch08-periodization-and-deloads.md)), so the shortcut has value as a first guess.
- **Does not hold when**: individuals differ a lot (some beginners progress extremely quickly, some veterans stall); see [ch01](ch01-reasoning-about-training-evidence.md).

## 11.3 When the beginner programme stops working
- **Thesis (position)**: a beginner programme of a few sets of five, adding load every session, stops working after somewhere between about two and six months (a rough, variable range); the early rise is commonly proposed to be largely neural (contested).
- **Mechanism**: after the learning phase, further strength needs more muscle and more specific skill; the usual answer is more volume at moderate loads (more total hard sets, accessory work for the lagging muscles), then a progression that adds load weekly or monthly rather than per session. Stalling does not mean wasting the effort; a month of the same loaded workout is not a month of wasted work.
- **Caution**: some online "volume" prescriptions are extreme (50-rep sets, very long sets); more is not automatically better (see [ch03](ch03-volume.md)).
- **Does not hold when**: the stall comes from sleep, food or stress; check those first ([ch09](ch09-fatigue-recovery-sleep-and-overtraining.md)).

## 11.4 Training for strength versus training for size
- **Thesis (position, one coaching framework)**: they overlap but are not identical; optimising one costs some of the other.
- **Loading**: basic strength is built best in sets of about 3-6; overall hypertrophy in sets of about 5-30 near failure; the overlap (about 5-6) is narrow, so a long programme in that overlap lacks variety and wastes some size potential, and sets of 20 produce a smaller strength gain than heavier loads.
- **Exercise selection**: strength emphasises the competition lifts and close variants (specificity, [ch06](ch06-exercise-selection-specificity-and-range-of-motion.md)); size permits a wider selection and more isolation.
- **Frequency and structure**: strength training benefits from frequent practice and from alternating hard and easy sessions; hypertrophy training has fewer "easy" days. Differences in volume and progression exist but are unclear in source.
- **Best of both**: use heavy sets of five or six for strength on the main lifts and higher-rep sets for size elsewhere; accept that gains in each will be somewhat lower than in a pure phase.
- **Does not hold when**: you are a beginner, for whom the same programme builds both.

## 11.5 Solving for training load (a beginner-friendly frame)
- **Thesis**: for general fitness, many intensities work; the task is to maximise the total useful training load you can sustain.
- **Mechanism**: the main bottlenecks are time and recovery; viable intensity ranges are broad (for strength, a floor around 65-70% of a one-rep max; for hypertrophy, about 30-85%; for cardiorespiratory fitness, almost any exercise above ordinary activity). Once inside the viable range, do more total work (frequency, time, volume, progression) rather than micromanaging intensity. A recommendation for someone tired of pushing hard: casual sets of the lifts plus other activity still provide a stimulus; maximal effort is optional unless you need to peak.
- **Trade-off**: lower effort per set means more sets for a similar effect; you must be able to do those sets.
- **Does not hold when**: you have a specific performance goal; then narrow down ([ch06](ch06-exercise-selection-specificity-and-range-of-motion.md)).

## 11.6 Cardio and the interference effect
- **Thesis**: combining endurance and strength training can reduce strength gains, and the penalty is larger for trained people and for same-session training; it is small when sessions are separated.
- **Evidence**: a meta-analysis (as reported, dated; 27 studies; healthy adults 18-40, resistance training at least twice a week at 60% or more of a max, moderate-or-harder endurance work, outcome maximal squat or leg-press strength) found: untrained subjects, no interference; moderately trained, a small, not statistically significant effect (about 0.2 standard deviations, p about 0.08); trained, a small but significant effect (about 0.35). When endurance and lifting were in the *same session*, effects were larger: about 0.23 for moderately trained (not significant) and about 0.66 for trained (a moderate effect). When sessions were *separate* (different times or different days), there was no significant effect at any training status.
- **Caveats**: the review lumps "moderately trained" with merely active people (so training status is blurry); the outcome is lower-body strength; hypertrophy is not the endpoint; averages hide individual variation; and a small effect can still matter to a competitor.
- **Practical** (position): separate lifting and hard endurance when possible; if combined in one session, think about which goal is the priority and put it first; keep endurance volume enough for your goals but not more; strength can still increase, just more slowly.
- **Out of scope**: how much cardio is right for heart health, and exercise in people with cardiovascular risk; consult a clinician. This skill does not cover performance-enhancing drugs.
- **Does not hold when**: you are untrained; at the start the interference is minimal, which makes combined programmes reasonable.
