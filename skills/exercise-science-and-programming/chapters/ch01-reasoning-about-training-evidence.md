# Chapter 01: Reasoning About Training Evidence

> Educational, not medical or coaching advice. It explains how training claims are built and tested; it does not diagnose injuries, prescribe programs for a person or replace a qualified coach or clinician.

## Core Idea
In this model, almost every training question has the same shape: a population-level result (an average over a group, for a few weeks or months) is being asked to answer an individual-level question (what should *this* person do next). Good reasoning keeps the two apart, asks what kind of evidence is on the table, and states how much is consensus and how much is position.

## 1.1 Evidence has layers, and each layer has a blind spot
- **Thesis**: no single kind of evidence settles a training question; each tells you something and hides something.
- **Mechanism**: cell and animal work shows what is *possible* but may not transfer; surveys show correlation; a randomised trial with a dozen people per group shows a causal effect in a small, usually young, usually inexperienced sample; a meta-analysis can only summarise what has been studied, so any bias in the underlying studies (for example, one condition tested mostly in older people and another mostly in young people) is baked in and cannot be untangled without new data. Broad-inclusion meta-regressions answer different questions than strict ones.
- **Trade-off**: waiting for stronger evidence is slow; acting on weak evidence is fast and sometimes wrong. A "wait and see" stance is legitimate for a headline built on a small or non-human study.
- **Does not hold when**: the action is low-cost and reversible (trying a variation for a few weeks); then weak evidence plus honest monitoring is enough.

## 1.2 "No difference on average" is not "no difference for you"
- **Thesis**: when two protocols give similar group means, it is correct to say neither is clearly better *for the average participant*; it is not correct to say the choice does not matter for any individual.
- **Mechanism**: a group mean can hide opposite individual effects. A basketball analogy: if a strength block and a conditioning block give the same group-level results, a very light player needs the weight room and a heavy, strong but gassed player needs conditioning. The same logic applies to, say, high-rep versus moderate-rep training, or two training frequencies.
- **Trade-off**: using the average as a default is efficient; treating it as a law removes useful personalisation (position: default to the average, personalise when a person has a reason, such as a joint that dislikes one option or a clear preference).
- **Does not hold when**: you have no information about the person; the average (or the simplest option) is then the rational default.

## 1.3 People differ a lot in response to the same program
- **Thesis**: identical training produces a wide spread of outcomes; large spreads are normal, not evidence of cheating or of a broken program.
- **Mechanism**: in one older study of about 60 people on a 16-week quadriceps program, the lowest-responding quarter showed essentially no average fibre growth, the middle half roughly a certain size, and the top quarter about double it, with the single best responder around three times the average. In a much larger older study (several hundred people, 3 months of biceps training), typical change was roughly 15-20% in cross-sectional area, a few people lost a little, and a few gained more than 50%. (Both figures are dated, come from untrained people and describe one muscle and one protocol each.)
- **Trade-off**: knowing this lowers anxiety about "slow" progress but can also become an excuse; the practical response is to change the variables you control (see [ch03](ch03-volume.md), [ch05](ch05-intensity-proximity-to-failure-and-effective-reps.md), [ch09](ch09-fatigue-recovery-sleep-and-overtraining.md)) before concluding you are a low responder.
- **Does not hold when**: progress is zero for many months despite adequate volume, effort, food and sleep; then the cause is more likely a programming or recovery problem than a fixed trait (general knowledge: check adherence and measurement first).

## 1.4 What study designs usually leave out
- **Thesis**: laboratory findings are conditional on how the study was built.
- **Mechanism**: most participants are untrained or lightly trained, so their recovery capacity is high and the stimulus needed is low (training age of a few years counts as "trained" in much of the literature); studies run weeks to a few months, which can hide slow accumulation of fatigue; many studies equate total volume between groups, which removes the very thing that some strategies (for example higher frequency) are meant to change, namely the volume you can *actually* do; the definition of "failure" differs between studies, and effort is often estimated rather than measured.
- **Trade-off**: control gives causal clarity; it costs ecological validity.
- **Does not hold when**: a result has been replicated in trained people over longer periods with free volume; confidence then rises.

## 1.5 Where anecdote and expert experience fit
- **Thesis (position)**: it is honest, and useful, to say "this is not evidence-based, it is something that seems safe and seems to work for me, and you may test it".
- **Mechanism**: practitioners observe many cases and generate hypotheses that research has not yet tested; the failure is not using anecdote but presenting it with the language of proof. A convincing story from elite bodybuilders is confounded by genetics, drugs, years of training and survivorship.
- **Trade-off**: anecdotes are cheap and fast, but prone to selection; evidence is slow and narrow.
- **Does not hold when**: the claim is risky (a method that could cause injury) or expensive; then the bar for evidence rises.

## 1.6 Consensus, controversy and how to label them
- **Thesis**: separate three states: (a) consensus (multiple reviews agree and mechanism is sound), (b) active controversy (credible people disagree and data are mixed), (c) speculation.
- **Mechanism**: in this skill, *consensus* includes: progressive overload is needed; training reasonably close to failure matters; about 10 or more hard sets per muscle per week beat fewer for most people; a very wide range of rep loads can build muscle when sets are hard; slow-burn strength comes from specific practice. *Controversial*: the shape of the volume curve at the top, whether to go to failure, the usefulness of counting "effective reps", whether periodization per se matters for size, high versus low bar squat, the best rest length when sets are held constant. See [ch12](ch12-evaluating-a-training-claim.md) for a checklist.
- **Trade-off**: labelling takes more words, but it stops a contested opinion from sounding like a law.
- **Does not hold when**: you are teaching a beginner; give one clear default and note that details are debated.
