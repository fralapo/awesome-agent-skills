# Chapter 04: Frequency

> Educational, not medical or coaching advice. This chapter compares how often to train a muscle; personal schedules and injuries need individual judgment.

## Core Idea
In this model, frequency is mostly a *tool for distributing volume and fatigue*, not a growth switch in itself. The sensible question is "what split lets me do good sets with adequate recovery", not "what is the best number".

## 4.1 What the controlled evidence says
- **Consensus**: training a muscle twice a week is at least as good as once, and beyond twice the effect on size is small or undetectable when weekly volume is equal.
- **Mechanism**: reviews of studies that equate weekly sets find 2x clearly better than 1x in some analyses; going from 2x to 3x to 4x yields progressively smaller or no extra benefit. One review that did *not* equate volume (treating higher frequency as allowing more volume) found a roughly asymptotic pattern: large gain from 1x to 2x, smaller from 2x to 3x, very small after that.
- **Trade-off**: the equated-volume design is cleaner but removes a main benefit of frequency; the unequated design is more realistic but confounds frequency with volume.
- **Does not hold when**: your constraint is time, schedule or joint tolerance rather than growth; then the best frequency is the one you can sustain.

## 4.2 Why higher frequency might help (position: moderate support)
- **Thesis**: spreading sets across more sessions lets each set be higher quality and total weekly volume higher.
- **Mechanism**: less work per session means less within-session fatigue, so loads and effort per set hold up (see [ch03](ch03-volume.md), section 3.3). A proposed physiology: the elevated protein-synthesis window after a session lasts about two days in novices but is shorter in experienced lifters, so frequent smaller doses could keep it elevated (position; the mechanism is debated and the acute measure does not map cleanly to growth). Full-body training several times a week is also easier to schedule around missed days.
- **Evidence caveat**: an unpublished high-frequency study on well-trained lifters (3x versus 6x per week, equal volume) reported a clear advantage for 6x, but it exists mainly as an abstract and cannot be fully evaluated; a later short replication in less-trained lifters (6 weeks) found no difference between 3x and 6x. Taken together: both work; benefit, if any, probably appears with more advanced trainees, longer periods and when it enables more volume.
- **Does not hold when**: you cannot recover between sessions (still sore, performance down), or the extra sessions mean more warm-up than work.

## 4.3 Why lower frequency might be better than the theory suggests (position)
- **Thesis**: the logic "more frequent = more volume = more growth" bends in the real world.
- **Mechanism, as argued by advocates of moderate frequency**:
  - Higher frequency often needs more frequent deloads, so fewer productive weeks per year (for example, if a 3x program needs a deload every 4 weeks and a 2x program every 6, the gap in total sessions narrows).
  - Most participants in frequency studies are untrained, who accumulate little fatigue; trained lifters with large muscles accumulate more and recover slower.
  - Practical limits: more sessions mean more warm-up, commuting and fatigue from joint loading (general knowledge: tendons and joints can limit before muscle does).
  - A once-weekly pattern that leaves a muscle fully recovered for several days is leaving growth time unused (the "if you are no longer sore and are stronger than last time, you can train it again" rule).
- **Trade-off**: lower frequency means bigger sessions per muscle; this is the main cost when volume is high.
- **Does not hold when**: your sessions are small (two to four sets) and you recover easily; then more frequent, shorter sessions fit.

## 4.4 Excessively low and high frequencies
- **Excessively low** (once a week or less, with a long gap): the muscle is recovered and idle for part of the week; soreness is a poor guide at this frequency; volume per session must be large, pushing past the per-session limit.
- **Excessively high** (for example daily for the same muscle, with high per-session loads): tissue and nervous-system stress accumulate, technique degrades and joint or tendon issues rise; the pump may never be reached with one or two sets, and the muscle may not be *disrupted enough* for a stimulus (position). Advocates list several mechanisms; a short list: not enough recovery, not enough stimulus per exposure, deload demand, joint wear, time cost.
- **Rule of thumb** (position): the right frequency is the one at which you are recovered (not sore, performance at or above last session) when you train the muscle again. Two to four weekly sessions per muscle is the common starting range.

## 4.5 Frequency in the programme
- If weekly volume per muscle is about 10-20 hard sets, 2 sessions of 5-10 sets or 3 sessions of 4-7 sets are common; heavier systemic lifts (squat, deadlift) usually need more rest between exposures than smaller isolation work (general knowledge).
- Skill lifts (strength-focused trainees) benefit from frequent practice of the lift at submaximal loads (see [ch07](ch07-rest-tempo-and-progression.md) and [ch11](ch11-novices-advanced-trainees-strength-vs-size-and-cardio.md)).
- For strength, evidence leans to higher frequency helping at least somewhat; for hypertrophy with equal volume, differences are small.
