# Chapter 07: Rest, Tempo and Progression

> Educational, not medical or coaching advice. Rules for adding load assume a healthy adult without injury; if a progression causes pain that persists, stop and consult a professional.

## Core Idea
In this model, rest and tempo are second-order variables: choose values that let you repeat performance with control and then spend your attention on *progression*, the logged, gradual increase in load, reps or sets that makes progress cumulative.

## 7.1 Rest between sets
- **Thesis**: rest long enough to keep the next set hard and productive; shorter rest costs growth unless you add sets.
- **Old rule**: about one minute for size, three to five for strength, under half a minute for endurance. Rationale for short rests was hormone spikes, which do not reliably translate to long-term growth.
- **Evidence**: an eight-week trial of full-body training found better growth (quadriceps and triceps) and better squat and bench strength with three-minute rests than one-minute rests, with no endurance difference; a review concluded that rests under about one minute likely reduce growth when sets are equal; a trial in which the one-minute group performed extra sets to match volume load found similar growth to the three-minute group.
- **Position**: for time-limited trainees, short rest plus extra sets can recover most of the loss, and the practical rule is "rest until you can perform the next set at close to the planned reps" (often two to three minutes for compounds, less for isolation). Do not shorten rests as a fat-loss tactic (position: training serves muscle; diet and conditioning serve fat loss).
- **Does not hold when**: the exercise is low-fatigue (arms, lateral raises); short rests lose little there.

## 7.2 Tempo
- **Thesis**: rep speed has a wide acceptable range; control and range matter more than the count.
- **Evidence**: a meta-analysis of rep durations from about half a second to eight seconds per rep found similar hypertrophy; sets with reps longer than about ten seconds each tended to be worse, probably because load must be reduced a lot.
- **Practical**: lower under control (about two seconds is a common default), no bouncing out of the bottom, full range, then lift with intent. Tempo notation lists seconds for lowering, bottom pause, lifting and top pause.
- **Trade-off**: slower tempos can help with technique and joint comfort but reduce load.
- **Does not hold when**: you are training power (fast concentric is the goal) or strength specifically with competition pauses.

## 7.3 Progressive overload: the generic rule
- **Thesis**: muscle and strength adapt to a demand; to keep adapting the demand must rise (progressive overload). Any measurable increase counts: more weight, more reps, more sets, better control, a longer pause, a new exercise to restart the progression.
- **Mechanism**: the body adapts to a stressor and the same stress eventually stops being a stimulus. Logging is the means of making the stress *quantifiable*: if you cannot see what you did last time, you cannot add to it.
- **Does not hold when**: you progress by random variety without tracking; hard and sweaty is not the same as progressive.

## 7.4 Double progression and the two-for-two rule
- **Double progression** (general technique): keep a rep range (for example three sets of 10-12), add reps until you reach the top of the range on all sets, then increase load and drop back to the bottom.
- **Two-for-two rule** (beginner heuristic): if you can do two extra reps on the last set for two consecutive weeks, raise the load.
- **Trade-off**: simple, forgiving, works for hypertrophy and for lifters who dislike percentages; progress is slower than a pure load jump but easier to sustain.
- **Does not hold when**: your weights jump in large increments (use micro-plates) or when you are peaking for a max.

## 7.5 Linear progression for novices (position)
- **Thesis**: a trainee with no history can add load every session for weeks, because the stimulus is large and recovery is fast.
- **Mechanism (as commonly proposed)**: establish a baseline with ascending sets of five until bar speed slows or form starts to degrade; train squat, press or bench and pull three times a week; do three sets of five (one heavy set of five for the deadlift); add a small load each session (a typical rule: a small fixed increment, with bigger jumps in the first sessions); rest several minutes between work sets; record everything. Large early gains in strength and body mass are claimed for novices (unverified practitioner claims, not controlled data).
- **Why sets of five?** It is said to be the best compromise between load, practice and fatigue; a related claim is that five reps is a manageable number that can be performed with good form by beginners, and that high reps fatigue form (position; not a controlled comparison).
- **Counter-points**: much of early progress is learning, not tissue (see [ch02](ch02-mechanisms-of-hypertrophy-and-strength.md)); the programme works because you are doing more than you did before (the *novice effect*: nearly anything works at first), so success of a method in beginners says little about its superiority; it stalls typically within a few months and then needs more volume, variety and a different progression (see [ch11](ch11-novices-advanced-trainees-strength-vs-size-and-cardio.md)).
- **Does not hold when**: you are already trained, have a history of joint problems with heavy singles or doubles, or want primarily size (different structure).

## 7.6 Effort scales and autoregulated progression
- **Beginner scale** (position): use easy / medium / hard with a form limit; working sets should feel medium to hard; the bar speed slowing is a cue. People differ in their internal sense of "hard" (an athlete used to maximal efforts may rate a set as easy that a sedentary person rates as hard), so the coach calibrates.
- **Add-a-fixed-amount**: simplest; rigid; can be stuck on a bad day.
- **Percentage-based**: requires a known max; the same percentage and reps is not the same stimulus on every day; beginners' maxes are unreliable and change quickly; rigid but good for consistent trainees following a fixed plan.
- **RPE / RIR**: flexible; day-to-day variation is absorbed; needs experience and gets fuzzier at high reps. One chart converts reps and RPE into a percentage of a max (for instance, five reps at RPE 8 is about 81% in one widely used chart; chart-specific). Velocity can supplement.
- **Estimated one-rep max (e1RM)**: a formula using weight and reps (or chart) gives an estimate of the max, useful for tracking progress across weeks with different rep targets; accuracy falls as the set is further from the max and reps are higher (a three-rep set predicts better than a ten-rep set). Comparing a 300x5 at a low RPE to 330x1 at RPE 10 shows why you need an estimate with RPE, not just numbers.
- **Trade-off**: the extra maths pay off for strength-focused trainees; for general hypertrophy a simple double progression does the job.
- **Does not hold when**: you skip the set because you "do not feel it today"; that is a misuse of RPE; adjust within a range and proceed.

## 7.7 Is it a stall? (position)
- **Thesis**: most stalls are not stalls; check measurement before changing the programme.
- **Mechanism**: a real stall is a lack of demonstrable improvement in a priority lift over several weeks (about three to four, longer in experienced lifters) *with* consistent training, adequate sleep and food and manageable life stress. Compare to a recent anchor (the last 6-8 weeks, not a lifetime best); compare like with like (same rep range and RPE target, or e1RM from similar sets); use block-start and block-end calibration sets (for example, four reps at RPE 6, 7, 8); use the warm-up as an in-session signal (a standard warm-up moving faster or slower than usual suggests the day's capacity).
- **Remedies** (general): check life stress, sleep and food; consider a deload or volume change (see [ch08](ch08-periodization-and-deloads.md)); if unexplained, consider whether something else is going on that needs a clinician (see `biomechanics-and-rehab-reasoning`).
- **Does not hold when**: expectations are miscalibrated; early linear progress cannot continue for years (see [ch11](ch11-novices-advanced-trainees-strength-vs-size-and-cardio.md)).

## 7.8 Progression without a measurable load (position)
- **Thesis (position)**: when the load is body weight, progress by moving along a ladder of variants and by reps, time or density, using the same logic as double progression.
- **Mechanism**: define a ladder for a pattern (for example kneeling, standard, feet-elevated, narrow base, one-sided). Work a variant in a rep range; when the top of the range is reached across sets with some reps in reserve, step to a harder variant and drop back to the bottom of the range. Because steps between variants are uneven, add intermediate rungs (tempo, a pause, a longer lever, a shorter rest) and rely on effort ratings (RIR) rather than on the variant name. A fixed-time format (as many quality reps as possible within a time cap, with free rests) is one way to organise density and consistency; claims that it maximises a muscular or hormonal response are position, not established (unclear in source).
- **Regressing inside a session**: if quality or explosiveness drops, switch to an easier variant instead of grinding; in jump-type work, stop the jumps and keep the base movement.
- **Trade-off**: effort is judged by feel, so the ladder needs honest ratings and a log; comparisons between people are weak.
- **Does not hold when**: you can add external load in small steps; then double progression on weight is cleaner (7.4).
