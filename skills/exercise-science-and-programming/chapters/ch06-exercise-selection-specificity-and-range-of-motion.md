# Chapter 06: Exercise Selection, Specificity and Range of Motion

> Educational, not medical or coaching advice. Exercise choice interacts with injuries, anatomy and equipment; for pain or a diagnosed condition see a qualified professional (see also `biomechanics-and-rehab-reasoning`).

## Core Idea
In this model, there are no mandatory exercises for muscle growth; there is a spectrum of specificity that matters a lot for strength skills and little for general hypertrophy. Choose a small core of well-practised, loadable movements, add isolation where a muscle is under-trained, and let preference and tolerance decide among near-equivalent options.

## 6.1 Compound versus isolation
- **Thesis (position, mainstream)**: build the programme around multi-joint movements and use single-joint work to fill gaps.
- **Mechanism**: compound lifts (squat, press, row, hinge) load a lot of muscle with one set, build total-body strength efficiently and save time; isolation work targets smaller or poorly loaded muscles (arms, side and rear shoulders, calves, hamstrings, abs) that compounds can leave under-stimulated. One simple way to organise: four movement patterns (squat, push, pull, hinge) plus isolation as needed.
- **Trade-off**: isolation lets you push closer to failure at lower systemic cost; compounds give more transfer per minute but cost more fatigue (see [ch05](ch05-intensity-proximity-to-failure-and-effective-reps.md)).
- **Does not hold when**: a person cannot do a compound safely or comfortably (injury, anatomy); there are many other equally valid ways to load the same muscle.

## 6.2 Specificity as a spectrum
- **Thesis**: the training adaptation is specific to the demand imposed; specificity is a continuum, not a binary.
- **Mechanism**: exercises sit between the competition or target movement itself (most specific), close variants, general strength and size work, and corrective or antagonist work (least specific). Keep most training toward the specific end; anything far from it needs a reason (for example, balancing a muscle pair that stresses a joint, or building muscle size that will later be converted to strength).
- **Proximity to the goal date**: far from a competition, a lifter can use less specific variants (higher-rep front squats, for example) because they train underlying quality (quadriceps size); as the date approaches, specificity increases (sets of 3-5 with the competition setup, then singles).
- **Trade-off**: more specificity improves the skill sooner but risks overuse of the same pattern; less specificity spreads stress but diverts time.
- **Does not hold when**: the goal is general hypertrophy; specificity then mostly means "heavy enough, close enough to failure, through a good range".

## 6.3 Why use variations if specificity is king? (position)
- **Thesis**: variations are not only a way to avoid boredom; they help skill learning.
- **Mechanism**: motor learning research (general) suggests that learning is driven partly by detecting and correcting errors, not only repetition. As you become very strong in the competition lift, the weights make you focus on survival and not on technique, and the movement becomes automatic; an unfamiliar variation (front squat, pause squat, close-grip bench, deadlift with a different stance or from blocks) makes errors obvious, giving more chances to correct them in a pattern that transfers to the main lift. In motor-skill research varied practice usually beats constant practice.
- **Trade-off**: too many variations dilute practice of the main skill.
- **Does not hold when**: you are a beginner still learning the base pattern; constant practice of the basics is better then (see [ch11](ch11-novices-advanced-trainees-strength-vs-size-and-cardio.md)).

## 6.4 Full versus partial range of motion (contested, leaning full)
- **Argument for partials (1): "more weight means more tension"**. Counter: when you halve the range you can lift much more weight but do less mechanical work per rep; the extra load does not compensate for the lost distance (an example: three sets of eight at a moderate load through full range equals more total work than a much heavier half-range set), so trading range for load is usually a bad trade for growth.
- **Argument for partials (2): constant tension in the mid-range**. A trial of lying triceps extensions compared full range with a half range that avoided the top (lockout) and found larger growth with the constant-tension version; limits: it is one study, the partial group may have been closer to failure, and it is a free-weight single-joint movement where the top of the range is easy. It is plausible that for such lifts, stopping slightly short of lockout is helpful. It probably does not apply to cables and machines, which keep tension anyway.
- **Argument for partials (3): professional bodybuilders use them**. Counter: this is anecdote confounded by genetics, drugs, years of training and other variables, and earlier-era champions used deep ranges.
- **Evidence summary (position, leaning full range)**: across the small body of trials (about six at the time of a review), all lower-body studies favoured full range, one compared lighter deep squats to heavier partial squats and found more growth along the quadriceps with the deep version, and upper-body results were mixed, with only one favouring partials (the triceps study above). Rule of thumb: use a reasonably full range most of the time, with a good stretch at the bottom and a good contraction at the top, without chasing extreme range.
- **Does not hold when**: injury or pain limits range (then work within the pain-free range and consult a professional); or when you deliberately use a "lengthened partial" or other technique that the evidence here does not cover (unclear in source).

## 6.5 Mind-muscle connection (internal versus external focus)
- **Claim A (bodybuilding tradition)**: concentrating on the working muscle improves its activation and growth.
- **Claim B (motor-learning research)**: an *external focus* (the outcome, moving the weight) improves performance across many tasks, while an internal focus does not.
- **Evidence**: a trial of 27 young men over eight weeks (barbell curl and leg extension, four sets of 8-12 to failure, progressive overload) found more biceps growth (over 5%) with an internal focus and no difference in quadriceps growth, and no strength difference. Possible reasons offered: better coordination of the upper limbs than the lower; an effect that might appear in the legs with more experienced lifters.
- **Caveats**: one study, short, young and not highly trained, two isolation lifts; effects on strength lifts were not tested; a small difference in effort between conditions could contribute.
- **Rule of thumb (position)**: use internal focus on isolation and machine work where feel is easy; use external cues (drive the floor away, push the bar) for heavy compound lifts.
- **Does not hold when**: attention to the muscle slows the movement so much that load or effort drops.

## 6.6 Assistance exercises
- Accessory work should be chosen by what limits the main lift (a weak lockout, a weak start) and by tolerance; the more advanced the lifter, the more tailored. Fixing technique (for example bar path) usually comes before adding more accessories (see [ch10](ch10-mechanics-of-the-big-lifts-and-when-form-matters.md)).
