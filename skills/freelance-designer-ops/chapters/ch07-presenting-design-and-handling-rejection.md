# Chapter 07: Presenting Design and Handling Rejection

## Core Idea
Presenting the design is as much of the job as making it, and a rejection is information, not a verdict. The presentation is built around the client's story and problem, shows the path from brief to concept so the idea does not look plucked from thin air, and closes by returning to the original problem. When a client rejects, stubbornly insists, or says "too simple, fill the space", the routine is the same: detach emotionally, ask questions to find the need behind the reaction, argue with facts tied to the client's money, and keep testing every iteration against the original goal. Strong designers are separated from average ones by business and communication skills (explaining decisions, asking smart questions, saying no), not by tools.

Delivery craft (voice, stage presence, persuasion psychology) is in `public-speaking-persuasion`; deck craft in `presentation-design`; ch01 here covers the "person / professional / project" mindset about criticism. This chapter adds the concrete routines.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Client presentation structure (8 beats)**:
1. Client backstory, ethos and the problem in one slide (reflect their words in taglines).
2. The three main objectives (optionally a checklist you tick off at the end).
3. Your process, as a short step-by-step.
4. The concept and its rationale (colour, type, shape, layout) with real research behind each claim.
5. The design in context, with mockups of real situations.
6. Alternatives, side by side, when the client's idea differs from yours.
7. Return to the original problem and show it solved, with one sentence on business value.
8. Next step: open line for feedback, comment section, contact.
When to use: any pitch of a logo, identity, deck or page.

**One long mockup document**: build one very tall page (about 1920 px wide) once and reuse it as a template: synopsis and goals; dissected technical view of the mark; colour scheme with rationale; type rationale; mockups (box, business card, brochure, signage) in the brand's style; imagery. Do not email a pile of separate designs with "please review".
When to use: logo and identity presentations.

**Reject-and-recover loop**: detach → ask → find the thing "on the cusp" → test against the goal → pause and push back if drifting → compromise by shipping the clear version and parking the fancy one.
When to use: when a client says "feels sterile", "not me", "too simple".

**Stubborn / "too simple" three-step**: (1) build your strong concept *and* the client's idea, side by side; (2) explain each decision with facts and point out concrete flaws in theirs (white space aids navigation and comprehension; longer dwell time with pleasant reading; a cramped layout loses readers); (3) tie the argument to money (fewer readers, fewer clicks on the CTA, fewer sales). Backstop: contract and deposit (ch06).
When to use: "fill the space", "make the logo bigger", "my child likes this colour".

**Designer-skill list (ten)**: business acumen; explaining decisions; talking about impact rather than aesthetics; asking smart questions; selective pushback; not getting defensive; offering multiple solutions; saying no; knowing when to go deep versus shallow; choosing which hills to die on.
When to use: self-review once a quarter; pick the weakest one and practise it on the next project.

## Rules & Practices

### Presentation
- Never present in a plain word-processor file. Use the same fonts, colour scheme, shapes and layout throughout; apply hierarchy (focal image, headings, body) exactly as in the design itself.
- Match industry conventions: sleek modern type for tech; formal, conservative for finance or law. Study the industry's palettes, type and visual elements before choosing.
- If the client has a strong identity, theme the presentation around their colours and typeface, with their logo in the header.
- Link choices to the brief's goals early (for example modernise an outdated logo against new competitors), and relate them to the target audience.
- Show the design in practical situations: clients cannot visualise a logo on a building or a package. Free or paid mockups are faster and cleaner than a DIY photo shoot.
- Justify colour, type and shape psychology only after real research (for example orange for action and energy). The client picks a colour because their child likes it: do not accept it without an argument.
- Stay polite, confident and calm. You are a salesperson for the idea; finish with the best form of the concept.
- Show process to reassure: sketches, keyword boxes, mood board (ch04 inspiration hygiene).
- Do not present too many options: offer one recommended direction, plus at most two alternatives with their trade-offs.

### Handling rejection and criticism
- Separate feedback on the work from a personal attack; apply what is needed and accept the rest without changing.
- Ask: priority level, expected evolution, what to avoid, differences per user, what success looks like. A "simple" feature often has much more underneath.
- Treat a reaction ("feels like a credit score, sterile") as information about what the client is reaching for (playful, warm) and test that against the real goal.
- Show early low-fidelity structural concepts to test direction before investing in finish.
- Check each iteration against the goal. If a playful variant weakens the meaning of the thing (a score, a warning, a price), stop and ask whether you are drifting. When quality or trust is the goal, clarity first, playfulness later. Park elaborate ideas (3D renders, mascots) for a later version.
- Do not rely on colour alone to convey status: add icons, micro labels and numbers. Avoid symbols that collide with existing meanings (stars next to a star rating).
- When told "too busy", ask why. Treat critique as free education; ask other designers for critique and give them thoughtful feedback in return.
- Avoid emotional investment in a direction: keep alternatives alive until the client chooses.
- Pick battles: most hills are not worth dying on; agree and move forward unless the issue affects the goal, accessibility, legibility or the client's business.

### Design principles to cite in the room
- Hierarchy: a three-level text structure (heading, body, subtle lower text; or heading, subheading, body) with contrasting pairs (serif with sans, bold with thin).
- Purpose first, and the exclusions it implies.
- Consistency across the series and across platforms; logos supplied as a kit of parts (sizes, more or less text, layouts, backgrounds, colour versions).
- Three-second glance test: look at the finished design for three seconds as a feed viewer would; where does the eye land first and next? If unclear, the hierarchy is broken.
- Simplicity wins fast-glance media; the most intricate concept is not always the one that wins.

## Procedures
**Prepare a presentation (90 minutes)**
1. List the client's story, three objectives, audience and industry conventions.
2. Choose three mockup contexts that the client will recognise.
3. Build or reuse the long mockup document; replace content.
4. Add one slide on process and one that returns to the problem.
5. Rehearse the first 60 seconds aloud.
6. Send a short summary of decisions after the meeting.

**Respond to a rejection (within 24 hours)**
1. Acknowledge, do not defend.
2. Ask two questions: what specifically is not working, and what would success look like?
3. Restate the goal in one sentence and check the objection against it.
4. Prepare a side-by-side: their idea and your recommended direction, each with pros and cons tied to the goal.
5. Agree a next step and a date; log the change in the revision count (ch06).

**Pushing back on a bad idea**
1. Say what is right in the idea.
2. State the problem in terms of the client's business ("readers lose the call to action").
3. Show a quick alternative next to it.
4. Ask which fits the goal better; let them choose with the evidence in front of them.

## Common Mistakes
- Slapdash or non-designed presentation; forgetting to close the loop on the original problem.
- Dumping several designs in an email with no story.
- Presenting only your concept when the client's idea is different, so they feel unheard.
- Treating rejection as failure or getting angry.
- Iterating until the design drifts from the project's mission.
- Letting a bad concept through because the client has a strong vision.
- Sounding like a know-it-all when critiquing others.
- Pushing back every time, which makes you the friction point.
- Seeking validation during the pitch.

## Mental Models
- A rejection tells you what the client could not see, not what you cannot do.
- The side-by-side moves a taste argument into an evidence argument.
- You are tied to the work for years: protect the portfolio.
- Present as if the client will repeat your story to their boss.
- Pushback is a spice, not the meal.

## Examples
- A playful score concept (toast with toppings up to caviar) was creative but rejected; the team moved to a clear structure (number, adjective, trend) with an emoji, colour system and progress bar, and kept the playful 3D idea for later.
- "Fill the empty space": a designer built both layouts, explained that white space speeds comprehension, pointed to faults in the crowded version, and linked the clean version to more clicks on the call to action.
- A logo pitch for a modernisation brief opened with the competitors' new look, showed the mark on a van, a shop sign and a phone app icon, and closed with one sentence on business value.
