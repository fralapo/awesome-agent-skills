# Chapter 01: What a Freelance Designer Actually Does

## Core Idea
A freelance designer's week is not "designing all day": it is two jobs, **organising** (timelines, project tracking, quotes, invoices, email, client calls) and **designing** (slides, websites, training material, content), plus — for many — building an audience that brings work in. There is no single correct shape for the job: each freelancer builds a personal "dimension" from strengths, weaknesses, aspirations, local context and skills, and is allowed to reshape it over time. A narrow niche such as presentation design is viable precisely because most designers dislike making slides, but a niche has a cost: the market must first be made *aware* it needs the service before the service can be sold, which takes far longer than selling a logo. The value sold is not "pretty slides" (any designer can do those) but graphic design + content structure/storytelling + public-speaking awareness, so the slides work together with the speaker's voice. Commercially, the healthiest clients are those who already know your positioning, trust your expertise, and accept a realistic timeline (a client deck takes about a month, part-time). Boundaries (hours, channels, scope), delegation (accountant, footage editor) and saying no to misaligned work are part of the job description, not extras.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Organise vs. Design split**: every working day mixes administration/coordination and production; plan both explicitly instead of treating admin as interruption.
When to use: when estimating capacity — only part of the week is billable design time.

**Your freelance "dimension"**: the job is shaped by (1) strengths, (2) weaknesses, (3) aspirations, (4) context (where you work, who is around you), (5) skills. The ideal formed at school (e.g. "the freelancer who serves local businesses") often turns out not to fit; let it change.
When to use: when deciding which services to keep, drop or add.

**Niche by specialisation**: just as "web designer" split into UI, UX, front-end and back-end roles, presentations deserve a specialist who combines design, storytelling and public speaking. The role exists if you name it and make it recognised; in the US and UK it is already a recognised corporate competence.
When to use: positioning statements, bios, proposals.

**Awareness before sale**: for an unrecognised niche, first educate (show bad vs. good slides, explain why design is not just "pretty/ugly"), then sell design, consulting and training to the people who now see the need.
When to use: when a niche service gets no requests even though it is on offer.

**Three reasons a deck takes ~1 month**: (1) the designer must absorb the client's subject until it is clear to them — otherwise it will not be clear to the audience; (2) many companies lack a usable brand identity, so a distinctive style must be researched; (3) "the first version is never the good one" — reviews and revisions are needed.
When to use: answering "how long can a few slides take?" and building quote phases.

**Person / Professional / Project separation**: criticism from a client is aimed at the professional, and really at the project — not at you as a person; and the project is not the most precious thing in the world.
When to use: difficult feedback, emotionally draining clients.

**Five rules for building your own dimension**: 1) start somewhere — you learn early what you do *not* like; 2) define your limits and communicate them; 3) allow yourself to change and test new activities; 4) get help — delegating is courage, not weakness; 5) build a made-to-measure job — take inspiration from others, never copy them.
When to use: early-career planning, yearly review.

## Rules & Practices
Services (a typical presentation/web freelancer's mix):
- **Client design projects**: presentation decks (corporate, convention/event decks, report decks with many numbers), websites (showcase sites, built with a developer partner who codes), templates.
- **Consultations**: 1–1.5 h maximum, for freelancers and small businesses. Receive the slides in advance and fix them *during* the session — deliver a concrete before/after, not just advice. Tiring (constant topic switching, no time to go deep) but transformative.
- **Training**: schools and companies; compact formats of 2, 3 or 4 h up to multi-day programmes (possibly with other professionals), online or in person. In person gives the client more value; cap travel because it drains energy.
- **Online course production**: e.g. pitching yourself to a course platform (what you do, your results, the course you propose), then writing and recording it to the platform's guidelines.
- **Content creation** for your own channels — treat it as a project where you are the client.
- Drop services that scatter energy or don't fit your brand (logo/brand identity work was dropped in favour of "few things done well": slides and websites). Refer out-of-scope requests (logos) to other designers.

Typical week (one proven pattern):
- **Monday**: own projects — morning recording content, preparing cover/b-roll material (screen recordings of slide building, exported pieces) and briefing the editor; afternoon a personal side project (e.g. a podcast with a peer).
- **Tuesday–Thursday**: client work — design, training, consultations, client management (calls and email; clients spread across the country, few local).
- **Friday**: write next week's content and plan next week's slots; lighter tasks in the afternoon.
- **Weekend**: no work; at most Saturday morning to catch up on content.
- Work Monday–Friday until 18:00 and rarely exceed it.
- An earlier split that also worked: ~2 days/week on social channels, 3 days on clients.

Client relationship rules:
- Email is the official channel; avoid WhatsApp and especially voice messages. Move Instagram DMs to email.
- Phone number to clients is your choice — you are not obliged to be always reachable.
- Ask many questions at the start to understand the real size of the project; a job described as "small" can turn out much larger.
- Tight deadlines are workable (one convention deck was done in ~2 weeks) only if you dedicate every day to that single project; a second tight request (a numbers-heavy report deck) had to be declined.
- Aim for clients who trust you: a "no, I don't like it" with no reason is the main source of friction; explain the *why* of each choice.
- Clients who come from your content are the best fit: they already know your skills and character, so collaboration grows instead of clashing.
- Be wary of print-only projects if errors cause you anxiety (print cannot be changed after the run; digital can); accept them as conscious exceptions.
- When you involve many contributors (e.g. art-directing 60 students), merge selected ideas and credit their authors so everyone feels ownership.
- Clients usually ask for PowerPoint; Keynote projects are the exception.

Client acquisition channels (in order of appearance in a career):
- Your school/training network (internships, teachers, professionals around the school) and referrals passed on by other freelancers.
- Teaching/collaborating with an institution (kept work flowing even during lockdowns: education never stopped).
- An agency that subcontracts (e.g. websites).
- Social content: the strongest channel over time; even TikTok brought corporate clients. A first large webinar (800+ participants) triggered training and design requests.

Admin and pricing (as covered):
- Admin = quotes, invoicing, bureaucracy; most freelancers procrastinate it — delegate the fiscal side to an accountant from day one.
- Track a budget per project and an estimated-hours total set from the budget and the time you have available; sell hour packages (e.g. 10 hours "on consumption") and monitor them (see Chapter 02).
- Quote phases explicitly: content immersion, style research, revision rounds.
- Rework your quote template when you want to — being able to reshape your business is one of the freelance freedoms.

Italy-specific (partita IVA) notes:
- While studying, work with *prestazione occasionale* / *ritenuta d'acconto* (occasional-work receipts) to test freelancing without opening a VAT number; this phase lasted ~3 years. It can feel less professional when pitching companies.
- Open the partita IVA when work becomes regular (here: the January after graduating).
- Under the *regime forfettario* the start-up rate was 5% for the first 5 years, then 15%.
- In the forfettario, income counts on the **cash-in date** (*incasso*), not the invoice date: an invoice issued in December and paid in January falls into next year's income.
- Invoices may need a *marca da bollo* (stamp duty) added manually; electronic invoicing is mandatory and usually done in the accountant's portal.
- Taxes, contributions (INPS) and ATECO codes are confusing at first — this is what an accountant is for.
- First-year revenue target: keep it prudent and reachable (account for your real living costs); small, reachable targets avoid demoralisation.

## Procedures
**Positioning a niche service**
1. Name the role (e.g. "presentation designer") and state the combination you sell: design + storytelling + speaking.
2. Publish educational content consistently on the niche (what makes slides bad/good).
3. Convert attention into a first event (webinar, workshop) to prove demand.
4. Package: design projects, 1–1.5 h consultations, 2–4 h trainings, multi-day programmes.
5. Keep one adjacent service you enjoy (e.g. websites) even if only partly aligned with your public positioning.

**Quoting a presentation project**
1. Discovery call: ask about audience, occasion, deadline, number of slides, existing brand assets, who will edit the file later.
2. Probe hidden scope (extra decks, animations, numbers-heavy sections).
3. Plan ~1 month part-time: immersion → style research → paper sketches → first version → revisions.
4. Set budget and estimated hours; check your Gantt/timeline for overlaps before giving a start date.
5. Send the quote by email; log the client in your CRM pipeline (call → quote → awaiting reply → client / lost).

**Running a hands-on consultation (1–1.5 h)**
1. Receive the deck beforehand.
2. Pick the slides with the biggest gain.
3. Redesign them on screen while explaining the reasoning.
4. Leave the client with the before/after file.

**Setting boundaries**
1. Decide working hours (e.g. Mon–Fri until 18:00), channels (email), phone policy.
2. State them clearly at onboarding.
3. Refer out-of-scope work to trusted colleagues.

## Common Mistakes
- Accepting "it's just a few slides" at face value → ask scope questions first; quote phases.
- Promising a deck in days → explain the three reasons it takes ~1 month.
- Treating every request as yours (logos, branding, footage editing) → specialise; refer or delegate.
- Handling clients over WhatsApp voice notes → email as the official, traceable channel.
- Taking client criticism personally → separate person, professional, project.
- Selling a niche nobody understands yet → build awareness with content before expecting sales.
- Copying another freelancer's career model → borrow ideas, test them, drop what doesn't fit you.
- Postponing the accountant to save money → delegate fiscal work from the start.
- Over-committing to travel-based training → keep in-person sessions but cap them.

## Mental Models
- "Designers hate making slides" — the dislike of others is your market.
- "There is something magical in doing well what everybody hates doing."
- The more complicated and ugly the starting material, the more interesting the redesign.
- Variety of industries is a feature: not "I only work for food/fashion"; the constant is turning ineffective slides into ones that attract the audience.
- Slides almost always work *with* the speaker's voice: a multi-form design object.
- Getting help is an act of courage.
- The freelancer you are now is not the one you were five years ago — and that is good.

## Examples
- A designer trained in a hands-on vocational graphics school (internships 2 days/week in the 4th year, a final-year business "project work" with SWOT and competitor benchmarking) gains business basics that later help price, position and plan a freelance practice.
- A convention deck for a tech company, built in Keynote with rich animations in ~2 weeks; the companion numbers-heavy report deck was declined because the deadline was impossible.
- A film-production company's showcase website: the smoothest project of the year because the client listened and shared a creative vocabulary, even though the style was far from the designer's own.
- Art direction of a school calendar with 60 students: ideas from a huge brainstorm merged into one concept, contributors credited by name, print production followed on site.
- A 1.5 h consultation where a small business's deck is fixed in real time on screen, leaving a concrete before/after instead of a to-do list.
