# Chapter 09: Career Habits and Designer-Founder Side Projects

## Core Idea
Two long-run questions sit beside client work: how a freelance designer keeps getting better and more visible over years, and whether (and how) to run a product of their own. The first is answered by a short list of habits: learn one scary thing a year, keep the fundamentals sharp, write the goal before designing, treat feedback as free education, and stay findable. The second is answered by discipline about *where design time goes*: a designer-founder's default is to polish, because polishing is comfortable, while the work that decides success (talking to users, distribution, asking for money, tracking) is uncomfortable. Treat a side product as a time-boxed experiment with a stated success measure, use design depth only where traffic exists, and keep expectations realistic: most advice online is survivor-biased.

The UX mechanics of a product (onboarding, paywall models, closed beta, validation signals, shipping versus polishing, design QA, audit method) are in `ux-ui-expert` ch38, ch40 and ch41; this chapter covers the business and career side that those chapters do not.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Six career-stalling habits and their fixes**:
1. Not learning → pick one new tool or skill per year that slightly scares you (3D, motion, a prototyping tool, AI coding tools).
2. Random colour → choose colour by meaning and audience; test combinations with a harmony tool. Neon green with bright orange is wrong for a luxury fashion brand.
3. Skipping fundamentals → keep practising hierarchy (an event date in tiny type at the bottom fails); train yourself to name the principles in ads, layouts and logos.
4. Resisting feedback → treat it as free education; ask why when told "too busy"; trade critique with other designers.
5. Designing without a goal → write one or two sentences stating the goal and keep them visible.
6. Staying invisible → a single-page showcase if there is no portfolio, one networking event a quarter, a findable name (ch08).
When to use: the yearly review in ch04; pick the one habit you are weakest at.

**Signature style = consistency + versatility**: reflect on what feels effortless; gather inspiration by noticing what you save or stare at; spot patterns instead of copying; let the style evolve. Forcing a style instead of leaning into strengths is the common mistake.
When to use: deciding which work to show and which to refuse.

**Niche discovery questions**: which industries excite you, which challenges do you enjoy, where are your skills strongest? Niching makes you the go-to expert (eco-conscious branding, event design) without meaning you refuse everything else.
When to use: with ch01 (dimension) and `brand-strategy-storytelling` ch03.

**Memorability toolkit** (for the work you sell): layered palettes and gradients; universal metaphors (sprouting seed for growth, overlapping circles for unity); a surprise detail; repeated brand elements varied across campaigns; tactile finishes or subtle sound. Craft detail in `art-director` and `graphic-designer`.
When to use: the concept feels competent but forgettable.

**Time-boxed experiment**: state in advance the duration, the effort and what counts as success; review at the end; decide to stop, change route or double down.
When to use: any side product, content push or outreach play.

**Lifecycle email jobs**: education (get value in the first 7 to 14 days), trust, urgency (an offer) and conversion (trial to paid). Flow: a 14-day onboarding series about every 2 days (about 6 emails with upgrade nudges); a purchase triggers a separate flow that skips the trial emails; an expired trial triggers a win-back every 2 days for a week; non-converters go to an ongoing nurture (weekly or 1 to 2 a month).
When to use: a product or paid offer with a trial; also a client project's email setup.

**Growth loop questions**: what do users create that others consume; where can sharing happen naturally; how do you reduce friction; what is the aha moment and how fast can a user reach it? Mechanics: every view of a shared item is brand exposure; a state-aware CTA (signed out: "Get it free"; signed in: "Share"); several share paths (link, email, embed, social); per-audience landing pages on one template with audience-specific language and proof; community content; nudges at positive moments.
When to use: designing a product's growth surface for a client or yourself.

## Rules & Practices

### Habits
- Treat your own project like a client project: goal sentence, brief, review.
- Learn design principles by looking: ads, layouts, logos; name the principle at work.
- Ask for critique in community spaces, and give some. A critic who sounds like a know-it-all is not asked again.
- Inspiration is for recognising patterns, never copying (ch04).

### Designer-founder discipline
- **Choose a proven category** with a small twist, so you do not need to prove demand; scope it so one person can design, build and ship it.
- **Time-box** design plus build to about two weeks, then fix bugs, then submit. Skip architecture documents, a marketing site and design details early.
- **Design 3 to 5 key screens** and use them as the style reference for the rest.
- **Anti-"AI slop" checks**: set a design direction yourself first; make tools reference existing screens for colour, type and spacing; replace placeholder icons and default backgrounds; change a few colours and images; add small touches.
- **Go deep on the surfaces that move numbers** (onboarding drop-off points, paywall, store page, landing page) and only when enough people see them. Three viewers a day is not enough data. Do not polish micro-interactions, rare edge cases or settings screens.
- **Design as coping mechanism**: when design time replaces marketing, outreach or a launch date, the discomfort is usually the right work. Any distribution activity is an acceptable use of early time; when one gives a signal, double or triple down on it.
- **Paying users over free users**: ten paying users give better signal, feedback and word of mouth than a thousand free ones; free tiers attract lurkers and blur product-market fit. For low-ticket or running-cost products a hard paywall after onboarding with a trial and an annual plan fits (see `ux-ui-expert` ch38 for the model by price).
- **No paid ads** until organic content shows which message converts; then fund the winner only while return exceeds spend.
- **Content for a product**: choose two or three specific target groups and make every piece speak to their pain points. Views are not the goal: nostalgia clips with 50,000 to 100,000 views produced no downloads, while lower-view niche content produced several. One targeted newsletter feature brought hundreds of sign-ups overnight; targeted beats broad.
- **Persona accounts**: for each persona (for example the self-improvement reader, the work-from-home professional) a dedicated account and content built on their real pain points.
- **Track from day one**: ask "where did you find us?" in onboarding. About three quarters of downloads came in the first three weeks of one launch with no source data other than the store's own numbers.
- **Test early, repeat before believing**: test hooks, opening visuals, formats and faces; check that a hit repeats before calling it a trend. At very low volume, views do not correlate to downloads.
- **When behaviour contradicts interviews**, follow behaviour while tracking both: a banner every tester disliked still raised sales in every other placement test.
- **Public site as trust**: the public website is the first impression; the product can be iterated after. Fully generic AI-built front pages risk looking like the cheap web of the past. Trades that live on referrals may not care.
- **Habit**: one small action on the project each day, before the day job, builds the consistency muscle.

### Expectations
- A 90-day experiment of 3 short videos a day produced 127 downloads, 18 trial starts, 30 in-app purchases, 11 payers and 526 in revenue, about half from people close to the maker. Online playbooks sell the dream outcome and hide earlier failures, luck and timing (the 41st app after 40 misses).
- A big agency-built first product, extra features, minimal marketing and a free tier kept too long was an expensive way to learn the same lessons; a second product iterated for six months without a second launch or any charge was the same error.
- Be sceptical of claims of large monthly revenue in three months; limit time on social feeds.
- Quality over volume once burnout appears; consider a route with more leverage (a B2B angle).
- Talk openly with a co-founder about self-pressure; avoid working weekends; keep it fun.
- Light retainer clients with a few small monthly updates can be handled asynchronously with short screen-recorded videos and fund travel or side projects.

## Procedures
**Quarterly career check (30 minutes)**
1. Which of the six habits did I neglect?
2. Which new thing am I learning this year, and what is the first small project?
3. When did I last ask for or give critique?
4. Is the goal sentence written on my current project?
5. Do I have a visible, findable body of work (ch08)?

**Side-product experiment brief (half a page)**
1. Persona (two at most) and their pain point.
2. Time box (for example 2 weeks to build, 30 days to market) and effort per day.
3. Success measure (for example 10 paying users, 100 email sign-ups) and stop rule.
4. Tracking: source question in onboarding, funnel events.
5. First distribution action this week.
6. Review date; one thing to change.

**Email lifecycle draft with AI**
1. Prompt: "Act as an expert email marketer. Audience, product, goal, trial length. Ask me questions before writing."
2. Answer, then request exact copy and cadence.
3. Treat the result as a draft; use a simple template (header, title, details, image, CTA, fallback link); one use case per email; a short GIF of the workflow in the welcome.
4. Build the template once in your design tool, then in the email tool; avoid custom layouts that need a developer.

## Common Mistakes
- Assuming experienced designers can skip basics.
- Taking critique personally, or ignoring it.
- Obsessive design before traction (a whole day on a paywall nobody sees).
- Collecting onboarding data and not using it.
- Chasing a viral hit; delaying analytics to week three.
- Copying another app's playbook and expecting the same result.
- Posting for volume alone, or obsessing over a single project.
- Designing email last or forgetting it exists.
- Relying on paid marketing instead of a built-in loop; one generic landing page for all audiences.
- Ignoring an inner doubt, or hiding stress.

## Mental Models
- Comfort is a signal: the task you avoid is usually the one that moves the business.
- A free hour of your time is cheap if it is capped; an open-ended one is not.
- Referral and network effects often matter more than the direct target of an effort.
- Beware of single-anecdote advice in both directions: the free-work story (ch05) and the app-growth playbooks are each one person's result.
- Core craft and taste outlast tools.

## Examples
- A product built in two time-boxed sprints: sticky-note the flow, write every onboarding step word for word, turn it into a requirements document, build screen by screen from 3 to 5 designed key screens.
- A launch whose creator learned only in week four, from a source question added late, that most downloads came from one platform and one audience.
- A weekly mix for a founder account: strategy, stories, product updates and shared work, with about an hour a day of thoughtful comments.
