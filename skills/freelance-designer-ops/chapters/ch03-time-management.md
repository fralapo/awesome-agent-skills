# Chapter 03: Time Management for Freelance Creatives

## Core Idea
Managing time as a freelancer is not scheduling every minute; it is creating **focused blocks** of similar work, placed where your energy fits them, each ending with a concrete **goal**. The result is working less but more focused, knowing what was and wasn't done, and living more calmly — physical and emotional wellbeing is the actual purpose. The method combines three tools: **Google Calendar** for blocks (one calendar per life/work area, used as colour labels), **Google Tasks** for the goal of each block, and a **paper notebook** for the small tasks that pop up and for writing down what you actually did. Blocks are 1–4 h, typically 3–4 h on a single project, because jumping between activities wastes energy. Planning happens weekly (Sunday evening or Monday) or day-by-day when there are only small tasks; buffers sit between events; unfinished work moves to tomorrow without guilt — the tools are there to help, not to enslave. Estimation and tracking close the loop: log hours per project, compare with the budgeted hours, use the timeline to give realistic start dates, and accept that a deck takes about a month. Finally, perfectionism needs a stop rule: after giving 100%, learning to settle for "good enough" is legitimate, because a slide the designer finds imperfect will most likely serve the client and their audience perfectly.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Calendars as labels**: create one calendar per macro-area, each with its own colour, so the week is readable at a glance (the default account calendar for calls; Administration; Content Creation; Family — shared; Training; Meeting; Personal; Project — client work; Selfcare; Task — linked to Google Tasks; plus Google's undeletable Reminders).
When to use: setting up any digital calendar; works on paper diaries with coloured markers too.

**Energy-mapped scheduling**: put high-energy/creative work where you are most productive (for many: mornings) and calls/less creative work in the afternoon; put calm tasks on days that drain you (e.g. travel days).
When to use: building the weekly template.

**Block + Goal**: every block has a stated outcome via Google Tasks, so you never spend 4 hours without a target. Goals can be partial ("reach the middle of the outline") or deliverable-based ("share with the client at the end of the block").
When to use: every design, writing or recording block.

**Batching**: group similar tasks into one block (record 2–3 pieces of content in a row instead of one and then something else).
When to use: recording, editing, admin, email.

**Notebook triage**: micro-tasks (reply to an email, do X) go into a paper notebook and are cleared at the end of the day or the next day; the same notebook records what was done — you usually did far more than it feels.
When to use: days full of small interruptions.

**Good-enough loop breaker**: sketch on paper → first digital version disappoints → it looks even worse days later → new references (Pinterest, Dribbble, Behance, Awwwards) → new versions… a perfectionist loop. Stop when you know you gave 100%; the audience's needs, not your taste, are the criterion.
When to use: a slide that has absorbed hours without converging.

## Rules & Practices
Calendar rules:
- Google Calendar chosen because it is free, synced on phone/tablet/computer (Apple or Android), customisable, and shareable with collaborators on multi-professional projects.
- Custom colours via hex codes (e.g. generated on coolors.co); never recolour single events — the calendar colour *is* the label.
- Optional emoji/icon in the event title for even faster recognition (easier to add from the phone).
- Recurring events for fixed rhythms: content shooting every 2 weeks on Monday 8:00–12:00; morning routine daily.
- Event fields worth using: Google Meet link, location, notification, calendar (= label), busy/available, description with what to do, guests.
- Leave a **buffer**: if an activity ends at 9:45, start the next at 10:00 — don't schedule back-to-back; don't schedule every break either.

Block sizes:
- Blocks of 1, 2, 3 or 4 h; the typical block is **4 h** of one activity.
- Design work: keep **3–4 h for one project at a time**; switching between activities is not effective.
- Tight-deadline projects: dedicate whole days to that one project; you cannot carry others in parallel.

Weekly rhythm (example template):
- Monday morning: content (shooting every 2 weeks, 8:00–12:00) — start the week with work you like while fresh; Monday afternoon: clients.
- Tuesday–Thursday: client projects.
- Thursday: editing of the long-form piece so it is ready for the following Monday (moved there because Monday releases kept slipping).
- Friday: write next week's content, plan next week; calm tasks in the afternoon if Friday involves travel.
- Weekend off; pauses are important.
- Planning: Sunday evening or Monday; weekly goals when projects can be fully scheduled; day-by-day when the load is small tasks. Next-week slots are blocked progressively during the current week as you see where projects stand.
- Fixed end of day: Monday–Friday until 18:00, rarely exceeded, to protect mental health.

Estimating and tracking:
- Log each work session (task, start, end, project) — ideally same day; a "Yesterday" view catches late logging.
- Compare hours done vs. budgeted total hours per project; over 100% = overrun.
- Use a Quarter-scale timeline before promising dates: if four projects close in the next month, start the new one after.
- Rate projects Low/High energy; overlap two Lows freely, avoid two Highs.
- A presentation for a client: ~1 month elapsed, not full-time (immersion in the subject, style research, revisions).
- Time tracking is an indication, not a constraint.

Protecting non-client work:
- Content creation always drops to the bottom of the list when client work piles up; protect its slot or delegate parts (editing handed to an editor with a fixed budget freed mental space).
- Rest: take brief breaks *within* the day before stress peaks; dutiful people only stop when a task is done and drift into burnout.
- Working alongside someone (a partner or peer in the same room) makes it easier to take breaks because they take them.

Example morning routine (5 habits kept for about a year):
1. Wash the face with cold water. 2. Drink a glass of water. 3. 5 minutes of breathing/meditation. 4. Stretching and 10 push-ups. 5. Journal, reflecting on the previous day.

## Procedures
**Set up the calendar system**
1. List your macro-areas (calls, admin, content, family, training, meetings, personal, client projects, selfcare, tasks).
2. Create one calendar per area.
3. For each: three-dot menu → "+ Add custom colour" → paste a hex code (pick a palette on coolors.co).
4. Enable the Task calendar and open Google Tasks in the right-hand panel.
5. Create recurring events: Does not repeat → Custom → Repeat every 2 weeks → End: never / on date / after N occurrences.

**Plan a week**
1. Sunday evening (or Monday morning), review project status in your project tracker.
2. Place high-energy blocks in your best hours, calls in the afternoon, light work on draining days.
3. For each block, add a Google Task with its goal (e.g. "record 2/3 long-form pieces", "record IG/TikTok pieces", "outline to halfway", "send to client at block end").
4. Insert 15-minute buffers between events.
5. Keep the notebook open for incoming micro-tasks; clear them at day end.

**Close the day**
1. Log hours in the tracker (or tomorrow via the Yesterday view).
2. Write in the notebook what you did.
3. Reschedule what didn't get done to the next day — without guilt.

**Break a perfectionism loop on a slide**
1. Return to the paper sketch: is the idea still right?
2. Gather a limited batch of fresh references once.
3. Produce a bounded number of new versions.
4. Choose the version that best serves the client's audience and stop.

**Estimate a new project's time**
1. Look up similar past projects and their logged hours in the tracker.
2. Set Total hours from the budget and from the time you actually have available.
3. Add the non-design phases: subject immersion, style research, at least one revision round, client feedback waits.
4. Check overlaps and energy on the Quarter timeline; give a start date only after that.
5. Track during the project; if Hours % passes ~100%, talk to the client before continuing.

## Common Mistakes
- Scheduling every minute → schedule blocks and goals, not minutes.
- Open-ended 4-hour blocks → add a Google Task goal to each.
- Back-to-back events → 15-minute buffer (9:45 → 10:00).
- Alternating unrelated tasks → batch similar work.
- Micro-tasks scattered in the calendar → paper notebook, cleared at day end.
- Recolouring individual events → colour comes from the calendar/label.
- Feeling guilty about unfinished items → move them; tools serve you.
- Letting content creation absorb Mondays only when there's no client work → fixed slot or delegation.
- Endless revisions of one slide → settle for good enough once you've given 100%.
- No fixed stop time → end at a fixed hour (e.g. 18:00).

## Mental Models
- The calendar is a map of energy: colour density per area shows at a glance whether a week is balanced between clients, content, admin and selfcare.
- A goal at the end of a block converts "time spent" into "result obtained".
- Partial goals are honest goals: "halfway through the outline" beats a vague "work on the outline".
- Time management is a wellbeing tool first, a productivity tool second.
- Energy, not hours, is the scarce resource: switching costs energy, batching saves it.
- "Even when it feels like you did nothing, you did a lot" — write it down.
- Periods differ: weeks immersed in one big project alternate with days of a thousand small things; the system must handle both.
- High and low workload phases are predictable after a few years; learn to schedule around them and to enjoy the quiet ones.
- The client's audience, not the designer's perfectionism, is the quality bar.

## Examples
- A Monday content block 8:00–12:00 recurring every two weeks, with tasks "record 2/3 long-form pieces" and "record Instagram/TikTok pieces if scripts are ready".
- A 2-hour block for a training outline with the goal "at least halfway", and a design block whose goal is "share the draft with the client at the end".
- An event "slide design XY" in the orange Project calendar ending 9:45; the next one starts at 10:00.
- Editing moved from Monday to Thursday so the weekly release is ready for the following Monday.
- A slide redesigned through several reference rounds that never quite satisfies its designer, delivered anyway — and it works for the client's audience.
