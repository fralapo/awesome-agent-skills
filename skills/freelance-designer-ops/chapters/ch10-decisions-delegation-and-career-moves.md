# Chapter 10: Decisions, Delegation and Career Moves

## Core Idea
A solo practice runs on a handful of recurring decisions: what to accept, what to drop, what to hand to someone else, when to change direction. Most of the cost is not in choosing wrongly but in choosing slowly, or in never choosing. This chapter gives a small toolkit for deciding at the right speed, auditing and protecting time, delegating step by step, and making career moves (finding work, losing a job, leaving one) from a position of choice. All numbers below are illustrative heuristics from practitioners' teaching, not measured rules; adapt them to your own risk and income.

Weekly planning, calendar blocks and energy mapping stay in ch03; contracts and cash collection in ch06; yearly goals in ch04; career habits and side products in ch09.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Reversibility test**: sort a decision by how hard it is to undo. Reversible choices (a tool, a posting rhythm, a trial collaboration) are taken fast, with partial information (a common heuristic is to act once roughly 70 percent of the information is in); irreversible ones (a long exclusive contract, a large investment, closing the practice) are taken slowly. Delegate technical choices to the person who knows far more than you.
When to use: any time you notice yourself researching a small choice for days.

**Process versus outcome**: a good process can produce a bad result and a poor one a good result. Judge a decision by what you knew when you made it, then improve the process, not your self-image.
Related: `behavior-and-decision-science`.

**Cost of inaction**: not deciding is also a choice and often the most expensive one (a lead that goes cold, a hire not made, a price not raised). Weigh it explicitly against the downside of a wrong reversible move.

**Four tools for a weighty decision**:
1. Compare at least two or three real alternatives, not "do it or not".
2. Scenario planning: write a best case, a worst case and an unexpected case for each option.
3. Pre-mortem: assume the choice failed a year from now and list the reasons.
4. Value model: list the criteria that matter to you, weight them, score each option, and read the total as a prompt for discussion, not a verdict.
When to use: pricing model change, taking a big client, hiring, moving country.

**WOOP and backward planning**: state the wish, the best outcome, the main inner obstacle and an if-then plan for it. For long goals, start from the end state and work back to this week.

**Time audit**: before reorganising, record what you actually do for about four weeks. Start from the time you really have, not from the to-do list; then ask what wastes time, what could be delegated, what could be dropped. Treat meetings as the exception. Work in continuous blocks of about 90 minutes with a break of about 20 (illustrative). Efficiency is doing things right; effectiveness is doing the right things.

**Few priorities, many "not now"**: write a long list of goals (for example 25), pick the few that matter (for example 5) and treat the rest as a "not now" list, not a "later" list. Do a task at once if it takes two minutes; otherwise one thing at a time. Plan the next day the evening before with a short list (about 3 to 6 tasks).
Related: ch03 (blocks and goals).

**Delegation in seven steps**:
1. Accept that early attempts often fail; the first helper is rarely the last.
2. Decide that you really want to grow beyond your own hours.
3. Find candidates on freelance marketplaces or in your network.
4. Shortlist three.
5. Give a paid test of about 10 days.
6. Hand over a precise task list and a report format.
7. Expect the collaboration to end at some point and keep the process documented.
Start with the tasks you do worst or enjoy least. "It is faster if I do it" is true for one task and false over a year.

**"Who can help?" instead of "How do I?"**: shape the practice like a conductor or gardener who chooses the components and sets the direction. Results come from a team and a network; a lone-hero story hides it.

**Attract work, do not only apply**: a CV is a low-cost signal about the past. Costly signals work better: an analysis made for a target company, a public record of how you think, a small case study proposed to a named contact, value offered before being asked. Choose sectors and companies first, then produce for them. Keep the CV as a passport next to this.
Related: ch05 (routes), `brand-strategy-storytelling` ch06.

**Job-loss sequence**: first steady yourself and avoid decisions made out of desperation. Then do a financial due diligence (how many months before the money runs out). Then a burst of actions: find organisations that are genuinely hiring (for example recently funded startups), consider B2B, consider geography, build a case study and propose it to a contact, use professional networks.

**Changing or leaving a job**:
- Why people stay: perceived security, loss aversion and sunk costs, identity, habit, fear of uncertainty.
- Before leaving: a cash cushion of at least three months (six to twelve is safer; illustrative); find the real cause of the unease (one practitioner estimates the context is the problem about half the time, not the work itself); run small experiments in spare time; talk to people in different jobs.
- Designing a role: three questions: what am I better at than the people around me, what do I enjoy, what does the market pay for today? Think in problems rather than job titles. Every path has a price (uncertainty or regret).

**Remote work and remote teams**: judge by results and deadlines; routines and discipline are the work; loneliness is the common weak point. For a remote team choose people who write well, give autonomy, set clear guidelines at the start and "trust but verify".

## Rules & Practices
- Time-box the research: set a deadline for a reversible decision and decide when it ends.
- Too many options produce paralysis: cut to two or three before comparing.
- Write down why you decided, so the outcome can be judged later against the information you had.
- Do a quarterly pass: which standing commitments still pass the "would I accept this today?" test.
- Delegate only what you can brief in writing; keep review short and on results.
- For leaving or switching, change one variable at a time (client type, then service, then location).

## Procedures
**Decision memo (15 minutes)**
1. State the decision and the deadline.
2. Reversible or not?
3. List two or three alternatives, including "keep as is".
4. For each: best, worst and surprising outcome.
5. Pre-mortem: why did it fail?
6. Choose, write the reason and the first action; set a review date.

**First delegation test**
1. Pick one recurring task you dislike (for example invoicing prep or footage editing).
2. Write the steps and the expected output.
3. Shortlist three people; give the same paid test to the final one or two.
4. Review the result against the written list; keep, adjust or replace.

**Four-week time audit**
1. Log blocks in the calendar as they happen, with the area label.
2. At the end, total hours per area and per client.
3. Mark what wasted time, what to delegate, what to drop.
4. Rebuild next month's blocks from the free time that remains.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating every choice as irreversible, or an irreversible one as a trial.
- Waiting for complete information.
- Judging decisions only by outcome.
- Planning from the to-do list instead of the hours that exist.
- Delegating with no written brief, or never delegating because "it is faster alone".
- Applying only through the front door while ignoring costly signals.
- Leaving a job in a panic with no cushion, or staying for years because of sunk costs.
- Copying a famous person's routine; most productivity advice is personal.

## Mental Models
- Most decisions are doors you can walk back through; a few are one-way.
- Time is the one resource you cannot buy more of, so audit it like money.
- Attention to a few things beats a long list of intentions.
- Work you attract tends to fit better than work you chase.
- Illustrative numbers (70 percent information, 90-minute blocks, 3-month cushion) are starting points to test, not rules.
