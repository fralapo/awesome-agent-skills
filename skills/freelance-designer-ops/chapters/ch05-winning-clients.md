# Chapter 05: Winning Clients: Routes, Outreach and the Prospect Pipeline

## Core Idea
Clients arrive through visibility plus trust, and trust is slow: a buyer may follow you silently for months, and a referral may land 1, 2, 6 or 9 months after the job that caused it. So client acquisition is a portfolio of routes run at the same time, each with a clear job: referrals (the cheapest), a maintained prospect list (the most neglected), time-boxed unsolicited work aimed at one named recipient (the most deliberate), content aimed at one segment (the slowest and most scalable), and freelance platforms (the most crowded, to be used as a funnel and not as a business model). Everything is measured by the leads it produces, not by views or applause. A rejected proposal is treated as a diagnosis of your own process, not as a verdict on you.

This chapter is about *finding and warming* leads. Pricing, discovery calls and closing are in `pricing-and-sales-negotiation` (ch05 to ch08); the content system is ch08 here; the portfolio is ch08 here and `brand-strategy-storytelling` ch08.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The five acquisition routes**: (1) referrals and past clients; (2) prospect list and periodic contact; (3) time-boxed free audit sent to one named recipient; (4) targeted content for one segment; (5) communities and platforms (professional network groups, niche forums, freelance marketplaces).
When to use: auditing why the pipeline is thin: which route have you not run for 90 days?

**Prospect list (five sources)**: previous clients; people who enquired but never started; people met at networking events; email-list contacts; social contacts with a visible business need.
When to use: every month. Someone on it will eventually need a designer, and being the one who kept in touch is the advantage.

**Free-audit play (time-boxed)**: pick a startup or small company without in-house design, spend a fixed 2 hours on its product, annotate what you find, redesign two or three key screens or pages, record a screen video of 5 minutes or less explaining your reasoning, and send it as a gift whether or not they ever hire you.
When to use: when you have a defined segment and a slot of spare capacity. See the decision rule below for how this fits the "never work for free" stance.

**Prospect temperature ladder**: cold (never heard of you) → warm (follows your work, replied once) → ready (has a budget and a date). Match the action to the rung: value to cold, a question to warm, a proposal to ready.
When to use: deciding whether to message, nurture or quote.

**Rejection audit**: after a lost proposal, ask three questions: was my communication polite and professional; did I follow my full process (discovery, process explanation, clear next step); did I invest enough hours to understand the client? Change the process, not your self-image.
When to use: after every lost proposal, in writing, in five minutes.

## Rules & Practices

### Referrals and past clients
- Do excellent work immediately, and add a small unrequested extra (for example an hour helping with a tool the client is stuck on). Rigid "that is not my job" boundaries cost referrals; proud professionals skip the extra, so it is cheap to stand out.
- Message every person you have worked with in the past: ask whether they have work, or know someone who does. This is the highest-return half hour in a slow month.
- Ask for the referral at the moment of client happiness (delivery day), with a specific, low-pressure request ("do you know one other founder who has the same problem?").
- Retention touches for existing clients: a short check-in that includes relevant news for their business; a yearly "wrapped"-style summary of what the work achieved; a small gift at project end. Previous buyers are the likeliest to buy again.

### Prospect list
- Keep it in the CRM (ch02): name, source, last contact, next touch, one note about their business.
- Touch each person periodically with something useful (a relevant example, an observation about their market), not with "do you have work?" every time.
- Keep a separate business account on each social network, not linked to personal ones, with one memorable identity (same name, photo, colours) everywhere. Put the words people search for ("graphic designer", "logo design", your niche) in bios, post text and image alt text.

### Time-boxed free work
- Target startups: they have no full-time designer, the most visible room for improvement, a pace that misses details, and openness to unsolicited help.
- Set a 2-hour timer. The cap is what makes it safe: little downside if it goes nowhere.
- Show how you think, not only visuals: list inconsistencies, hierarchy problems, onboarding length and what could be merged, plus two or three genuine questions for the team.
- Send it as a direct message to the founder or decision-maker, framed as a gift. Expect nothing: in the cited example the recipient never replied, but three months later a referral thread shortlisted the designer from about 200 candidates, and an 8 to 9 month contract worth roughly 25 to 30 thousand followed, then another referral. The payoff is usually indirect.
- This is one anecdote. It worked because it was specific, personalised and capped, not because free work is good in general.

**Decision rule (free work)**: *you* choosing to send one capped, targeted sample to a named prospect is marketing spend. A *client asking* you for free work, "exposure" or unlimited spec work is a pricing question: use the triage in `pricing-and-sales-negotiation` ch08 (charity versus client, value-equivalence) or decline. Never run open-ended free work, and never do it for the same recipient twice.

### Content for clients
- Aim at one specific segment (for example marketplace founders at one funding stage). Content for "the masses" reaches no one.
- Clients rarely need you on the day they see the post; the goal is staying top of mind. People buy from people they like and relate to.
- Full system in ch08.

### Communities
- Professional-network groups: join niche, closed communities, settle in, answer questions, give specific feedback. Never drop your link into threads.
- Facebook-style groups and forums: add value first (answer, help, share a resource). Spamming links is the fastest way to be muted.
- Creative networks (Behance, Dribbble, audio rooms): build themed collections ("luxury branding for boutique hotels") and comment with specific, constructive points, never "great design".
- Attend at least one networking event per quarter. A client who searches your name and finds nothing hires someone else (see "personal SEO" in ch08).
- Pitching design publications works with a personalised submission: research their tone and audience, address the editor by name, send a detailed process breakdown, and mention awards even if local.

### Freelance platforms
- Marketplaces are saturated and price-led (logos at a few currency units each). Use them as a funnel for a few good first clients, not as a business model.
- Find a niche within a niche ("logos for DJs", "manga illustrators") and give it an appealing title; stand out with thumbnail, profile photo, title and a short intro video.
- Write a custom proposal that reuses the client's own phrases; templates read as cold. Answer fast, because response time is a ranking factor and a trust signal.
- Offer fixed, named packages; ask every client for a review; work toward platform badges; treat competitors as a network, not as enemies.
- Move repeat clients off the platform only if its terms allow it; leaving a platform removes its payment protection, so take a deposit (ch06).

### Engagement models in the current market
- Entry-level UX roles are very hard to get; experienced designers with a network do better. Graphic design and branding are an easier entry for beginners: build a portfolio, ask for referrals, volunteer on real projects for mentorship.
- Startups increasingly use a pattern of *intensive, then light, then intensive*: a designer full-time for about 3 months on an MVP, redesign or major feature; scaled back to roughly 15 to 20 hours a week or 20 to 40 hours a month for upkeep; then called back for the next big piece. Offer this shape explicitly and plan your capacity around it. For how to price it, see `pricing-and-sales-negotiation` ch11.
- Experienced designers should learn AI prototyping tools: companies prototype and validate with them, then bring in a designer to refine.
- Business and communication skills (ch07) differentiate you more than tools.

## Procedures
**Monthly 90-minute pipeline routine**
1. Update the prospect list: add new names from the month, set the next touch for each.
2. Send three useful touches to people at the "warm" rung.
3. Message two past clients with a specific question, not a generic "any work?".
4. Pick one targeted account and run the 2-hour free audit if capacity allows (otherwise skip, do not squeeze it in).
5. Log the source of every new enquiry (ch08, attribution).

**Rejection audit (5 minutes, written)**
1. Re-read the thread: tone, response time, clarity of next step.
2. Check against your process: discovery done? process explained? scope and price stated once, clearly?
3. Ask the client, politely, for one reason. Treat the answer as free education.
4. Write one change to your workflow. If the reason was price, do not discount; see `pricing-and-sales-negotiation` ch12 (raise value, not cut price).

**Launching a platform profile**
1. Pick the niche-within-niche; write the title around the outcome.
2. Record a 30 to 60 second intro video.
3. Publish 2 to 3 fixed offers with visible deliverables.
4. Reply to every request within hours, with the client's phrases quoted back.
5. After each job ask for a review the same day.

## Common Mistakes
- Relying only on freelance platforms.
- Spamming links in groups, or sending templated proposals.
- Sending a bare design with "please review".
- Open-ended unpaid work with no time box.
- Treating a lost proposal as failure or getting angry instead of auditing the workflow.
- Letting the prospect list go cold; contacting it only when you are short of work.
- Content for everyone, so that no one feels addressed.
- Expecting an immediate return from outreach or content; referrals are delayed.
- Rigid boundaries on what you will do for existing clients.

## Mental Models
- Trust compounds silently: the buyer who books after six months treats the first call as an existing relationship.
- A referral is the payoff of work you did earlier, so the quality of delivery is the acquisition strategy.
- Time-boxing converts a gamble into a cheap experiment.
- Saturation is a reason to narrow, and a competitor can be a colleague.
- Keep two lists, one of people who might buy and one of people who might refer.

## Examples
- A founder ignored a free audit and video; three months later a referral thread named the designer, who was shortlisted from about 200 candidates and won an 8 to 9 month engagement.
- A designer who messaged every past contact, did an hour of unrequested help for a client stuck on an email builder, and kept doing excellent small jobs received referrals months later through team chats and meetups.
- Platform positioning contrast: a generic "I design logos" listing competes with thousands; "logos for DJs" with a short intro video and proposals quoting the job post's own wording competes with a handful.
