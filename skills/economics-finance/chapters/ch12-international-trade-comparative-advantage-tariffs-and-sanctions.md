# Chapter 12: International Trade: Comparative Advantage, Tariffs, Quotas and Sanctions

## Core Idea
Trade lets people and countries specialise in what they do at the lowest opportunity cost, so total output rises; but the gains are spread unevenly, so there are always losers, and restrictions have well-understood costs and beneficiaries. In this model the useful questions are: who has the lower opportunity cost, who bears the cost of a barrier, who captures the rent it creates, and whether the stated reason for restricting (jobs, security, leverage) survives scrutiny.

## 12.1 Comparative advantage
- **Thesis**: gains from trade depend on comparative, not absolute, advantage.
- **Mechanism**: suppose labour is the only input. Country A needs 1 unit of labour for a computer and 1 for a shirt; country B needs 12 for a computer and 2 for a shirt. A is better at both (absolute advantage), yet trade still pays. Opportunity cost: in A, one computer costs one shirt; in B, one computer costs six shirts and one shirt costs one-sixth of a computer. B has the lower opportunity cost of shirts, A of computers. If each specialises and trades at a price between the two ratios, both consume more than before. The same logic explains why a very productive professional should not iron their own shirts: the hour has a higher use elsewhere. It applies to people, groups and countries.
- **Trade-off**: aggregate gain does not mean everybody gains: if A starts trading with C, B can lose even while A, B and C together gain.
- **Counter-view**: the model assumes labour is the only input, no transport cost and mobile workers between sectors; in practice adjustment is slow and local (12.6).

## 12.2 A tariff in supply and demand
- **Thesis**: a tariff is a tax on imports; it raises the domestic price, reduces consumption, raises domestic production and generates revenue.
- **Mechanism**: with free trade the country buys at the world price; the gap between domestic demand and domestic supply is imports. A tariff shifts the effective supply curve up; imports fall; government earns tariff times remaining imports. Consumers lose more than producers and the government gain (a deadweight loss remains, general knowledge).
- **Trade-off**: protects a domestic industry at the cost of consumers and of exporting sectors.
- **Counter-view**: the large-country argument (12.5) says a country with market power can shift part of the cost abroad.

## 12.3 Tariffs versus quotas, and who gets the rent
- **Thesis**: a quota that limits imports to the same quantity as a tariff leaves price, consumption and domestic output unchanged; the difference is who captures the gap between domestic price and world price.
- **Mechanism**: model the quota as a leftward shift of domestic demand by the quota amount. The equilibrium is the same as under the tariff, but there is no tariff revenue; instead there are quota rents. Three possible holders:
  1. *Government auctions the licences*: equivalent to a tariff, rarely used.
  2. *Licences go to domestic firms*: they buy low abroad and sell high at home, so firms spend lobbying, bribes and waiting time to obtain them (rent seeking), which wastes the rent. Classic estimates put the rents from licences at about 7 percent of GDP in India and about 15 percent in Turkey (dated, approximate). Allocating licences by past output also encourages excess capacity.
  3. *Licences go to foreign exporters* (a so-called voluntary export restraint): the exporters earn the rent, so they oppose restrictions less, and incumbents may like the quota because it cements their position against new entrants.
  When the good has varying quality, a per-unit tariff or a quota pushes exporters to ship higher-quality versions (each unit counts equally); a percentage tariff does not distort quality the same way. The tariff-quota equivalence holds under competition; under monopoly or other market structures they can differ.
- **Trade-off**: quotas hide the cost in prices and rents instead of showing it as revenue.
- **Counter-view**: rent seeking is a more general idea than import licences: it applies to any political allocation of a valuable privilege.

## 12.4 Hidden protection through the exchange rate
- **Thesis**: deliberately weakening a currency can achieve what a tariff does without breaking trade rules.
- **Mechanism**: a cheaper currency makes imports dearer and exports cheaper to foreign buyers; domestic producers keep their market. Prices should adjust over time (purchasing power parity), so the effect depends on how long the gap lasts. Details and regimes are in chapter 05.
- **Trade-off**: consumers pay more for imports; the policy requires holding foreign assets or controls.
- **Counter-view**: trading partners may retaliate; a weak currency also raises the cost of foreign-currency debt.

## 12.5 Common arguments for restriction, in turn
- **Jobs**
  - **Thesis**: trade moves jobs rather than destroying them overall.
  - **Mechanism**: a country pays for imports with exports, so more imports mean more exports in the long run: jobs shrink in import-competing sectors and grow in export sectors; average wages rise through comparative advantage. Jobs are created and destroyed all the time because of technology and taste, not only trade.
  - **Trade-off**: the transition is hard for low-skill workers if education and retraining do not work.
  - **Counter-view**: concentrated, local job losses can persist for decades; compensation schemes are often weak.
- **Child labour**
  - **Thesis**: child labour falls as income per head rises; refusing to trade with poor countries tends to backfire.
  - **Mechanism**: a ban without alternatives can push work underground at lower pay and make families poorer and more reliant on it; free school meals and schooling raise the incentive to attend.
  - **Counter-view**: targeted standards on specific supply chains may work differently from blanket trade bans.
- **National security**
  - **Thesis**: some capacity (vaccines, defence inputs) is worth keeping at home.
  - **Mechanism**: nearly every industry makes the claim (even a livestock fleece was once protected by it), so the argument invites abuse.
  - **Trade-off**: security justifies a narrow list, chosen with an explicit cost.
- **Key industries and spillovers**
  - **Thesis**: some industries generate learning that spreads to others.
  - **Mechanism**: in practice nobody knows which; commodity chips earn little while design and retail productivity may spill more. Politics tends to choose based on the wrong reasons.
- **Strategic trade (market power)**
  - **Thesis**: a large exporter can limit or tax exports so domestic firms behave like a cartel and take more of the gains.
  - **Mechanism**: works if substitutes are scarce; retaliation and substitutes erode it in the long run, so the pie can shrink.
- **Summary (position)**: free trade is a policy that works well in most circumstances; protection works only in limited ones.

## 12.6 Who is exposed: an export-dependence and fragmentation map
- **Thesis**: a trade relationship can double as a dependency.
- **Mechanism**: the post-war reconstruction programme tied recipients to payments, convertibility and trade with the donor, and to the dollar's role in trade; it raised growth and built a security and trade alliance. The transatlantic goods-and-services flow is on the order of two trillion dollars a year (a snapshot of the reported period). When the partner relation weakens, the cost of replacing both markets and defence is large and slow.
- **Trade-off**: diversification costs efficiency.
- **Counter-view (position)**: whether a middle power can become independent of both large trading blocs within a realistic horizon is open (unverified).

## 12.7 Sanctions
- **Thesis**: sanctions are trade or finance restrictions imposed for political, not economic, reasons.
- **Mechanism**: types include military, diplomatic, sport and economic; economic ones restrict trade, currency flows, investment, corporate governance, or target sectors and individuals (asset freezes, travel bans). A tariff protects a domestic industry; a sanction aims at a security objective. Trade bodies police restrictions made for economic benefit but not those made for political reasons. The recent heavy package on a major economy blocked high-tech purchases, military equipment, froze assets of key figures and cut major banks from cross-border messaging; such side effects hit the sanctioning countries and the global economy, because trade partnerships carry trust.
- **Trade-off**: pressure on the target versus cost to the sender and to neutral third parties; sanctions can be circumvented through alternative payment channels.
- **Counter-view (position)**: whether they achieve their political aim is contested; here only mechanism and costs are given, not a verdict (unverified).

## 12.8 Export bans on raw materials
- **Thesis (position)**: a ban on exporting a raw material is meant to force local processing and capture more of the value chain.
- **Mechanism**: a resource in rising demand (lithium for batteries, with electric-vehicle sales rising) gives the holder leverage; smuggling can be large when mine wages are low and inflation history poor. Banning raw exports may attract processing investment if the country has power, skills and rule of law; otherwise it may only cut revenue.
- **Trade-off**: lost short-run export income versus a possible higher-value industry later.
- **Unverified**: the net effect of the ban on the country and on world prices; treat as an open question.
