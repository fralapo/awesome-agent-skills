# Chapter 04: Public Debt and Fiscal Sustainability

## Core Idea
A government's debt is sustainable not when the number is small but when it can keep refinancing at a cost that grows no faster than its income. In this model the quantities to watch are **debt relative to income (GDP)**, the **real interest rate against the real growth rate**, the **currency the debt is issued in**, and the **path** of spending and interest costs. A fixed "danger" ratio does not exist in the evidence.

## 4.1 Why debt is measured against GDP
- **Thesis**: euros of debt say little; GDP is the base that generates tax revenue and drives spending.
- **Mechanism**: taxes are a share of national income, so revenue rises when the economy grows; social spending rises when it contracts. So the debt-to-GDP ratio rises when debt grows faster than income. A "national debt clock" that counts euros ignores the denominator.
- **Trade-off**: GDP is itself affected by fiscal policy (see 4.6), so the ratio can worsen even when borrowing falls.
- **Counter-view**: some analysts prefer net debt (debt minus financial assets), or interest payments as a share of revenue, because the burden is the cost of servicing, not the stock.

## 4.2 There is no magic threshold
- **Thesis**: governments have defaulted at very different ratios, and some run very high ratios safely.
- **Mechanism** (dated snapshot): defaults occurred at roughly 75% of GDP (a large post-communist economy, 1998), about 65% (a South American economy, 2001), roughly 172% (a euro-area member, 2012), 160% (a commodity exporter, 2017) and 114% (an island economy, 2022), while a very advanced economy sustained around 250% without crisis. Research does not find a ratio at which collapse becomes automatic.
- **Trade-off**: the ratio is not useless, because a higher stock raises sensitivity to interest changes.
- **Counter-view**: earlier research claimed a threshold effect around a high ratio; later work disputed the data and method. Treat any single number as a rule of thumb, not as a law.

## 4.3 What actually precedes a default: rising real interest rates
- **Thesis**: governments default when borrowing costs rise faster than revenue, not just when debt is large.
- **Mechanism** (the doom loop): high interest on new debt raises the deficit; investors see higher debt and demand a higher rate; the deficit rises again; the government borrows just to pay interest; at some point it stops. In most of the studied cases (typically with foreign-currency debt) real interest rates (nominal minus inflation) were extremely high, often above roughly 20%, just before the collapse. Inflation alone did not predict it: it was very high in two cases, moderate in one and negative in two. The loop can explode quickly after years that look fine.
- **Trade-off**: lenders' reaction is partly self-fulfilling, so a country can be solvent on paper and still be driven into default by panic.
- **Counter-view**: some economists emphasise fundamentals (primary balance, growth) and others the role of a lender of last resort for governments, which stops self-fulfilling runs (a central bank willing to buy bonds changes the game, but see the risks in chapter 02).

## 4.4 The sustainability test: r versus g
- **Thesis**: compare the average real interest rate on the debt (r) with real growth (g).
- **Mechanism**: if r is below g, debt shrinks relative to GDP even with a primary budget balanced (balance excluding interest). If r is above g, the debt ratio rises on its own and the government must run a primary surplus, or cut spending, to stabilise it.
- **Trade-off**: a low r today may not last; the forward-looking comparison uses expected future rates (for example inflation-protected bond yields) against forecast growth.
- **Counter-view**: r is not given: it depends on investors' belief about the path. And g depends on the policy itself. Small differences in expectations flip the conclusion.

## 4.5 A case reading of the largest advanced-economy debt (dated)
- **Thesis (position, 2026 reading)**: a large issuer of the reserve currency is not at risk now, but its path can become unsustainable.
- **Mechanism**: the average rate paid on existing debt was about 3.8% against inflation near 3.5%, so the current real rate was only about 0.3% and below growth of 1.5%-2.5% (projections). But interest payments as a share of the economy were back at highs last seen in the 1990s and about 14% of spending, more than the defence budget in that reading; one budget watchdog projected interest overtaking a large health programme before the end of the decade. Investors' implied future real rate (about 2.15% from inflation-protected bonds) was roughly equal to expected growth. The sustainable-level claim is that the path, not the level, is the problem.
- **Trade-off**: AI investment may raise productivity and growth, which would help; if it proves a bubble, the burden gets worse.
- **Counter-view**: warnings about this debt date back decades and have not come true; the exact timing cannot be forecast (as with a bubble, direction is knowable, timing is not).
- **How a crisis would look (position)**: because the debt is issued in the issuer's own currency and the economy is diversified, the likely adjustment is **financial repression plus moderate inflation** (as in the UK after the Second World War), eroding the debt and making holders poorer, not a Venezuela-style default. The counter-view holds that markets could punish a loss of credibility much faster.

## 4.6 Austerity and the multiplier
- **Thesis**: a government is not a household; cutting spending cuts its own income.
- **Mechanism**: government spending is around half of GDP in many economies. Cutting jobs lowers incomes, which lowers tax revenue and raises social payments; the lost incomes reduce spending by others, which multiplies the fall. Cuts to education and infrastructure also lower future growth. A household's cut does not reduce its own income.
- **Trade-off**: spending cuts do reduce the deficit directly; whether the ratio falls depends on the size of the multiplier.
- **Counter-view**: for one southern European economy the evidence suggests that austerity after 2011 lowered GDP by an additional five percent (an estimate that is controversial) and may have **raised** the ratio. Austerity is not always counterproductive: if the multiplier is small, the economy is at capacity or investors demand credibility, it works. The slowdown of that southern European economy has several causes: productivity stagnating after joining the euro (no devaluation), small family firms slow to digitise, and weak public administration.

## 4.7 A growth problem, not a debt problem (position)
- **Thesis**: a high ratio can reflect a stagnant denominator more than rising borrowing.
- **Mechanism**: in euros the debt of the country grew about as fast as that of its peers after the euro crisis, because of strict primary surpluses; the ratio rose because output stayed flat while peers' output grew. Policy then shifts toward productivity: administrative efficiency, investment in education, health, rail and digital infrastructure, industrial policy for lagging regions, and EU-level support to cut borrowing costs for growth investment.
- **Trade-off**: those investments raise the deficit in the short run.
- **Counter-view**: northern creditors argue that debts need credible consolidation; the structural-reform camp says without reform more spending is wasted.

## 4.8 Borrowing currency and who bears the adjustment
- **Thesis**: a government that borrows in its own currency can always pay in that currency, and the question becomes inflation and the exchange rate; a government that borrows in foreign currency or has given up its currency cannot.
- **Mechanism**: the cases of default discussed here involve foreign-currency debt, a currency lock (a euro-area member, a dollar peg) or dependence on one commodity. A country issuing a reserve currency escapes some of these limits; the cost may fall on holders of the currency globally through inflation.
- **Trade-off**: control of the currency brings the temptation of monetary finance (chapter 02).
- **Counter-view**: giving up the currency (chapter 05) removes the temptation and the bailout at the same time.

## 4.9 Inflation and financial repression as ways of reducing debt
- **Thesis**: governments can reduce real debt without default by holding rates below inflation.
- **Mechanism**: force institutions to hold bonds at low rates while moderate inflation erodes the principal; use currency zones or allies to share the burden.
- **Trade-off**: savers lose; it works only when the debt is in a currency the government controls.
- **Does not hold when**: investors can move capital freely and the debt is short-dated or indexed.

## 4.10 Reading the global balance sheet (a dated case)
- **Thesis**: public debt sits in a world where every financial liability is someone else's asset; the net worth of the world is its real assets.
- **Mechanism**: in the reported accounts, world net worth was about 600 trillion (about 5.4 times GDP; peak 6.1 in 2020), debt about 2.9 times GDP, households owned about 95 percent of wealth, real estate over two thirds of real assets. Net-saver countries lend to deficit countries through safe government bonds, which lowers rates and raises asset prices in the borrower. About 75 percent of net worth growth since 2000 came from price rises rather than investment, so borrowing can chase the same assets. Case study: chapter 13.9.
- **Trade-off**: low rates ease debt service and inflate asset values; the gains accrue unevenly to asset owners.
- **Counter-view**: the claim that financial instruments net to zero is accounting only; solvency of each debtor still matters (4.1 to 4.4).

## Checklist
- Ratio, but also: interest bill as a share of revenue, real rate against growth, currency of issue, maturity, who holds it.
- Is the problem a stock (level), a flow (deficit) or a path (rising rate)?
- Check the denominator: did the ratio rise because debt grew or because output stalled?
- Remember that globally one party's debt is another's asset; ask who holds it and in which currency.
