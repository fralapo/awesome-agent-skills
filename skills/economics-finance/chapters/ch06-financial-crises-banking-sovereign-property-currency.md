# Chapter 06: Financial Crises: Banking, Sovereign, Property and Currency

## Core Idea
Crises happen where promises to pay on demand meet assets that cannot be sold quickly: bank deposits against loans, bonds against future taxes, mortgages against house prices, a fixed exchange rate against reserves. In this model a crisis has a slow cause (debts, valuations, mismatches) and a fast trigger (a loss of confidence). The direction of fragility is knowable; the **timing is not**.

## 6.1 Banking crisis: how a bank fails
- **Thesis**: a bank that loses depositors' confidence fails even if it might be solvent on paper, and often is also insolvent.
- **Mechanism** (chain from a large private-bank case, 2022-2023): losses on investment banking and wealth management exceed profits from the traditional retail unit; big depositors, especially wealthy clients, pull money out; turmoil elsewhere accelerates outflows; the bank's assets have lost too much value to cover liabilities; the central bank and other banks decline to save it alone; a rival bank takes it over.
- **Trade-off**: fast action prevents contagion; it concentrates the sector.
- **Counter-view**: economists dispute whether the outflows were rational (depositors were right to run if assets were impaired) and whether the failure threatened the whole system.

## 6.2 Resolution options: bail-out, bail-in, ring-fence, merge
- **Thesis**: when a bank with a vital deposit unit fails, there is a menu of ways to share losses.
- **Mechanism**: in the 2023 case, shareholders were heavily diluted, risky foreign bondholders lost their investment, depositors and key staff were protected, and the state offered a cheap loan to the acquirer and a guarantee on part of the losses, officially "not a bailout". An alternative used in an earlier European case: rescue **only the local banking units** that are critical, let the rest fail or be sold to the highest bidder. A bidder reportedly explored buying the risky parts.
- **Trade-off**: merger creates a "too big to fail" firm (here about twice the size of the home economy) and reduces competition; a split rescue is slower and legally harder.
- **Counter-view**: officials stress payment-system stability; critics suspect that the sector's interests were protected. The full motives cannot be known.

## 6.3 Moral hazard, deposit insurance and the lender of last resort
- **Thesis**: protection against runs encourages risk-taking.
- **Mechanism**: if shareholders expect rescue, they accept more risk; if creditors expect that depositors are always protected while bondholders are not, pricing of different instruments adjusts; early central bank rules limit support to solvent institutions at penalty rates (chapter 02).
- **Trade-off**: stability now against discipline later.
- **Counter-view**: the full-reserve camp argues the whole problem disappears if banks cannot create run-prone money; the opposing camp argues the credit supply would shrink.

## 6.4 Sovereign debt crisis
- **Thesis**: see chapter 04. The pattern is a rising real interest rate on public debt, not a particular ratio.
- **Mechanism**: investors' reaction creates the doom loop; a currency lock removes the lender of last resort; foreign-currency or single-commodity dependence amplifies it.
- **Trade-off**: austerity may stop the loop or make the denominator shrink.
- **Counter-view**: debt-to-GDP alone predicts poorly.

## 6.5 Property bust: investment, consumption and sentiment channels
- **Thesis**: a long fall in house prices harms the economy through three non-financial channels, even without a credit crunch.
- **Mechanism** (from a cross-country comparison of China with Japan and the US):
  1. *Investment channel*: less residential building means less work for architects, builders, realtors, electricians and furniture sellers. Residential investment as a share of GDP rose in China from about 2% to about 10% (1997 to 2020) and had halved by the time of the dated reading, much steeper than in Japan.
  2. *Consumption channel*: when households hold wealth in second or third homes, falling values reduce spending.
  3. *Sentiment channel*: areas where prices fell most also became more pessimistic about the economy, reducing spending further.
  The "credit channel" (banks stop lending, as in the West after 2007) was **absent** in China: state banks cut property lending but raised lending to advanced industries by almost 40%, keeping total lending growth around 10%. So the bust was offset by a manufacturing investment boom until a campaign against overcapacity cut that offset.
- **Trade-off**: shifting credit avoids an immediate crunch but builds new overcapacity and sends the surplus abroad.
- **Counter-view**: the pace of the adjustment is the open question: a sharp, fast clean-up like the US (banks cleared their debts quickly), or a long, Japan-style stagnation. A decline of roughly 30% in second-tier city prices and 10% in the top tier (dated snapshot) puts China at less than halfway on the Japanese path (comparison inexact) or about two thirds on the US path. Position: closer to Japan, so years of weak consumption. An economy that large cannot export its way out.

## 6.6 Property bubbles and speculation
- **Thesis**: when prices rise, speculators join, which raises prices more, until supply catches up or credit stops.
- **Mechanism**: positive feedback; construction booms; vacant homes appear (investors do not even rent them out); laws protecting tenants make quick sale of rented property hard.
- **Trade-off**: bubbles end in a rapid collapse that hits builders and lenders.
- **Counter-view**: low vacancy in a boom market suggests there is no bubble there, only shortage (chapter 07).

## 6.7 Currency crisis
- **Thesis**: a fixed rate collapses when the central bank cannot defend it.
- **Mechanism**: the central bank issues more currency than its foreign reserves cover; holders sell; reserves run out; the peg breaks and inflation reappears. A currency board or dollarization is a commitment device against this. A case: inflation fell sharply after a currency board in 1991 and rose after it was abandoned in 2002; a decade of high inflation followed.
- **Trade-off**: commitment brings credibility and also removes room to manoeuvre.
- **Counter-view**: critics blame the political failure to adjust fiscal policy, not the board itself; proponents of dollarization say the board is too easy to abandon.

## 6.8 Hyperinflation and why it is rare
- **Thesis**: very high inflation tends to come from sustained, massive money creation to finance the state, usually alongside collapsing supply or loss of currency trust.
- **Mechanism**: the government and central bank create money to pay for spending; the money supply rises faster than output; prices skyrocket; workers and firms index prices.
- **Trade-off**: governments get quick resources and destroy the currency.
- **Counter-view**: the existence of hyperinflation in some countries that used monetary finance does not show that moderate use causes it; the dispute is about the boundary.

## 6.9 Financialisation and asset-price inflation
- **Thesis**: when more transactions are financial, easy money inflates asset prices before it affects consumer prices.
- **Mechanism**: low rates and QE raise valuations of shares, homes, crypto-assets; leverage builds; central banks become cautious about tightening for fear of triggering a crisis; the asset market becomes a transmission channel back into the economy through wealth effects.
- **Trade-off**: supports credit and growth in the short run; inequality of wealth and unaffordability in the long run.
- **Counter-view**: some economists argue that asset prices reflect fundamentals, and that using monetary policy against bubbles is too crude.

## 6.10 How to read a crisis story
- **Thesis**: separate the slow vulnerability from the trigger; ask who is exposed to what.
- **Mechanism**: list the promises (deposits, bonds, pegs, mortgages), the assets that stand behind them, the maturities and currencies, and who will lose in each scenario.
- **Trade-off**: more detail brings more uncertainty about timing.
- **Does not hold when**: policy changes the rules mid-crisis; rescue design (who is paid, who is wiped out) is itself a policy choice with distribution effects.

## 6.11 Uninsured deposits and the 2023 US bank failures (a dated case)
- **Thesis**: a bank with a few very large deposits is run-prone even in a system with deposit insurance.
- **Mechanism**: the failed banks served niche clients (crypto firms, technology start-ups) with very large accounts (average account over one million in one bank, versus a national median near 5,300 in the 2019 data). Deposit insurance covered only the first 250,000, so most balances were uninsured and depositors had an incentive to run on any rumour; insurance had made runs by ordinary households a thing of the past. The backdrop was high rates, high inflation and falling bond values.
- **Trade-off**: unlimited insurance would calm large depositors but expands moral hazard and leaves the bank with many insured liabilities and high leverage.
- **Counter-view**: the case for and against removing the cap is a trade-off between panic risk and moral hazard; this trade-off has always existed in deposit-insurance design.
- *Whether such failures cause recessions is not settled here; chapter 13.6 shows a case where a collapse was followed by fast recovery.*

## Checklist
- What promise to pay on demand is at risk, and what backs it?
- Is there a currency or maturity mismatch?
- Which loss-sharing rule applies, and who decided it?
- For any bank failure, check size of deposits, share insured and how fast losses are shared.
