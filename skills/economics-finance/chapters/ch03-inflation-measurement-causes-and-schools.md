# Chapter 03: Inflation: Measurement, Causes and Schools of Thought

## Core Idea
Inflation is a simultaneous rise of prices on average. In this model there is **no single cause**: money creation, demand outrunning supply, supply shocks, expectations, wage and profit setting, exchange rates and sector shifts can each dominate in different episodes. Schools of thought each explain some episodes well and fail on others, which is why a good analyst asks which channel is operating now and what each school would predict.

## 3.1 Measuring inflation: basket, weights, housing
- **Thesis**: headline inflation is the change in the price of a representative basket, so what is in the basket and how it is weighted matter.
- **Mechanism**: the consumer price index multiplies average prices of goods and services by weights reflecting household spending (housing, food and transport dominate). Weights are updated only periodically. House purchase prices are left out of the index in both the US and euro area because a house is both consumption and investment and because the true cost depends on the mortgage rate. The US includes **owner's equivalent rent** (what an owner thinks the home would rent for), which moves more slowly than house prices when mortgage rates fall.
- **Trade-off**: leaving house prices out understates the felt cost of housing; including them would mix investment and consumption.
- **Counter-view**: critics say the index hides asset price inflation; defenders say asset prices are a different problem needing a different measure (chapter 07).

## 3.2 Statistical distortions in a shock
- **Thesis**: a sudden change in behaviour (lockdowns) makes fixed weights misleading, and missing prices distort comparison.
- **Mechanism**: in 2020 households shifted from services to goods but the basket weights were set earlier, so real inflation was probably higher than reported (one academic estimate put the official index too low by about 0.8 percentage points); closed restaurants had no posted price, so their prices were assumed unchanged. The result: **base effects**, where low measured prices in the shock year inflate the next year's rate.
- **Trade-off**: headline numbers can alarm or reassure more than the facts justify.
- **Does not hold when**: the underlying pressure is real; base effects explain part, not all, of a high number.

## 3.3 The supply-and-demand frame
- **Thesis**: if aggregate demand rises faster than aggregate supply, average prices rise; if supply falls with demand constant, prices also rise.
- **Mechanism**: *demand pull*: too much spending relative to productive capacity (stimulus, tight labour market, easy credit). *Cost push or supply push*: energy shortages, factory closures, port congestion, chip shortages, a war. A supply system built with maximum efficiency and minimal buffers transmits shocks strongly. One international banking institution estimated in 2021 that without supply-chain disruptions inflation would have been at least 2.8 points lower in the US and 1.3 in Europe (a dated estimate).
- **Trade-off**: the cures differ: demand pull responds to higher rates; supply push does not, and raising rates against it costs output.
- **Counter-view**: central bankers in 2022 described the US as mostly a demand shock (stimulus, strong labour market), the euro area as a cost shock (gas), the UK as both; critics note that the demand/supply split is hard to measure.

## 3.4 The monetary channel (quantity theory)
- **Thesis (monetarist position)**: inflation is always a monetary phenomenon; if money grows faster than output, prices rise.
- **Mechanism**: the equation of exchange (money times velocity equals price level times output) is true by definition. It becomes a theory only if velocity and output are assumed stable. Then new money must raise prices. Historical illustration: a sudden gold and silver inflow raised prices in early modern Europe.
- **Trade-off**: the framework is simple and gives a clear rule (steady money growth), and the idea of "too much money" sits well with intuition.
- **Counter-view (why it faded in universities)**: (a) central banks do not control the money supply; early-1980s UK money-supply targets were overshot by a wide margin and only a small part of broad money was created by the central bank, because most money is credit money created by private banks; (b) when a regulator tried to restrict institutions, they found new forms of credit money; (c) money growth and inflation diverged: the euro area, Japan and the US had large monetary expansion with low consumer inflation, because velocity fell and money went into asset markets; (d) credit money usually finances new production, so output rises with money. Monetarists reply that asset price inflation is inflation and that expansion can still bite later.
- **Question to ask**: is the new money spent on goods, saved, or placed in financial assets?

## 3.5 Employment, wages and the unemployment-inflation trade-off
- **Thesis**: when unemployment is very low, workers have bargaining power and firms can raise prices; when slack is high, wages and prices rise slowly.
- **Mechanism**: a stable historical relationship between low unemployment and high wage and price growth was built into policy. It broke in the 1970s with high unemployment and high inflation together (stagflation). It is also hard to read now: unemployment statistics count only active job seekers, early retirements and withdrawals from the labour force hide slack or shortage, and wages respond differently in different countries.
- **Trade-off**: a tight labour market is good for lower earners (wages and inclusion) and, per this model, inflationary only in the part not matched by productivity.
- **Counter-view**: one camp warns that overheating hurts the middle class most because it spends mostly on affected goods; another says overheating raises labour's income share, which may be the real point of dispute (a distributional conflict, not a technical one). Weakened unions are given as a reason wage growth stayed subdued even with low unemployment.

## 3.6 Expectations and the 1970s
- **Thesis (new Keynesian synthesis)**: inflation depends on current slack and on expected inflation; if the central bank is credible, expectations stay anchored.
- **Mechanism**: workers bargain for raises that compensate expected inflation, shops reprice in advance, banks lend more to pay for higher prices, so the expectation validates itself. Two versions: *adaptive* (past inflation shapes expectations, so an oil spike persists) and *forward-looking* (people judge whether the central bank will act). The policy implication: set the rate at the level consistent with non-inflationary unemployment and be credible.
- **Trade-off**: credibility requires being willing to impose costs; a central bank that cannot be believed has to do more.
- **Counter-view**: empirical support is thinner than the theory implies; correlations between expectations and inflation can run both ways. Labour unions were stronger in the 1970s, so expectations could pass into wages faster than now. Whether central banks can set expectations at will is doubtful: announcing a 10% target would not necessarily make people expect 10%.

## 3.7 Sector shifts and sticky prices
- **Thesis**: a large move of demand from one sector to another can lift the average price even if total demand is flat.
- **Mechanism**: after lockdowns, spending shifted toward goods (cars, with shortages and steep price rises) while service prices, tied to contracts and wages, adjusted slowly. Because prices are sticky downward, the average rises. If the shift reverses, the pressure fades.
- **Trade-off**: not a lasting cause on its own.
- **Counter-view**: a second wave from energy, wages and housing can make it persistent.

## 3.8 Profits, price-setting and "greedflation" (a contested school)
- **Thesis (post-Keynesian and some Marxist positions)**: firms with market power raise prices faster than costs, and corporate profits can drive inflation, particularly when supply is constrained and everybody can blame costs.
- **Mechanism**: bottlenecks reduce competition in the short run; firms pass cost increases and add margin; wages lag.
- **Trade-off**: gives a candidate explanation for 2021-2022 when profits rose while wages stayed subdued.
- **Counter-view**: profit-based accounts do not explain inflation in earlier periods (the 1970s, the 1990s steady years), and margin changes are hard to separate from cost increases. Treat as a candidate channel, not a general theory.

## 3.9 Government-centred theories
- **Thesis**: two schools tie the price level to the fiscal stance rather than to central bank money.
- **Mechanism**: (a) *Modern monetary theory* (position): the price level is set by what the government pays when it spends and by wage stabilisation through spending and monetary policy; a government that issues its own currency is limited by inflation and real resources, not by financial ability to pay. (b) *Fiscal theory of the price level*: the price level adjusts so that the real value of government debt equals the present value of future surpluses.
- **Trade-off**: both offer a story for the low inflation after 2008 (governments were cautious) and the spike after large stimulus, and bring fiscal policy back into the discussion.
- **Counter-view**: mainstream critics think MMT understates the political difficulty of raising taxes or cutting spending in time and the role of expectations, and that the fiscal theory is hard to test. These are positions; they remain live hypotheses.

## 3.10 Exchange rates and inflation
- **Thesis**: causality runs both ways, which makes the relation tricky to study.
- **Mechanism**: a depreciating currency raises import prices; excessive domestic inflation depresses the currency. For countries whose currencies dominate trade, the channel is minor; for smaller currency areas with high inflation (examples include Turkey, Lebanon, Brazil) it is a major channel (chapter 05).

## 3.11 The 2021 "transitory versus persistent" debate (a dated case)
- **Thesis**: in 2021 one camp argued the spike was temporary (supply bottlenecks, base effects, fiscal support ending, anchored expectations); the other argued it would last (labour shortages, aging, energy transition costs, loose policy).
- **Mechanism**: each side was right on different channels. Bottleneck supply issues were real; labour shortages and fiscal spending kept pressure on. The discussion also showed that the debate hinged on definitions: whether "inflation" means consumer prices or money growth, and on timing.
- **Trade-off**: waiting risks letting expectations drift; acting early risks a recession.
- **Does not hold when**: new shocks arrive; the same year a war raised energy and food prices, and the spike lasted longer than the transitory camp expected. Use this episode as a lesson in separating what is certain (channels exist) from what is not (duration).

## 3.12 How the schools arrived here (a short map)
- **Demand-side (Keynesian)** explained post-war inflation by overheated demand, failed in the 1970s.
- **Monetarism** won the argument in the 1970s, then failed when central banks tried to target money and when velocity moved.
- **New Keynesians** added forward-looking expectations and a controllable interest rate; their models seemed to work in the 1990s and 2000s and missed the 2008 crisis, a decade of low inflation despite stimulus, and the post-pandemic spike.
- **Challengers**: monetary revival, government-centred theories, profit-led accounts, cost-push accounts.
- **Position of the model**: "all of these theories are partly right"; inflation is like war, with many routes. The practical advice is to hold a list of channels (government spending, money creation, expectations, supply disruption, demand shifts, labour market, exchange rate) and check each against data.

## 3.13 Price indexes, real GDP and the quantity-theory arithmetic
- **Thesis**: three measurement ideas underlie most inflation discussions: the price index, the real versus nominal distinction, and the equation of exchange.
- **Mechanism**:
  1. *Price index*: a weighted average of the price of a large basket, set to 100 in a base period (1982 to 1984 in the US case); inflation is the percentage change over a year. In the reported series the index went from 44.4 to 49.3 between 1973 and 1974, an inflation rate of 11 percent; the US rate peaked a little over 14 percent near 1980 and was about 2.5 percent on average in the decades after. Index values near 330 in early 2026 mean prices more than tripled since the base period, which does not mean people are worse off if wages rose faster. Other countries have seen far higher rates (annual rate in the hundreds of percent in one recent year; monthly rates in the billions of percent at the extreme).
  2. *Real versus nominal GDP*: nominal GDP can rise because prices rise, so real GDP re-values output at the prices of a fixed year. In the reported series nominal GDP grew about 96 times from 1950 to 2025, but real GDP only about 9.5 times; per person the standard of living rose about 4.4 times. A fall in real GDP defines a recession and tends to come with a rise in unemployment.
  3. *Quantity theory in growth form*: money growth plus velocity growth equals inflation plus real growth. Velocity and real output are fairly stable in the short run, so large sustained price changes must come from money growth; in a sample of about 110 countries over 1960 to 1990 one extra point of money growth went with about one extra point of inflation, and in a hyperinflation case money and prices rose together by thousands of percent a year.
  4. *Aggregate demand curve*: all combinations of inflation and real growth consistent with a given growth of nominal spending (money plus velocity); more spending growth shifts the curve outward.
- **Trade-off**: the long-run neutrality of money is a strong claim that sits uneasily with short-run effects on output.
- **Counter-view**: for moderate inflation in the short run, velocity and credit money break the one-to-one link; the schools in 3.3 to 3.8 disagree on how much of 2021-type inflation this arithmetic explains.

## Checklist
- Is the figure headline or core, current or base-affected?
- Which channel (demand, supply, money, expectations, profits, exchange rate, sector shift)? Which school's evidence fits?
- Is "inflation" meant as consumer prices or asset prices?
- Convert any nominal series into real and per-person terms before comparing eras.
