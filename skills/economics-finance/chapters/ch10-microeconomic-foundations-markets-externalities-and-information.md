# Chapter 10: Microeconomic Foundations: Markets, Externalities, Monopoly and Information

## Core Idea
In this model a market price is a signal wrapped in an incentive: it tells buyers what a unit costs to make and sellers what it is worth to use, and it coordinates millions of choices without a planner. The free-market equilibrium maximises the gains from trade between the people who buy and sell. The interesting questions start where that result stops: when some costs fall on bystanders, when property rights are missing, when one seller faces no rival, or when one side of a deal knows more than the other. Each failure has a named cause and a menu of repairs, and each repair has its own cost.

## 10.1 Opportunity cost and trade-offs
- **Thesis**: the cost of a choice is the value of the next best alternative given up, not only the money paid.
- **Mechanism**: time, attention and capital are scarce, so every use excludes another. A rule that adds safety (a stricter building code) raises the money cost and the effort cost (permits, inspections) of opening a business; at a higher cost fewer businesses open. The trade-off is between the benefit of the rule and the activity it prices out.
- **Trade-off**: ignoring alternatives wastes scarce resources; calculating everything to the last cent is not the point either.
- **Does not hold when**: the choice is a binding legal obligation with no alternative; there the cost is compliance, not the foregone option.

## 10.2 Preferences and indifference curves
- **Thesis**: a consumer chooses among bundles by taste, within income and prices; an indifference curve joins bundles that give equal satisfaction.
- **Mechanism**: more of a good is preferred, so curves slope down and curves further from the origin mean higher utility; curves cannot cross (a crossing point would carry two utility levels). The slope is the marginal rate of substitution: how much of one good the person gives up for one more unit of the other. It usually falls along the curve (curves bow inward) because the more of a good one has, the less each extra unit adds, and the scarcer the other good, the harder it is to give up. Perfect substitutes give straight lines (constant rate); perfect complements give right angles (extra units of one good alone add nothing). For bads (pollution, waste) the direction reverses.
- **Trade-off**: a tidy model of taste; the budget line (income and prices) is what turns preferences into an actual choice.
- **Counter-view**: real preferences shift with fashion and information and are not always consistent; the model is a thinking tool, not a measurement.

## 10.3 What shifts demand
- **Thesis**: a price change moves along a curve; a change in anything else shifts the curve.
- **Mechanism**: an increase in demand means a larger quantity at every price, or equivalently a higher willingness to pay for each unit (the "vertical" reading). Five shifters: income (normal goods rise with income; inferior goods fall), population (number of potential buyers), tastes (fashion, information, ethics), prices of related goods (a rival good's price rises, demand for the substitute rises; a complement's price rises, demand for the partner good falls), and expectations (expected future price or scarcity moves purchases forward or back in time, such as stocking up before a storm or waiting for a sale).
- **Trade-off**: none in the definition; the care is in not mixing "demand rose" with "quantity demanded rose".
- **Does not hold when**: the good is rationed or capped (see 10.7); then the quantity bought is set by the rule, not the curve.

## 10.4 What shifts supply
- **Thesis**: supply shifts when costs change. Higher costs shift the curve up and left; lower costs shift it down and right.
- **Mechanism**: cost shifters are technology (automation lowers cost), input prices, taxes (a tax shifts supply up by exactly its amount; a subsidy is the opposite), entry and exit of sellers (opening a market to foreign producers adds supply; tariffs and quotas remove it), the opportunity cost of inputs (land can grow one crop or another, so a rising price of one crop cuts supply of the other), and expectations (storing output for a better expected price cuts supply today).
- **Trade-off**: a tax lowers supply; who ends up paying it depends on both curves and is not settled by the shift alone.
- **Counter-view**: in the short run some supply is fixed (existing buildings, planted crops), so the curve is very steep and the response comes later.

## 10.5 Equilibrium, surplus and the gains from trade
- **Thesis**: the equilibrium price and quantity are the only stable pair, and they maximise the sum of consumer and producer surplus.
- **Mechanism**: above the equilibrium price a surplus pushes sellers to cut; below it a shortage pushes buyers to bid up. For quantity: where buyers value the next unit more than it costs to make, there are unexploited gains and trade expands; where cost exceeds value, units are wasted and trade shrinks. Trading stops when value to the marginal buyer equals cost to the marginal seller. The price splits buyers (highest valuations) from non-buyers and sellers (lowest costs) from non-sellers, so goods go to those who value them most and are made by those who can make them most cheaply.
- **Trade-off**: efficiency in this sense says nothing about fairness of who has the money to buy.
- **Does not hold when**: costs or benefits fall on bystanders (10.8), rights are unclear (10.9), one seller dominates (10.11) or information is lopsided (10.12).

## 10.6 Elasticity (general knowledge)
- **Thesis**: elasticity measures how strongly quantity responds to price; it decides who bears a tax or a cap and how much revenue changes.
- **Mechanism (general knowledge)**: demand is elastic when many substitutes exist, when the good is a large share of the budget, or when buyers have time to adjust; it is inelastic for necessities and for short horizons. Supply is inelastic when capacity is fixed. The more inelastic side bears more of a tax. Elasticity grows with the time horizon.
- **Trade-off**: knowing the elasticity needs data; textbook labels (necessity, luxury) are rough.
- **Unverified**: elasticity is not developed here; treat this section as textbook background.

## 10.7 Price ceilings in one line
- **Thesis**: a price below equilibrium creates a shortage, lines and search costs, quality cuts, discrimination, side-payments and misallocation.
- **Mechanism**: the full seven-effect analysis for rents is in chapter 08; the generic logic here is that a cap shifts the allocation rule from price to something else (queues, connections, favouritism).
- **Counter-view**: caps can transfer income to covered buyers in the short run; see chapter 08 for the design caveats.

## 10.8 Externalities and the Pigouvian tax
- **Thesis**: when a decision-maker ignores a cost borne by others, the price is too low and the quantity too high.
- **Mechanism**: private cost is what the buyer or seller pays; external cost falls on bystanders; social cost is the sum. The supply curve reflects private cost only, so the market quantity exceeds the efficient quantity; the units between the two have social cost above value (the deadweight loss). Example: each use of an antibiotic adds a little resistance that spreads to everyone, yet the user does not pay for it, so use is too high. Who bears the cost is irrelevant to the efficient level; what matters is that all costs count. A tax equal to the external cost (a Pigouvian tax) shifts supply up until private cost plus tax equals social cost, so the market internalises the externality. External benefits mirror this: too little is produced.
- **Trade-off**: the tax needs a measurement of the external cost; too high or too low misses the target.
- **Counter-view**: command-and-control rules and bargaining (10.9) are alternatives; they have their own distortions.

## 10.9 The Coase theorem
- **Thesis**: the problem with an externality is not that it is external but that property rights are vague and transaction costs are high.
- **Mechanism**: if transaction costs are low and property rights are clear, private bargains reach the efficient outcome whichever side holds the right. Pollination services show it: bees fly short distances, so one beekeeper and one farmer can contract and internalise the whole benefit; pollination is a paid service industry. Pollution and flu shots show the opposite: thousands of unidentified parties, hard-to-measure costs and no agreed answer to who owes whom (an unvaccinated sneezer can be called a polluter too). Policy corollary: the state can define rights and cut transaction costs so a market can form (tradable permits), possibly with some rules.
- **Trade-off**: building a market needs clear measurement and enforcement.
- **Does not hold when**: parties are many, rights are contested, or bargaining power is wildly unequal; then costs of agreement dominate.

## 10.10 Common resources and the tragedy of the commons
- **Thesis**: a resource that is rival (use depletes it) but non-excludable (no one can be barred) is overused and under-maintained.
- **Mechanism**: a rancher who owns the stock keeps it for tomorrow; an open-access fisher who conserves only leaves fish for a rival. Three families of repair: command and control (cap boats or days) tends to fail by pushing equipment races (bigger boats), with rules growing ever more complex and seasons shorter; norms and local rules can work for small stable groups after a long time to evolve (studies of community management show the tragedy is not inevitable); property rights, such as individually transferable quotas that sum to a total allowable catch and can be traded, give each holder a stake in the stock, remove the equipment race and, in the reported case, led to recovering stocks and rising catch. Public goods (non-excludable, non-rival) are the neighbouring case; common resources are depleted by use, public goods are not.
- **Trade-off**: property rights require that the resource can be bounded and monitored.
- **Does not hold when**: the resource moves across borders (migratory fish): rights need a multi-country agreement, with high transaction costs and weak enforcement.

## 10.11 Monopoly: static loss, dynamic gain
- **Thesis**: a monopoly prices above marginal cost, so some mutually beneficial trades do not happen (deadweight loss); the rise in price is partly a transfer to the seller, which is neutral in efficiency terms.
- **Mechanism**: a half-empty theatre shows it: some viewers would pay more than marginal cost, but cutting the price for them means cutting it for everyone. A drug priced far above its pill cost wastes the trades of those who value it between cost and price. Against this, ideas have high fixed cost and near-zero marginal cost; if price fell to marginal cost the developer could not recover research cost, so fewer would be created. Hence a trade-off between static efficiency (quantity today) and dynamic efficiency (incentive to invent). Alternatives to patents: the state buys a patent at its expected monopoly profit and releases it (prices fall to marginal cost, incentive stays); prizes paid on a goal reached, with the result placed in the public domain; price discrimination (selling at different prices to different buyers) as a partial escape.
- **Trade-off**: buyouts need taxes (with their own deadweight loss), a valuation of the patent and protection from corruption.
- **Counter-view**: monopolies born of political favour (exclusive rights handed to relatives or allies) have costs and no offsetting benefit.

## 10.12 Asymmetric information and adverse selection
- **Thesis**: when one side knows more, the market can unravel.
- **Mechanism**: in health insurance, buyers know their expected cost; the insurer prices at the average. Healthier buyers find the price above their expected benefit and leave; the remaining pool is sicker, so the price rises, more leave: a death spiral. Repairs and their problems: inspection (a check-up) lets the insurer price by risk, but sorting costs more for the already sick, and a perfect test turns insurance into a bill; group coverage through employers works because the employer does not know much more than the insurer and buys for everyone, but ties cover to a job; a mandate to hold cover forces the healthy into the pool. In reality the insured can be healthier than average (propitious selection) because cautious people both look after themselves and buy cover, which can stop the spiral.
- **Trade-off**: risk pooling versus risk-based prices; a fully informed insurer cannot pool.
- **Counter-view**: the model predicts the sick buy and the healthy do not; the data often show the reverse because tolerance for risk differs. Moral hazard is the companion problem (behaviour changes once insured) and is not treated here.
