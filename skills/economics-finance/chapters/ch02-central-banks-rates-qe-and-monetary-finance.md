# Chapter 02: Central Banks, Interest Rates, QE and Monetary Finance

## Core Idea
A central bank is a bank for the government and for other banks that also issues currency and runs monetary policy. In this model it does **not** control prices directly: it sets the cost of bank reserves and shapes expectations, hoping that spending, credit and wages respond. Every tool works through the financial system, so its reach is partial and its side effects land first on asset prices and credit.

## 2.1 How central banking evolved (a pattern, not a date list)
- **Thesis**: central banks grew from private and city deposit banks that took on three jobs: settle payments for merchants, lend to the state, and rescue other banks in panics.
- **Mechanism**: early public deposit banks promised a coin per deposit and broke the promise in emergencies. A seventeenth-century Dutch city bank became a lender of last resort and later issued deposits not redeemable in coin, resting on trust. A Swedish private bank was among the first in Europe to issue banknotes and failed in the 1660s; a separate state-run bank was then founded. The English bank, chartered in the 1690s, was set up largely to fund a war; funding wars by issuing notes caused inflation, and the bank later suspended convertibility into gold from 1797 to 1821, issuing fiat money in practice.
- **Trade-off**: the same institution that stabilises banks can finance the state too cheaply, which weakens price stability.
- **Counter-view**: the historical record on whether central banks improved stability is mixed. Longer-run inflation was lower after the English bank's founding, but silver inflows and population swings confound that. Financial crises seem to have become less frequent in the nineteenth and early twentieth century under a strict lender-of-last-resort rule; high inflation and renewed instability came after the 1950s and again in later decades. "Correlation, not proof" applies both ways.

## 2.2 Lender of last resort rule
- **Thesis**: lend freely in a panic, but only to solvent institutions, against good collateral, at a penalty rate.
- **Mechanism**: early lending stops a run from turning a liquidity problem into insolvency; collateral and a high rate protect the central bank and discourage banks from relying on it; refusing insolvent banks limits moral hazard.
- **Trade-off**: the line between illiquid and insolvent is hard to draw in real time.
- **Does not hold when**: the government itself wants the bank to extend credit to it, or a bank is too large for the lender to save.

## 2.3 The standard toolkit: rate, guidance, balance sheet
- **Thesis**: three tools, one aim: change spending in the economy without touching goods prices directly.
- **Mechanism**:
  1. **Policy rate**: raised or lowered on loans to and deposits from banks; banks usually pass changes to borrowers and savers.
  2. **Forward guidance**: statements about the future rate path. Asset values depend on expected future rates, so markets react to the path, not only to today's move; a hike can lift stocks if the future path is milder than expected.
  3. **Balance sheet**: buying long-term assets with new reserves (quantitative easing, QE) or selling or letting them run off (quantitative tightening) shifts the supply of long-term assets and so long-term rates.
- **Trade-off**: each tool is blunt and works with lags, and communication can surprise markets.
- **Counter-view**: economists agree on the direction (higher rates reduce demand) but there is **no consensus on which channel dominates**; some research finds that interest rates have a small direct effect on firms' investment decisions.

## 2.4 Transmission: from the rate to prices
- **Thesis**: the rate affects prices through at least four chains.
- **Mechanism**:
  1. *Credit chain*: higher rates make loans and credit-card spending less attractive, so less demand; fewer loans to firms means fewer hires and slower wage growth, so slower price growth. Higher rates also reduce money created by private banks.
  2. *Asset-price chain*: higher rates make risky assets less attractive, prices fall, households feel poorer and spend less; lower share valuations make firms invest and hire less.
  3. *Expectations chain*: firms and workers that expect inflation raise prices and wages in advance; a credible central bank anchors expectations.
  4. *Exchange-rate chain* (open economies): higher rates attract foreign funds, the currency appreciates, imports get cheaper (see chapter 05).
- **Trade-off**: working through the labour market means that curbing inflation can mean job losses; the optimistic case is a tight labour market, where fired workers find new jobs and real incomes rise as inflation falls; the pessimistic case is overshooting into recession.
- **Counter-view**: critics say central banks "manufacture a recession" and have little power over supply shocks; defenders say a small rise in unemployment is the cheapest cure for demand-driven inflation. The expectations chain is disputed: a published central-bank staff paper argued the empirical case that expectations drive inflation is thin, and that realised inflation matters more once it is noticeable.

## 2.5 The zero lower bound and why QE was invented
- **Thesis**: cash pays 0%, so a policy rate cannot fall far below zero without risking a flight into cash.
- **Mechanism**: after the 2007-2009 crisis the policy rate was near zero, but the long-term rates on mortgages and car loans were not. The idea of QE: buy long-term assets with newly created reserves, which removes long-term assets from the market and adds short-term, low-interest ones; the price of long-term assets rises and their yield falls. Control of spending would then go back to the interest-rate framework, now applied at both ends of the curve.
- **Trade-off**: lower long rates support borrowing and asset prices at once.
- **Counter-view**: event studies of unexpected announcements find a substantial effect on asset prices, so the first step works; evidence on a large effect on consumer inflation is weak (see 2.6).

## 2.6 QE and inflation: why consumer prices stayed flat
- **Thesis (position, backed by studies)**: QE raised asset prices much more than consumer prices; it is not the main cause of the post-pandemic inflation spike.
- **Mechanism**: lower rates across the board encourage spending, but in a financialised economy a large share of the extra spending goes into homes, shares and other assets, not goods. Consumer inflation therefore stayed near target through years of QE, and the biggest buyer of all (Japan's central bank) saw inflation near zero. The spike came after the pandemic, supply chain shocks and fiscal stimulus.
- **Trade-off**: because asset prices are inflated, central banks fear that withdrawing support will trigger a financial crisis, which makes unwinding slow.
- **Counter-view**: monetarist-leaning economists argue that large money creation must show up somewhere and warned of inflation; some say lagged effects explain the 2021-2022 spike. A third view is that QE raised wealth inequality and housing costs, which are real harms even if not captured in the consumer price index.

## 2.7 QE is not the same as money printing
- **Thesis**: QE swaps bonds for reserves; the public's money holdings change little.
- **Mechanism**: the central bank buys bonds from banks or investors and credits the seller's bank with reserves (money that only banks use). Money held by the public changes only to the extent that sellers are non-banks (their deposits rise) and banks or investors re-lend or re-invest. There is no check posted to households.
- **Trade-off**: more reserves, fewer bonds in the system; the public sector's consolidated debt changes composition (reserves instead of bonds).
- **Counter-view**: critics say that distinction is academic if the end result is cheaper government borrowing and higher asset prices; defenders say that distinction matters for predicting inflation.

## 2.8 Monetary finance: financing the government by creating money
- **Thesis**: a central bank that holds government bonds issued for the purpose is financing spending with new reserves, and consolidated, the government pays itself.
- **Mechanism** (balance-sheet chain): the government issues bonds (liability) and gets reserves (asset); the central bank holds the bonds (asset) and issues reserves (liability). When the government spends, the recipient's bank gets reserves and credits a deposit, so households end up with deposits. Compared with a bond sale to investors, the total deposits the public holds are the same in theory (banks can also create deposits when they buy government bonds). Advantages: speed and certainty (no need to guess a rate that clears the bond market) and lower effective cost (the cost is the rate paid on reserves).
- **Trade-off**: monetary finance is "too easy": it can tempt governments to overdo it, and excess money creation raises demand while supply cannot follow, so prices rise.
- **Counter-view**: many economists accept it as a **temporary** crisis instrument and fear permanent use (historical hyperinflations are tied to heavy use); others (government-centred schools) argue the line between monetary and fiscal policy is mostly legal convention. A promise that it is temporary rests on credibility, which is exactly what is in question.

## 2.9 Central banks as inflation fighters: is control complete?
- **Thesis**: central banks are not fully in control of inflation in a decentralised economy of millions of firms and households.
- **Mechanism**: they can lean on demand through credit and asset prices; they cannot produce gas, chips or shipping capacity. Their failures in both directions (no inflation for a decade despite stimulus, then forecasts missing a spike) show how limited forecasting is.
- **Trade-off**: credibility is built by acting firmly even at a cost.
- **Counter-view**: a school of thought emphasises that profit margins, supply shocks and fiscal policy matter more than rates; mainstream economists answer that rates still lower demand and so cut inflation, as the doctor-and-smoking analogy goes: probable, not certain.

## 2.10 The post-crisis toolkit: interest on reserves and repos (one central bank, dated)
- **Thesis**: when rates are near zero and reserves are abundant, a central bank needs tools that act on the demand for reserves rather than on their supply.
- **Mechanism**: (1) *Broader asset purchases* (QE): buying mortgage securities and longer-dated bonds, not only bills, targets long rates and specific markets such as housing finance; reserves in the system rose from about 2 billion to about 2.7 trillion after 2008 in the reported case. (2) *Interest paid on reserves*: raising it increases banks' demand for reserves and puts a floor under short rates, because banks will not lend below what the central bank pays them. It made policy tightening possible with a huge balance sheet. (3) *Repurchase and reverse repurchase agreements*: overnight swaps of reserves for bills (repo) or the opposite (reverse repo), which drain or add liquidity; they extend to non-bank intermediaries so that the tools reach beyond narrowly defined banks. With so many excess reserves the old market for lending reserves between banks became small, so open market operations lost bite.
- **Trade-off**: paying interest on reserves is a cost to the budget when rates rise; large reserves change what the balance sheet is for.
- **Counter-view**: why rates stayed so low for years is debated and unresolved. A swap of zero-interest cash for near-zero-interest bills has little macro effect by itself, which is why balance-sheet policy works through longer maturities and risk assets.

## Checklist
- Which of the three tools is being used and by what channel?
- Is the change a supply shock the bank cannot reach, or demand it can?
- Does the story confuse QE (reserves for bonds) with giving money to households?
- Ask which tool acts on the price of reserves (interest on reserves) and which on their quantity (QE).
