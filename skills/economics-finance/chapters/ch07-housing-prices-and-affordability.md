# Chapter 07: Housing Prices and Affordability

## Core Idea
A house is at once a **consumption good** (people live in it and it wears out) and an **investment good** (an asset bought with debt, whose price can rise). In this model three explanations, with different evidence, account for expensive housing: **supply failing to keep up with people**, **speculation**, and **cheap, abundant mortgage credit**. Which one dominates depends on the country and period; mixing them up leads to wrong policy.

## 7.1 Why housing is not a normal market
- **Thesis**: for consumption, higher prices reduce or leave unchanged demand (everyone needs a roof); for speculation, higher prices can increase demand.
- **Mechanism**: as a consumption good the price follows population, households and the stock built. As an investment, rising prices attract speculators who expect momentum to continue, which raises prices further; builders respond with a construction boom, which may still not meet investor demand.
- **Trade-off**: the same asset serves two roles; policies for one affect the other.
- **Counter-view**: some economists see the investment motive as mostly rational expectation of scarce land, not speculation.

## 7.2 Theory one: construction has not kept pace
- **Thesis**: prices rise when homes added fall short of the needs of the population.
- **Mechanism**: in three large high-price economies, new construction relative to population fell over the long run, while population grew through immigration and households became smaller and migration to cities left rural stock less useful. Why build less? The key explanation: **local opposition** ("not in my backyard") blocks or delays new homes. Each objector acts rationally, but the collective result is shortage: a collective action problem.
- **Trade-off**: more building changes neighbourhoods; some existing owners lose view, quiet or asset value.
- **Counter-view**: construction volumes alone do not prove shortage, because depreciation, household size and location matter; data on "homes needed" are impossible to pin down. Others argue immigration should be limited rather than building increased; many economists reject that as a first resort.

## 7.3 The evidence from upzoning
- **Thesis**: allowing denser building where people want to live lowers rents relative to the rest of the country.
- **Mechanism**: a large New Zealand city rezoned a big share of its land (the exact share is unclear) in 2016 to allow apartments in place of low-density single homes; construction rose; its rent-to-income ratio fell while the national ratio increased; rents there stabilised in real terms while a comparator city kept rising.
- **Trade-off**: higher density needs infrastructure and acceptance.
- **Counter-view**: one case; simultaneous changes (interest rates, migration) are hard to separate. The result is still cited as a rare quasi-experiment.

## 7.4 Governance of the collective action problem
- **Thesis**: if local veto blocks building, move the power up or remove vetoes.
- **Mechanism**: shift planning powers from municipal officials to provincial or national government; remove laws that let a few objectors frustrate projects; allow higher densities by right.
- **Trade-off**: reduces local democratic control.
- **Counter-view**: communities argue that they should decide their own surroundings; the distribution question is who bears external costs.

## 7.5 Theory two: speculative boom
- **Thesis**: a speculative boom shows up as vacant or underused property.
- **Mechanism**: during the early 2000s in the US, Ireland and Spain, prices rose and vacancies rose alongside a construction boom; in China an estimated 7.2 million homes were empty (dated estimate). Today vacancy in most booming markets is low, which argues against a bubble now, with exceptions (China; reports from Canadian cities).
- **Trade-off**: anti-speculation taxes (for example on underused homes) can discourage hoarding; they do not add supply.
- **Counter-view**: vacancy also rises where population declines, so low or high vacancy has to be read with price and population trends.

## 7.6 Theory three: cheap credit and mortgage markets
- **Thesis (one researcher's position)**: liberalised mortgage lending after the 1980s, plus decades of falling interest rates, pushed house prices up.
- **Mechanism**: most mortgage loans finance existing homes, not new ones; with a fixed housing stock and population, more borrowing capacity means higher prices and more household debt. Mortgage credit to GDP and real house prices rose after the reforms in English-speaking economies, which started the reforms first; countries without the same boom (Korea, Germany for a long time) saw falling real prices.
- **Trade-off**: easier credit helps first-time buyers briefly and harms them later.
- **Counter-view**: in some countries household debt stopped rising after the 2007 crisis while prices rebounded, which points to supply constraints there rather than credit. Home ownership rates did not clearly rise after liberalisation.

## 7.7 Is housing less affordable? Prices versus payments
- **Thesis**: affordability depends on price **and** financing cost.
- **Mechanism**: an index combining prices and mortgage rates across about forty economies showed housing more affordable since the 1980s for buyers who can get a mortgage, because rates fell. Illustration: a 100,000-unit mortgage over thirty years costs about 660 a month at 7%, and about 1,500 at 19%, the rate level of the 1980s (a dated illustration). So a lower price in an era of high rates can cost as much per month as a higher price in an era of low rates.
- **Trade-off**: low rates raise prices; monthly cost is lower, but the debt and deposit required are higher.
- **Counter-view** (three limits): not everyone can obtain a mortgage, especially vulnerable people; averages mix countries with falling prices (Japan, Korea) with those with sharp rises (Canada, Australia); and recent rate rises quickly reversed affordability.

## 7.8 What measures follow from each theory
- **Thesis (position, 2026 reading)**: the strongest evidence now favours supply constraints in most economies; credit is still a big problem in a few (Australia, Canada); bubbles are rare outside China and some Canadian cities.
- **Mechanism**: *Supply*: upzoning in low-density cities (US, Australia, New Zealand, Canada); in dense European countries (UK, Netherlands) expansion of cities onto land reserved for nature or parks may be needed. *Speculation*: anti-speculative taxes. *Credit*: reconsider lenient mortgage standards that have permanently raised debt and prices; make housing more of a consumption good again.
- **Trade-off**: each lever has losers: existing owners, borrowers, developers, environmental land.
- **Counter-view**: others favour demand-side subsidies, immigration limits or rent regulation (chapter 08).

## 7.9 Property, wealth and the wider economy
- **Thesis**: housing links to inflation measurement (chapter 03), to central-bank policy (chapter 02) and to crises (chapter 06).
- **Mechanism**: low rates and QE pushed up housing as an asset; high prices reduce the appeal of consumption of other goods; a bust spreads through investment, consumption and sentiment.
- **Trade-off**: policies to cool the market can trigger the bust.
- **Counter-view**: central banks say asset prices are not their target.

## 7.10 Mortgage mechanics: payment, schedule and rate conversion
> Educational concepts, not investment, tax or legal advice.

- **Thesis**: a constant-payment loan is a recursion in which the balance grows by interest and falls by the payment; the payment is the one that brings the balance to zero after the agreed term.
- **Mechanism**: balance(t+1) = balance(t) x (1 + monthly rate) - payment, so payment = P x r / (1 - (1+r)^-n). Rate, term, payment and principal are linked: given three, the fourth follows. A payment below the interest makes the debt grow. Mortgages quoted with a nominal annual rate apply nominal/12 per month (common convention, as in Italy); (1+i)^(1/12) - 1 is the equivalent monthly rate only when the quoted rate is an effective annual rate (on 200,000 over 20 years at 4%, the two readings differ by about 7-8 units a month). Two schedules exist: the **constant-payment ("French") schedule**, with equal instalments, interest-heavy at the start and principal-heavy later; and the **constant-principal ("Italian") schedule**, with a falling instalment, a heavier first payment and less total interest for the same rate and term. The split between interest and principal matters for the lender's books and for any tax deduction, not for the borrower's cash flow, so "prepay early because early instalments are only interest" is a myth. A quote can be checked: the internal rate of return of the loan's cash flows, fees included, must equal the stated rate on the same nominal or effective basis; upfront costs move the effective rate (4,000 borrowed with 100 of upfront costs can lift an apparent 4% toward 5.5%; illustrative).
- **Trade-off**: a longer term lowers the first payment but raises total interest; a constant-principal schedule saves interest but needs higher early income.
- **Does not hold when**: a variable rate resets. The lender usually keeps the term and recomputes the payment on the residual balance.
- **Worked example**: 100,000 at 5% with a payment of 500: monthly rate about 0.4074%, first-month interest about 407, principal about 93.

## 7.11 Fixed versus variable rate, and switching lender
- **Thesis**: a fixed-rate loan that can be moved to another lender without penalty is a one-sided option: the borrower follows the market down and is protected on the way up.
- **Mechanism**: if rates rise, the fixed payment is unchanged while a variable payment rises. If rates fall, the borrower switches to a lower fixed rate (in Italy a lender change with the same loan is called "surroga"; snapshot 2024, a statutory right with no penalty on the main residence mortgage, to be re-checked), while a variable falls automatically. All else equal, fixed with a switching right dominates. Lenders offset this by pricing the variable slightly below the fixed in some regimes. In a stylised model, when market rates are low the variable sits at the base rate and the fixed about one point above; when high, the reverse. Simulations on a long history show variable payments reaching 40-50% above the starting one, while fixed-with-switching payments rarely exceed the starting payment. Switching has costs: the new lender adds about 20-30 basis points in some cases, and small residual balances (below about 50,000-60,000) may be refused. Sustainability means checking the **maximum possible payment**, not the average cost. Short-rate futures are poor predictors beyond one year (hit rate about half).
- **Trade-off**: a slightly higher initial fixed rate versus protection and optionality.
- **Does not hold when**: the switching right is removed, the fixed spread is very large (about 2 points), or rates sit at zero under easing programmes.
- **Worked example (snapshot 2024)**: moving from a variable at 4.5% to a fixed at 2.8% saves money even after 0.2-0.3 points of extra spread.

## 7.12 Buying versus renting as an opportunity-cost comparison
- **Thesis**: once all owner costs are counted, buying and renting the home you live in are close to a draw; the drivers are appreciation, the return on the alternative use of capital and the mortgage rate.
- **Mechanism**: the renter pays rent (here about 6% of value, range 4-8%) and keeps the after-tax return on the capital not spent on the purchase. The owner pays no rent but bears building costs, periodic renovation (about a third of value every 40 years), transaction taxes and notary fees at each move (amortised) and the opportunity cost of the capital. With a mortgage rate below the investable return, the owner keeps the investment return while paying less interest, so buying wins on the numbers; with no mortgage and a 5% net return, renting wins narrowly. Appreciation compounds: 1% a year is +10.5% after 10 years and about x1.49 after 40, not +10% and +40%. Compare yearly cash flows: on a 200,000 home with rent 12,000 and a 10,000 investment return, the renter pays about 2,000 more per year while the owner's extra costs are about 2,800 (illustrative).
- **Trade-off**: mobility and no capital tied up versus freedom to alter the home and security of tenure.
- **Does not hold when**: the renter cannot invest the capital productively (many earn well under 5%) or the rental market is closed to them; then buying with a mortgage can be quasi-forced. Ch08 covers why rental markets can be closed.

## 7.13 Gross to net yield on a rented property
- **Thesis**: a flat bought to let usually yields little net of tax, vacancies, management and renovation; headline returns come mostly from appreciation.
- **Mechanism**: purchase costs add 6-13% to the price (agent, notary, purchase taxes). Gross yield 4-6%. Subtract tax on rent (21% flat, or 10% on regulated rents; Italy, snapshot 2021-2026), service charges (up to 1-2 months a year plus extraordinary works), property tax, maintenance, vacancies (about 3 months per tenant turnover, tenants staying about 4.5 years), agent fees, administrative time, and a renovation of about a third of value after 40 years (or an equal price discount). Net yield ends near 1-3% before appreciation. A financial alternative needs no management and is divisible and liquid. The internal rate of return must include renovation and extraordinary costs as dated flows. **Neutral selling price**: the capital that, in the alternative portfolio, gives the same net income. With a 50/50 equity-bond mix expected at 8% and 4% (gross 6%) and tax of 26% on equity gains and 12.5% on bond income (blended about 20%), net is about 4.8%; net rent of 6,710 on a 160,000 flat (4.2%) gives break-even 6,710 / 0.048 = about 140,000. Taxed distributions raise it (about 156,000); an accumulating fund with gradual drawdown lowers it. A let flat is a nearly binary income (about 98% reliable) while financial income fluctuates.
- **Trade-off**: access to lender financing (leverage) versus effort, illiquidity and idiosyncratic risk (default, litigation).
- **Does not hold when**: appreciation is high, the owner has expertise or enjoys the work, or the price was a deep discount. Owners tend to overestimate what their property would fetch (position).
- **Worked example**: bought for about 23,000 and sold for 100,000 after 23 years is about 6.6% a year on capital; net rent adds about 3.7% on the sale value, but the capital gain came from two market bubbles.

## Checklist
- Which of the three theories fits this city or country? What data separate them (vacancy, construction per capita, credit to GDP, rates)?
- Affordability: compare price to income **and** monthly cost to income.
- Who loses from the proposed fix, and is there a way to compensate them?
- Mortgage: what is the maximum possible payment, the effective rate with fees, and can the loan move lender without penalty (7.10-7.11)?
- Rent or buy: compare yearly cash flows including renovation and the return on the capital not spent (7.12); rental property: go from gross to net before comparing (7.13).
