# Chapter 09: How to Reason in Economics

## Core Idea
Economics gives a way of checking claims: follow the incentives, name the trade-off, look for the people outside the story, then ask what the evidence says and how strong it is. In this model the most useful habit is to **separate settled mechanism, contested channel and political choice**, and to say which one a claim belongs to. Good analysis also states what the field cannot predict.

## 9.1 Theory proposes, evidence disposes
- **Thesis**: an elegant argument is a hypothesis; the weight comes from tests.
- **Mechanism**: the competitive-market argument against rent control predicts supply falls; a review of dozens of studies confirmed the direction. The quantity theory predicts money growth raises prices; episodes with large money growth and low inflation weakened it as a general rule. A theory that fits one episode but not the others (every school of inflation) is partial.
- **Trade-off**: evidence is slow, noisy and often from one place and time.
- **Counter-view**: absence of evidence in a new situation is not evidence of absence; use theory to say what to look for.

## 9.2 Incentives and the unintended consequence
- **Thesis**: a rule changes behaviour of the people it covers and also of those who can avoid it.
- **Mechanism**: capped rents lead owners to sell, skimp on repairs or build less; tax on vacant land led owners to sell cheaply; financial regulation led institutions to invent new forms of credit money; deposit insurance changes bank risk-taking.
- **Trade-off**: policies that ignore reactions fail in the short run; policies that fear reactions never act.
- **Does not hold when**: the target cannot adjust (a monopoly utility's network), which is why some price controls are fine.

## 9.3 Who is outside the picture? (insiders and outsiders)
- **Thesis**: benefits are visible, costs are diffuse.
- **Mechanism**: rent control benefits the lucky sitting tenants and burdens future tenants; an existing homeowner blocking a development gains a little; those who would have lived in the new homes lose a lot but have no voice; austerity helps creditors now and hurts workers.
- **Trade-off**: identifying losers often does not change the political outcome.
- **Counter-view**: compensation schemes (to owners, to tenants) can build coalitions.

## 9.4 Collective action problems
- **Thesis**: individually rational acts can produce a collectively bad result.
- **Mechanism**: local objection to building; bank runs; every firm raising prices in advance because everyone expects inflation. Solutions move the decision up a level, add a coordinating rule, or change the payoff.
- **Trade-off**: higher-level authority reduces local voice.
- **Counter-view**: voluntary coordination sometimes works at small scale.

## 9.5 Accounting identities are not causal stories
- **Thesis**: an identity constrains, it does not explain.
- **Mechanism**: money times velocity equals prices times output holds by definition; it says nothing about which side moves. A balance of payments must balance; the question is how the adjustment happens. Debt-to-GDP changes with the numerator and denominator; the ratio alone does not say which.
- **Trade-off**: identities are good for checking the arithmetic of a claim.
- **Does not hold when**: someone assumes stable velocity or output without evidence; then the identity becomes a hidden hypothesis.

## 9.6 Denominators, levels and paths
- **Thesis**: ask "relative to what" and "which direction".
- **Mechanism**: debt relative to GDP; house price relative to income and mortgage cost; inflation relative to a statistical basket; the level of a debt matters less than the path of interest costs; the ratio can worsen because the denominator stalled.
- **Trade-off**: ratios hide absolute size; absolute numbers hide scale.
- **Counter-view**: choose several measures and report the spread.

## 9.7 Causation runs both ways
- **Thesis**: many macro variables feed back.
- **Mechanism**: exchange rates and inflation; interest rates and growth; house prices and credit; expectations and realised inflation (low inflation produces low expectations, and low expectations produce low inflation). Identification needs timing, natural experiments or comparison cases.
- **Trade-off**: honest uncertainty about order of events.
- **Does not hold when**: a clear shock (a policy reform, a rezoning) allows before-after comparison against a control.

## 9.8 Where economists agree, and where they do not
| Question | Wide agreement | Contested |
|---|---|---|
| Do higher policy rates reduce demand? | Yes, through credit and asset prices | Which channel dominates; how large; effect of expectations |
| Is QE inflationary for consumer prices? | Raises asset prices (event studies) | Size of effect on consumer prices |
| Can a government cut debt by cutting spending in a slump? | Not always | Size of the multiplier; when austerity works |
| Does debt-to-GDP have a danger threshold? | No universal number | Where risk becomes serious; role of the interest-growth gap |
| Does rent control lower rents for covered tenants? | Yes | Net welfare effect; role of design |
| Should cities build more? | Most agree supply matters | Mix of private, social and subsidised; how much weight to credit |
| Should a country give up its currency? | Lower inflation; weaker buffer | Net growth effect; irreversibility; worth for specific countries |
| What causes inflation? | Supply and demand channels exist | Dominance of money, expectations, profits, fiscal policy |
| Do banks create money? | Yes, by lending | Whether the multiplier is useful; regulation design |

## 9.9 Prediction limits and honest forecasting
- **Thesis**: economics can often say what direction a policy pushes things and rarely when.
- **Mechanism**: central-bank forecasts missed inflation in both directions; debt crises and bubbles can be foreseen as unsustainable but not dated; exchange-rate forecasts are fruitless because expectations are already priced. The doctor analogy: smoking raises the probability of cancer, but nobody can say who or when.
- **Trade-off**: a forecast with ranges is less quotable than a headline.
- **Counter-view**: scenario analysis (what would have to be true) is a more honest tool than point forecasts. This skill does not predict markets or advise on investments.

## 9.10 Reading a claim: a short protocol
1. State the claim in one sentence with its unit and date.
2. Mechanism: draw the causal chain.
3. Trade-off: who gains and who pays, now and later.
4. Evidence: is it a theory, a correlation, a natural experiment or a review of many studies? How old is the number?
5. The rival story: which school or camp says otherwise, and what would settle it?
6. Label: fact, mechanism, position or value choice.

## 9.11 Facts versus values
- **Thesis**: economics can show costs of a policy; the choice among costs is political.
- **Mechanism**: a tight labour market raising labour's share of income is good for workers and bad for profits; whether that counts as "overheating" is partly a distributional judgement. A rent freeze helps the current tenant at the cost of future ones; choosing whose welfare counts is not a technical matter.
- **Trade-off**: pretending the choice is technical hides it.
- **Counter-view**: some economists say they should recommend, others that they should lay out options and costs.

## 9.12 Opportunity cost, trade-offs and who bears the cost
- **Thesis**: every choice excludes another, and a rule's real cost includes the foregone alternative.
- **Mechanism**: ask for each proposal what is given up (money, time, activity that is priced out), compare with the next best alternative, and then ask who bears the cost: price is a signal; who pays is a separate question. Costs that fall on third parties are externalities (chapter 10.8); costs that are hidden in quality, queues or rents are still costs.
- **Trade-off**: more careful analysis delays decisions.
- **Counter-view**: not every decision should be priced; the point is to name what is traded off, not to monetise everything.

## 9.13 Mechanism, case and shock: reading a country story
- **Thesis**: a country narrative mixes a shock, a structure and a mechanism; separate them.
- **Mechanism**: use the case-sheet format of chapter 13: dated facts, proposed causes, evidence for each, contested points, lessons. Check whether a comparator (a resource-rich country that did well) differs in one variable or many.
- **Counter-view**: a single comparison cannot isolate causes.

## Checklist
- Did I tag every contested claim as a position and give the rival view?
- Did I separate nominal from real, level from path, stock from flow, average from distribution?
- Did I avoid giving investment advice or forecasting a market move?
- Ask who bears the cost and what the next best alternative was.
- Put a country story into the case-sheet format before drawing lessons.
