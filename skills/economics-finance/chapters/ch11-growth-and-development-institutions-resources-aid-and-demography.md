# Chapter 11: Growth and Development: Institutions, Resources, Aid and Demography

## Core Idea
Why some countries are rich and others poor is the largest question in economics and the least settled. In this model, growth comes from three inputs (people, capital, ideas), but what lets those inputs be used well is the set of rules and norms around them: property rights, courts, honest government, open markets, trust. Resources, aid and demography matter, but mostly through institutions. Many claims here are positions; evidence is mixed, and country stories (chapter 13) are best read as proposed causes, not proofs.

## 11.1 A simple growth model and catching up
- **Thesis**: output rises with human capital (labour times education), physical capital and ideas; a country far from the frontier can grow faster than one on it.
- **Mechanism**: in the standard growth model the capital stock settles where investment equals depreciation, which fixes a steady-state income per head set by saving, depreciation, population growth and the productivity of the economy's organisation. Catching-up growth (adopting known technology and machines) can run at 7 to 10 percent a year in the cases quoted (a doubling every 7 to 10 years), whereas frontier growth runs near 2 percent (a doubling about every 35 years). That explains why war-damaged economies rebuilt faster than the winners and why a poorer fast-growing country can outpace richer ones with better institutions.
- **Trade-off**: catching up is quick because copying is cheap; it slows as the gap closes.
- **Counter-view (position)**: some economies never start catching up; the model says nothing about why. That is the job of institutions (11.2).
- *Rates are the figures quoted for the era described; check current data.*

## 11.2 Institutions
- **Thesis**: laws and regulations (property rights, reliable courts, political stability) and cultural norms (honesty, trust, cooperation) set the incentives that decide whether capital and talent are used well.
- **Mechanism**: a divided peninsula with the same people, language and history ended on two institutional tracks: one with secure profit, bankruptcy for failures and market prices; one with central planning, controlled prices and capital allocated by a party. The first developed a modern economy; the second faced recurrent famine because prices and property rights did not reward growing food. The contrast is a natural experiment, extreme but clear. A second case: a small South American country with shallow colonial extraction built welfare, education and a stable democracy early, and later survived a regional bank run (chapter 13).
- **Trade-off**: institutions take decades to build and can be captured.
- **Counter-view**: causality can run both ways (rich countries can afford better institutions); one natural experiment does not settle the weight of institutions versus geography, culture or resources.

## 11.3 Colonial extraction and inherited rules (position)
- **Thesis**: where colonial rule was built to extract, the rules about land, courts and voting outlast the empire.
- **Mechanism**: (position) regions with the highest historical land concentration still show lower income and weaker state capacity; a leftover shell of institutions is easy for a strongman to seize and use for extraction rather than for new economic activity. A sudden handover (almost no trained nationals in the civil service at independence, in the reported case) can start a spiral of chaos, conflict and weak investment.
- **Trade-off**: explanations by history are hard to test and can sound fatalistic.
- **Counter-view**: countries with similar histories diverged (see the South American case in chapter 13); this account needs a mechanism for the exceptions.

## 11.4 The resource curse
- **Thesis**: natural-resource wealth does not produce prosperity by itself and often hurts.
- **Mechanism**: (1) *Dutch disease*: a booming export raises the currency and costs, so other tradable sectors shrink; the economy depends on one price. (2) *Rentier state*: government income from rent rather than taxes weakens accountability, because rulers do not need citizens' consent for revenue and jobs become patronage. (3) *Cycle*: when prices rise governments spend and subsidise; when prices fall the spending cannot be cut, so deficits and debt grow. (4) *Weak property rights* make foreign investors leave, so even extraction can fail for lack of capital and skills. Counter-example: a country that sets a rule to spend only the expected return of a sovereign fund (a 3 percent rule in the reported case) turns finite resources into long-run assets.
- **Trade-off**: saving oil income means less visible spending today.
- **Counter-view (position)**: commodity dependence is not destiny: advanced resource exporters coexist with weak ones; the difference tracks institutions rather than geology. External shocks (sanctions, price swings) add to but rarely explain the whole collapse.

## 11.5 Commodity dependence in practice
- **Thesis**: when raw materials exceed roughly 60 percent of exports, national budgets track world prices.
- **Mechanism**: in the region quoted, every country passes that threshold; a commodity boom (in one decade, annual growth near 5 percent and poverty cut by more than half) reversed when prices fell after 2014 and growth went to almost zero. The instability appears linked to inequality that predates the debt, not only to inflation. One country with an even higher agricultural share avoided the cycle through steadier institutions (chapter 13).
- **Trade-off**: managing the cycle (counter-cyclical rules, funds) is possible; breaking it needs diversification.
- **Does not hold when**: the exporter holds a strong fund or rule that caps spending.

## 11.6 Foreign aid and growth: big push versus steady state
- **Thesis**: aid rarely makes a poor country take off on its own.
- **Mechanism**: the "big push" idea says enough investment gets a country past a tipping point and into self-sustaining growth. The steady-state model says extra capital without better fundamentals depreciates back: roads and factories decay without savings and organisation to maintain them. The evidence is mixed: early studies found no link between aid and investment; one famous result (aid works with good policy) proved not robust to small changes in sample and method; later pooled work found modest effects, limited and non-linear (a little helps; more adds less), and far more of the variation in growth explained by other factors. Obstacles to measurement: long lags (a bridge takes years), and reverse causality (aid goes where growth is slowest).
- **Trade-off**: tying aid to better policy can raise its effect but depends on enforceable conditions.
- **Counter-view (position)**: low-income poverty traps (low savings, so low investment) are real, so a start-up transfer can help in principle, like jump-starting a battery; the question is whether the donor-intermediary-government chain delivers it.

## 11.7 Aid and poverty: better evidence than for growth
- **Thesis**: aid does better at reducing particular harms than at raising national growth.
- **Mechanism**: reported cases: a public-works safety net that cut food insecurity and built infrastructure; health investment linked to a large fall in child mortality (a 68 percent decline over 2002 to 2012 in the case quoted); agriculture and infrastructure reforms linked with falling poverty from about 50 percent to under 5 percent in another. Yet extreme poverty counts in the poorest region have risen in absolute terms despite well over a trillion dollars of aid since the 1960s. Aid money moves through a chain of intermediaries (donor, development organisations, governments, local groups) and, where governments do not want a wealthier, harder-to-control population, can be embezzled or written off.
- **Trade-off**: targeted programmes work better than general budget support.
- **Counter-view**: claims that aid itself creates dependence or distorts politics have supporters; positions differ and there is no verdict.

## 11.8 Demography and the fertility transition
- **Thesis**: population growth is slowing worldwide; the economic issue is age structure and the fertility rate, not total numbers.
- **Mechanism**: the growth rate peaked near 2 percent in the 1960s and is now below 1 percent; the share aged 65 or more is projected to rise from about 10 to 16 percent over 25 years. Replacement needs roughly 2.1 to 2.3 births per woman. The fall in births follows industrialisation: urbanisation makes children costly (space, supervision, no farm work), child labour becomes a liability, contraception and women's work grow, and children shift from low-cost workforce and pension to expensive obligation. In low-income countries with no welfare, children are both workers and a retirement plan; in high-income countries the cost-benefit reverses.
- **Trade-off**: more elderly means more dependants per worker; the cost shows as higher taxes or more labour in care.
- **Counter-view (position)**: cash payments for births are criticised as an expensive mistake; their effect on births is modest and its effect on births is modest (general knowledge). Others argue that ageing is a success of nutrition and medicine and that productive elderly can offset.
- *Figures are projections of the era; re-check.*

## 11.9 Brain drain
- **Thesis**: skilled people move from poorer to richer countries, which hurts the origin and helps the destination.
- **Mechanism**: a trained engineer can earn a multiple of the home wage abroad. The origin loses its highest taxpayers and innovators right after paying their schooling. Advanced economies gain: skill shortages are filled by visas, and if the job market turns, unemployed migrants return home, so unemployment is partly exported.
- **Trade-off**: emigrant remittances and returnee skills partly offset (general knowledge).
- **Counter-view**: a ban on leaving is not a policy; the alternative is making staying attractive, which requires the institutions of 11.2.

## 11.10 Measuring poor economies
- **Thesis**: low recorded GDP per head overstates misery in the sense of survival but understates what is missing.
- **Mechanism**: GDP counts market output of final goods; it excludes non-final inputs (to avoid double counting), illegal goods and household production. People in very poor economies grow food, collect water and build shelter themselves, so a recorded drop matters less to them than in an economy where almost all income comes through the market. Adjusting for purchasing power roughly doubles a very low per-capita figure in the case quoted, still a hard life.
- **Trade-off**: purchasing-power adjustment improves comparison but rests on price surveys.
- **Does not hold when**: comparing countries where most work is already for the market.

## 11.11 Why advanced economies rarely collapse
- **Thesis**: advanced economies have buffers that developing ones lack.
- **Mechanism**: diverse export industries give reliable foreign income; high living standards attract skilled migrants on demand; borrowing is cheap, from rich domestic savers and in the economy's own currency (no foreign-exchange mismatch, chapter 05); infrastructure and education can be funded. Developing economies are exposed to sudden stops when global rates rise and lenders retreat to safe government bonds; they must pay higher yields and cannot fund the public goods growth needs.
- **Trade-off**: none; it is a condition.
- **Counter-view**: advanced economies can still suffer deep crises (chapter 06).
