# Chapter 08: Rents, Rent Control and Social Housing

## Core Idea
Rent control is popular with tenants and unpopular with most economists. In this model the disagreement is not ideology; it comes from asking what a price cap does to **supply, mobility, quality and the people outside the protected group**. The alternative most economists support is to **build more of every kind of housing**, with public housing as a backstop. A mixed model (a large controlled and social sector that also builds a lot) shows that the control/no-control axis is the wrong one: the **building** axis decides.

## 8.1 When price controls are the right tool
- **Thesis**: economists accept price caps where a seller is a monopolist.
- **Mechanism**: with a single wire or pipe, customers have nowhere to go, so the seller can raise prices; a cap leaves the firm to earn more through lower costs or serving more people. The rental market is not like this.
- **Trade-off**: a cap on a monopoly rarely reduces supply, because the network already exists.
- **Counter-view**: in tight markets with strong tenant lock-in (moving costs, scarcity), landlords may have local market power; the competitive model is a theory whose strength depends on the vacancy rate.

## 8.2 Are landlords monopolists? (dated snapshots)
- **Thesis**: ownership is dispersed, so the market is competitive.
- **Mechanism**: in Spain about two thirds of rental units belong to owners with one or two homes, owners with ten or more units about 3.6% and investment funds about 8%; in Germany only about 13% of rental apartments belong to private companies with large portfolios; in the US, entities with more than a thousand single-family units own under 2% of that market, three quarters of units belong to owners with fewer than ten. If rents get too high, owners have an incentive to build or add units, which restrains profits.
- **Trade-off**: dispersion does not prevent profit in a shortage.
- **Counter-view**: the pattern in a given city can be different (concentrated in a few neighbourhoods); concentration data is not market-power data.

## 8.3 What the evidence says about rent control
- **Thesis**: rent control works for sitting tenants and hurts the rest.
- **Mechanism** (from a review of 92 peer-reviewed studies in 36 countries, 1967-2022, as reported; counts are of published studies):
  1. *Rents*: 36 of 41 published studies find controlled rents fall.
  2. *Sales*: some owners sell; most studies find home ownership rises.
  3. *Supply*: nine of ten studies find the rental stock shrinks as units are sold.
  4. *Mobility*: almost all find lower mobility; sitting tenants fear losing the controlled unit even when work or family would justify moving, so fewer units become available to newcomers.
  5. *Spillovers*: nearby uncontrolled markets get higher rents.
  6. *Quality*: about 11 of 13 studies find quality falls; landlords with capped income cut maintenance.
  7. *New construction*: most studies find less building of rental homes because investment is less attractive. Exemption for new buildings softens but does not remove this.
- **Trade-off**: lower rent for insiders against scarcity and weaker maintenance for all, with outsiders (newcomers, young people, those moving cities) bearing the cost.
- **Counter-view**: supporters stress security of tenure, protection from sudden increases and the social cost of displacement, which standard measures of rent levels do not capture. Some designs (second-generation controls with vacancy decontrol, indexed increases, exemptions for new buildings) are said to reduce the harms; most economists in the survey remain sceptical. A 2012 survey of prominent US economists found only about 2% agreed that rent control improves the amount and quality of affordable housing (a dated figure); even left-leaning economists doubted it.

## 8.4 The insider-outsider problem
- **Thesis**: any quota on cheap housing produces two classes of tenants.
- **Mechanism**: those inside controlled or social units pay far less (a dated gap in one city: about 12-13 currency units per square metre in the uncontrolled private market versus almost half of that in municipal housing), regardless of income; high earners can sit in cheap units while a working family pays the market price; informal side payments for introductions appear.
- **Trade-off**: fairness among renters versus predictability for those who hold the unit.
- **Counter-view**: means-tested allocation reduces the problem but weakens political support (see 8.7).

## 8.5 The alternative: build more, including luxury
- **Thesis**: shortage is what gives landlords their power; adding supply reduces rent without a cap.
- **Mechanism**: with options, tenants choose, and landlords compete; building at the top of the market starts a "filtering" chain, like musical chairs: the better-off move into new units, freeing older ones, and the chain reaches down the income ladder further than it seems. A city that rezoned and built (the same case as chapter 07) saw rents stabilise in real terms while a comparator city's rents kept rising.
- **Trade-off**: results are slower than a freeze; displacement during redevelopment is real.
- **Counter-view**: filtering can be weak where new units are scarce or demand is very high; critics want direct subsidies and tenant protection alongside building.

## 8.6 Why voters prefer controls
- **Thesis**: public intuitions about supply differ across goods.
- **Mechanism**: in a survey experiment (as reported), roughly 42-43% of respondents expected more construction to raise prices or rents, while 86% understood that fewer new cars raise used-car prices; most blamed developers and landlords for high prices. So voters see construction as the cause, not the cure; rent control is simple, visible and cheap in the budget, while building is slow and local.
- **Trade-off**: policy that is popular may be counterproductive.
- **Counter-view**: voters' scepticism may be informed by past developments that raised prices locally (luxury gentrification) or by the political economy: they do not trust that supply will be affordable.

## 8.7 Public and social housing: Vienna as a reference case
- **Thesis**: public housing works when it builds at scale, targets the middle class and is paired with land and financing capacity.
- **Mechanism** (history and structure): after the First World War, hyperinflation plus strict rent controls made private rental unprofitable and private landlords refused to build; a new municipal government taxed vacant land (owners sold cheaply, often at 10-15% of pre-war values), introduced a progressive housing construction tax and a luxury tax, and built about 60,000 apartments between 1923 and 1934 for roughly 11% of the population. After the Second World War the city and non-profit developers built again while strict rent control pushed private owners to sell or convert. Today (dated): about 31% of renters live in city homes, 26% non-profit, 33% in controlled private units and only 10% in free-market rentals. Output is high (about 16,000 new units in the city in one year and about 71,000 in the whole country, while a very large US city with more inhabitants than the entire country added units at less than half that rate). Rents in the free segment are lower than in other big capitals.
- **Trade-off**: heavy intervention gives affordability and a mixed-income city; waiting lists, in/out inequality, high public spending.
- **Counter-view and limits** (position): three obstacles block copying: (a) Vienna built while its population was shrinking, so demand pressure was low; (b) buying cheap land in an economic collapse is not repeatable and a big program is very expensive; (c) politics: Vienna's support comes from serving the middle class, which is criticised as unfair, while programs for the poor concentrated poverty in other countries and lost public support. One conclusion: do both, encourage private construction and build good public housing, because **building more homes** is what makes the model work.
- **Does not hold when**: the city lacks the institutions, land or political base to build at scale.

## 8.8 Combining rent control and mass building
- **Thesis**: possible in theory, hard in practice.
- **Mechanism**: control lowers the return to private construction; a city promising a big public program together with controls must supply the homes itself; a plan that only builds a narrow type (permanently affordable, publicly subsidised, union-built) is difficult to scale.
- **Trade-off**: speed against restrictions on the building programme.
- **Counter-view**: some jurisdictions exempt new construction from controls for this reason.

## Checklist
- Which tenants are inside the controlled group, and who is outside?
- Does the plan add supply, or only cap the price of existing supply?
- Is the vacancy rate high enough for landlords to compete?
