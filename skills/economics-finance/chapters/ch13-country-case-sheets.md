# Chapter 13: Country Case Sheets

## Core Idea
A case sheet is a structured reading of one country's episode. It lists the facts as reported with their date, the causes that have been proposed, the evidence offered for each, and what is contested. A case is an illustration of a mechanism from another chapter, not a proof: causes interact, the counterfactual is unobservable, and popular narratives are simpler than the data. Figures are snapshots from the period of the underlying discussion (around 2023 to 2025) and some are estimates; re-check before use. Nothing here is a forecast.

## How to read a case sheet
1. Separate **facts** (a rate, a share) from **causes** (a story about why).
2. Ask which chapter's mechanism is being invoked and which rival mechanism would predict the same facts.
3. Note which part of the case is a *shock* (price, war, sanction) and which is a *structure* (institutions, geography).
4. Treat cross-country comparisons (a resource-rich country that stayed rich) as hints about differences, not as controlled tests.

## 13.1 Oil-rich collapse (South American case)
- **Facts (reported, around 2024)**: the largest proven oil reserves in the world; peak GDP about 372 billion in 2012; later estimates put the loss of output at over 80 percent in under a decade; oil revenue reported down 93 percent from 2012 to 2020; inflation reported near 130,000 percent in 2018 and 190 percent in 2023; over 6 million people emigrated. International agencies stopped publishing some series, so figures are estimates.
- **Proposed causes**:
  1. Dependence on oil exports (about 90 percent of exports) and Dutch disease (ch11.4).
  2. Spending of oil income on subsidies and a patronage payroll, without a savings rule; inflation financed by money creation (ch02, ch03).
  3. Weak property rights and expropriation: foreign investors withdrew; the state company lost skilled staff after a purge and a lack of capital.
  4. Corruption: unaccounted transfers.
  5. Sanctions and political isolation (ch12.7).
- **Evidence**: comparison with a country that put oil income in a sovereign fund with a spending cap equal to expected returns; the decline followed the fall in oil price; emigration and brain drain (ch11.9).
- **Contested**: how much of the fall is sanctions versus domestic policy; this sheet weights domestic policy more heavily; alternative views weight external pressure more. Which comes first, falling production or falling investment, is unverified.
- **Lessons**: price controls, subsidies and monetisation produce a cycle; fund-and-cap rules are the counter-example.

## 13.2 Oil state inside a federation (northern US state)
- **Facts (reported)**: oil production began in earnest in the late 1960s; by 1968 oil was over 20 percent of state revenue. A permanent fund created from oil revenue exceeds 60 billion and pays an annual dividend to residents (1,114 per person in 2021). Cost of living about 30 percent above the national average; about 95 percent of food imported; GDP per head above 90,000 but inflated by non-resident workers; seasonal worker share high.
- **Proposed causes of fragility**: dependence on a few resource industries (oil, fishing, mining); remote location; dividend too small to offset costs; a flying workforce that sends earnings home.
- **Evidence**: the dividend range; the unemployment ranking; output per head versus a shrinking population.
- **Contested**: whether warming opens new opportunities (shipping routes, agriculture) or mostly adds infrastructure and fishing costs; federal transfers (over 1 trillion to all states and localities in 2022) are a buffer that no sovereign country has.
- **Lessons**: a resource fund helps; a federation's transfers are an insurance a small isolated country lacks.

## 13.3 A resource-poor country that avoided the regional cycle
- **Facts (reported)**: more than 80 percent of exports raw agricultural goods, yet low corruption and stable democracy by regional standards; early welfare state (eight-hour day, free schooling, pensions) in the early 20th century; a regional bank run and bank holiday in 2002; debt restructured, currency floated, banking rebuilt, growth by 2003, IMF debt cleared in 2006; poverty fell from about 18 percent to under 10 percent in one presidential term; a fiscal deficit rose and the successor raised taxes.
- **Proposed causes**: no gold or silver, so shallow colonial extraction; immigrants and ranchers rather than administrators; early investment in a healthy, educated population; credible institutions.
- **Evidence**: comparison with neighbours' coups, riots and political turnover.
- **Contested**: whether the story is institutions or smallness and luck; why other countries have not copied it; the model is not utopian: crime and cost of living rose; young talent still emigrates.
- **Lessons**: institutions, not resources (ch11.2); a banking haven is exposed to its neighbour's crisis (ch06).

## 13.4 Mineral-rich, poor, conflict-affected country
- **Facts (reported)**: at independence in 1960 only 16 university graduates among about 20 million people; a Cold War proxy conflict; a long dictatorship; GDP per head about 584, about 1,179 adjusted for purchasing power; many households live by household production.
- **Proposed causes**: sudden handover without administrators; dictatorship's need to avoid a wealthier, harder-to-control population; lack of secure investment, so mining stays manual and unproductive; loans from international lenders embezzled and largely written off.
- **Evidence**: loan write-offs; productivity gap between local and advanced mining.
- **Contested**: whether outside intervention could help given its record; whether low recorded GDP overstates deprivation (it omits household production, ch11.10).
- **Lessons**: the resource curse as the absence of institutions that translate rents into capital.

## 13.5 Africa's largest economy and its rebasing-era swings
- **Facts (reported)**: oil discovered in 1956; independence 1960; regional conflicts and a civil war; six successful coups between 1966 and 1993; oil about 87 percent of exports and 77 percent of federal revenue by the late 1980s; growth near 7 percent in the early 2000s, and oil only about 14 percent of GDP on the rebased series (the 2014 statistical rebasing added service and informal sectors that were previously uncounted, which lowered oil's measured share, so the figure is not comparable with the export and revenue shares); telecom licences in 2001 and rapid internet adoption.
- **Proposed causes of volatility**: colonial institutions left as a shell open to extraction; ethnic and regional distrust; election fraud and elite capture; dependence on one export; fraud industry exploiting a niche of banking and telecom infrastructure.
- **Contested**: the loss of about half of dollar-measured GDP is attributed to a currency devaluation (this mechanism was not checked in detail; unverified); the claim that a landlocked, ethnically diverse neighbour did better through institutions is used as contrast.
- **Lessons**: a currency fall can cut dollar GDP without a real contraction (ch05); hollow institutions beat good fundamentals.

## 13.6 Small island recovery from a banking collapse
- **Facts (reported)**: overleveraged banks failed in the global crisis; the central bank did not act as lender of last resort for foreign deposits; the state guaranteed domestic deposits and secured a loan of about 5 billion from the IMF and neighbours; key bankers were prosecuted; debt peaked near 130 percent of GDP in 2011; income per head back to pre-crisis level about 5 years after the peak; debt more than halved by 2023.
- **Proposed causes of recovery**: floating currency (devaluation helped exports); cheap renewable energy from hydro and geothermal powering energy-intensive industry such as aluminium smelting; fishing rights gained in the territorial disputes; small and flexible.
- **Contested**: the decision not to rescue the banks was controversial; a court ruling spared the state from foreign deposit guarantees; whether this would work in a larger economy or one without its own currency is doubtful (general knowledge: no devaluation channel).
- **Lessons**: let banks fail while protecting domestic depositors can work for a small country with a currency of its own; compare ch06.

## 13.7 Export champion that stagnated
- **Facts (reported)**: a 1985 agreement among five major economies to lower the dollar and raise the yen, then a rapid asset boom, then a bust in the 1990s and decades of low growth; an ageing population and low birth rate; share of the population speaking English in the range reported 15 to 28 percent; a middling ranking for ease of starting a business.
- **Proposed causes**: currency appreciation hurting exporters and fuelling an asset bubble; risk aversion after the bust, a preference for stable salaried work; regulatory hurdles to start businesses; language barrier; ageing.
- **Contested**: the claim that stagnation was handled well is a position: low unemployment and stable living standards per head versus lost innovation leadership; no single explanation is accepted.
- **Lessons**: a bubble followed by a prolonged balance-sheet clean-up (general knowledge; ch06); demography (ch11.8); interest rates near zero for a long time.

## 13.8 Hyperinflation economy that dollarised
- **Facts (reported)**: month-on-month inflation near 80 billion percent in late 2008; the local currency replaced by foreign currencies (dollarised in 2009; later reversals: own currency reintroduced in 2019 and again in 2024; dated); mineral smuggling worth about 2 billion a year; mine wages low despite large union-negotiated rises.
- **Proposed causes**: money creation to finance government (ch02, ch03); weak property rights; lack of trust in the local currency.
- **Contested**: whether a ban on raw exports will raise local value (12.8).
- **Lessons**: after hyperinflation, giving up the currency is common (ch05.5).

## 13.9 Global net worth and debt as a case
- **Facts (reported, around 2024 to 2025)**: world net worth about 600 trillion, about 75,000 per person; about 5.4 times GDP (peak 6.1 in 2020, up nearly 50 percent from the historical norm); total debt about 2.9 times GDP (about 322 trillion in 2024). Households hold about 95 percent of wealth; the top 1 percent of households hold over 20 percent. Real estate is over two thirds of real assets; intangible capital less than 5 percent.
- **Proposed cause of the gap with GDP**: about 75 percent of net worth growth since 2000 came from price increases (about 38 percent of that from general inflation); only about 28 percent from investment; long periods of near-zero interest rates; share buybacks outpace productive investment.
- **Contested**: whether rising asset values reflect real capital or paper wealth; "wealth" counts market prices, so it can fall with a sentiment swing; the framing that financial claims net to zero globally is accounting, not a statement that debt is harmless (ch04).
- **Lessons**: stock versus flow; GDP is like an income statement, net worth like a balance sheet.
