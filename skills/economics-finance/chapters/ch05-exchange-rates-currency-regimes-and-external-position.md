# Chapter 05: Exchange Rates, Currency Regimes and the External Position

## Core Idea
An exchange rate is the price of one currency in another, set by supply and demand in decentralised markets. In this model the practical questions are: what drives the demand and supply (price differences, interest differences, expectations), whether the country should let the price float, fix it or give up its currency, and what the choice does to inflation, growth and crisis risk. Prediction is very difficult; understanding the regime trade-offs is not.

## 5.1 Reading an exchange rate
- **Thesis**: quote it unambiguously.
- **Mechanism**: an exchange rate can be written either way (units of A per unit of B or the reverse). Say "currency X appreciated" when its price rises against others and "depreciated" when it falls, to avoid confusion.
- **Trade-off**: a stronger currency makes imports cheap and exports dear; a weaker currency does the reverse.
- **Does not hold when**: the "rate" is a managed peg; then the quote conceals the rule being defended.

## 5.2 What moves supply and demand
- **Thesis**: two building-block theories, each incomplete.
- **Mechanism**:
  1. *Purchasing power parity* (money as medium of exchange and unit of account): if goods are cheaper in country A, arbitrageurs buy them there and sell where prices are higher; the demand for A's currency raises its price until the gap closes. Evidence is weak for monthly moves, stronger in the long run and in hyperinflation, where the currency falls in line with prices. Limits: trade barriers, non-tradable goods and services.
  2. *Interest and return differentials* (money as store of value): traded money is mostly bank deposits, which pay interest; funds flow toward higher returns, including returns on shares, property and the currency itself.
- **Trade-off**: a theory that works for the long run may mislead in the short run.
- **Counter-view**: speculators try to anticipate arbitrageurs, so expected inflation and rate changes are already in the price; behavioural finance argues traders' biases create further patterns. Which variable dominates at which time is still unknown.

## 5.3 Exchange rates and inflation interact
- **Thesis**: the exchange rate influences inflation and interest rates, and is influenced by them.
- **Mechanism**: depreciation raises import prices; high domestic inflation weakens the currency; higher rates strengthen it. Large currency areas that trade in their own currency feel the channel little; smaller ones (high-inflation economies) feel it strongly.
- **Trade-off**: using the exchange rate to fight inflation (an anchor) can cost competitiveness.
- **Counter-view**: causality is hard to separate; studies of single episodes often disagree about the order of events.

## 5.4 The spectrum of regimes
- **Thesis**: currency regimes lie on a scale from floating to full surrender of the currency.
- **Mechanism**:
  - *Floating*: the market sets the price; the central bank sets its own rate.
  - *Currency peg*: the bank promises to keep the rate near a target; vulnerable if people believe more currency has been issued than the reserves back, and call the bluff.
  - *Currency board*: commits to hold the foreign currency for every unit issued; almost the same as dollarization except that a country that runs out of reserves can abandon it.
  - *Dollarization or joining a currency union*: abandon your own currency; almost impossible to reverse. "Dollarization" in the economic sense includes adopting a currency union's money.
- **Trade-off**: the more fixed the regime, the lower the inflation and borrowing costs, and the less policy freedom.
- **Counter-view**: floats are not the cure-all: exchange-rate swings can be harmful for small open economies with foreign-currency debts.

## 5.5 Evidence on giving up the currency
- **Thesis**: giving up the currency crushes inflation and cuts borrowing costs but tends to make output slower and more volatile.
- **Mechanism**: in the cases studied, inflation converged toward the anchor country's rate (southern European countries preparing for the euro; two Latin American economies after full dollarization; one large country after adopting a currency board in 1991, with inflation climbing again after the board was abandoned in 2002). Interest rates fell toward the anchor's. A literature summary reports that dollarized countries on average show slower and more volatile growth and lower inflation, and that fully dollarized ones grew more slowly than partially dollarized or floating ones, with wide variation between studies.
- **Trade-off**: credibility gained against lost buffers.
- **Counter-view**: proponents say removing the central bank's ability to print is the point, since it forces fiscal discipline; opponents say the euro-area crisis showed that governments still overspent and then faced harsh austerity in the crisis.

## 5.6 The four arguments against giving up the currency (and replies)
1. *Loss of the exchange-rate buffer* (when a large economy raises its rate, funds leave; a floating currency absorbs the shock, a dollarized one must raise rates too). Reply: the buffer is far from perfect.
2. *No lender of last resort for the government or banks* (forces austerity in a crisis). Reply: that is the intended discipline.
3. *Loss of seigniorage* (money issued at zero interest is free revenue; given up to the anchor country). Reply: seigniorage is small where the economy is already largely dollarized; the anchor could return part of it by agreement.
4. *Lack of the foreign currency to replace the stock of local money* (the central bank holds too few dollars). Reply: dollars hidden at home often reappear once inflation and capital controls end.
- **Position**: dollarization likely ends inflation; growth costs and deeper crises are the price; a currency board or peg is a softer alternative. **Counter**: Those backing dollarization argue that a board lacks the irreversibility that stops future governments reversing the policy. Whether to accept the trade depends on how much hope one has for domestic institutions.

## 5.7 Small open economies and foreign currency
- **Thesis**: an economy that cannot produce everything needs foreign currency.
- **Mechanism**: a bank sector that creates domestic money can finance domestic projects without foreign funds; imports and foreign-currency debts still need reserves. Hence reserve accumulation, deposit attraction (chapter 01) and, for some states, a lender of last resort in foreign currency.
- **Trade-off**: reserves cost money to hold.
- **Counter-view**: floating regimes reduce the need for reserves but increase currency risk.

## 5.8 Balance of payments in outline (general textbook account)
- **Thesis**: the payments record is an accounting identity: a country that spends more than it earns abroad must finance the gap.
- **Mechanism**: the current account (trade in goods and services, income, transfers) must be offset by the capital and financial account (borrowing, investment flows, reserve changes). A current-account deficit means net borrowing from, or sale of assets to, the rest of the world; a surplus means lending. The exchange rate is one of the adjustment variables, together with interest rates and reserves.
- **Trade-off**: deficits finance investment when returns are high; they leave the country exposed to sudden stops when lenders retreat.
- **Counter-view**: economists disagree about whether persistent deficits matter for a country that issues a reserve currency, and whether surplus countries share responsibility for global imbalances. Skill coverage of this topic is thin; check a primary source.

## Checklist
- Is the question about level, change or regime?
- Does the regime match the country's debts (currency of denomination), openness and institutions?
- Is a cause being assigned to the exchange rate when it is also an effect?
