# Chapter 17: Pensions, Pay-as-You-Go and Demographic Funding

> Educational reading guide, not advice. Pension rules, ages and coefficients change by law and by country; check current official sources before quoting any figure.

## Core Idea
A public pension can be financed by current contributions (pay-as-you-go, PAYG), by an accumulated fund (funded), or by a mix. The choice decides who bears demographic and wage risk, and whether a promised benefit is a claim on a fund or on future workers. An individual account can look like savings and still be paid from current contributions. Real resources at retirement are always produced by the workers of that time; the financing layer changes who holds the claims.

## 17.1 Pay-as-you-go versus funded systems
- **Thesis**: in a PAYG system, today's retirees are paid from today's contributions. A funded system pays from assets accumulated in advance. Both depend on the output of the economy in the future.
- **Mechanism**: a PAYG system's sustainability depends on the ratio of contributors to beneficiaries, on wages, employment and productivity. A funded system's sustainability depends on investment returns, on the prices at which assets are sold to pay benefits, and on the institutions that hold them. In both, the goods and services consumed by retirees are produced by people working at the time.
- **Trade-off**: PAYG shares demographic risk across generations, and it fails when the ratio falls. A funded system holds assets whose value and returns vary, and it does not depend on the ratio in the same way.
- **Counter-view**: some argue that a funded system is no protection if the real economy cannot supply the goods that the claims represent. Others argue that funding gives a buffer and makes the cost visible.
- **Does not hold when**: the system is small relative to the economy and the demographic ratio is stable.

## 17.2 Dependency ratio and the arithmetic of contributions
- **Thesis**: the ratio of people past retirement age to people of working age drives the cost of PAYG, and falling fertility and rising life expectancy raise that ratio.
- **Mechanism**: for a given replacement rate, the contribution rate needed rises with the dependency ratio. With benefits held constant, a falling worker-to-retiree ratio requires higher contributions, lower benefits, later retirement, or higher productivity or employment to offset it. Projections depend on assumptions about fertility, migration and productivity, which are uncertain. Replacement fertility is about 2.1 to 2.3 births per woman (ch11).
- **Trade-off**: a later retirement age shortens the benefit period and lengthens the contribution period. The net effect depends on health, job design and the demand for older workers.
- **Does not hold when**: productivity or employment rises enough to offset the ratio, or migration changes it.

## 17.3 Notional accounts and what "individual" means
- **Thesis**: a notional defined-contribution account records contributions with a revaluation index. The pension is still paid from current contributions, so the balance is a bookkeeping claim, not a fund.
- **Mechanism**: the account is credited with contributions revalued by a growth index (often wages or the economy's growth). At retirement, the balance is converted into an annuity with a coefficient that depends on age and life expectancy. Whether the balance can be paid depends on the system's finances at that time. In a funded individual account, the balance is backed by assets that exist regardless of later contributors.
- **Position**: notional accounts give transparency and link benefits to contributions over the working life. Critics say that the balance is a promise and that the revaluation rule is a policy choice, not a market return.
- **Trade-off**: transparency against the impression of ownership.
- **Does not hold when**: the balance is backed by assets held in a separate fund.

## 17.4 Replacement rate and the typical benefit
- **Thesis**: a benefit is judged against earnings through the replacement rate (pension divided by last or lifetime-average earnings), and the typical benefit is the median, not the mean.
- **Mechanism**: the replacement rate depends on the earnings history, the number of contribution years, the coefficient at retirement and the indexation rule. A mean pension is pulled up by high earners, while the median pension is the typical one. Headline figures can use different denominators (last salary, lifetime average, net or gross), and comparing them is misleading.
- **Trade-off**: a replacement rate looks adequate for people with long, continuous careers and falls for those with gaps, part-time work or low pay.
- **Does not hold when**: earnings are stable and careers are continuous.

## 17.5 Separating contributory benefits from assistance
- **Thesis**: a pension system often mixes contributory benefits, financed by contributions, with assistance benefits, financed by general taxation. Their balances must be read separately.
- **Mechanism**: the contributory balance is contributions minus benefits for contributors. Assistance benefits show a deficit covered by taxes. The total cost is the sum. Reclassifying a benefit moves a deficit between the two columns without changing the total. Special early-retirement and supplementary rules can be costly relative to the contributions that finance them.
- **Trade-off**: separation improves transparency. It can also be used to present a balanced contributory account while the assistance cost is reported elsewhere.
- **Does not hold when**: the system has no assistance component.

## 17.6 Early retirement, rule changes and acquired rights
- **Thesis**: rule changes move costs across cohorts. A reform is judged by its transition rules as much as by its long-run cost.
- **Mechanism**: early exits reduce contribution years and extend the benefit period, so each such rule transfers cost to later cohorts unless it is financed. Acquired rights protect past accruals, so reforms usually apply to future accruals and to new entrants. Gradual reforms reduce shocks for people close to retirement; abrupt reforms reduce future costs faster but harm those close to the change.
- **Position**: some argue that early retirement is a fair reward for long careers. Others argue that it is an unfunded transfer to the cohort that benefits. Both are positions.
- **Does not hold when**: the rule is fully financed and the transition is clear.

## 17.7 The Ponzi analogy (position)
- **Thesis**: the analogy is often used. It is mostly wrong on the mechanism and right on one dependency.
- **Mechanism**: a Ponzi scheme pays earlier investors from new investors' money and promises returns that the underlying activity cannot produce. PAYG pays current retirees from current workers, with no promised return on a private fund. The shared feature is dependence on new entrants and on growth of the base. Whether that dependency is sustainable is a question of demography and productivity (17.2), not of the bookkeeping.
- **Counter-view**: critics argue that a system which needs an ever-growing base of contributors is Ponzi-like in its structure. Defenders argue that the analogy ignores the tax base, the economy's growth and the fact that the system does not promise a fixed return.
- **Does not hold when**: the question is about the real mechanism rather than the label.

## 17.8 Occupational and supplementary pensions
- **Thesis**: supplementary funds, financed by employer and employee contributions, add a funded layer. Their fees, default options and tax design shape the net outcome.
- **Mechanism**: employer contributions are an immediate addition to the saver's balance, which the investor cannot replicate with private saving (see `investing-and-financial-history` ch14). Fees accumulate over a working life. Default options drive most outcomes because most members do not change them. At retirement, a lump sum can be converted into a lifetime income, and the price of that conversion depends on the mortality table and the loading.
- **Trade-off**: lock-up rules protect long horizons and limit flexibility.
- **Does not hold when**: there is no employer contribution and fees are high relative to the balance.

## 17.9 Checklist for a pension reform
1. Which part is contributory and which is assistance?
2. What is the replacement rate for the median worker, and for people with long and short careers?
3. What dependency ratio, and what fertility, migration and productivity assumptions, does the projection use?
4. Who bears the risk: current workers, retirees, taxpayers or fund holders?
5. What are the transition rules and the acquired rights?
6. What is the long-run cost, and is it disclosed on the same basis as the benefit?
7. What rival assumption gives a different answer, and by how much?

## Common mistakes
- Treating an individual notional balance as a fund.
- Comparing a mean pension with a median wage, or the reverse.
- Reclassifying spending between columns to change the apparent deficit.
- Treating demographic projections as facts rather than assumptions.
- Quoting a rule without its year and transition period.
