# Chapter 01: Money, Banks and Money Creation

## Core Idea
Most of the money people use is **bank debt that the public accepts as payment**, not cash. In this model the key questions about any monetary claim are: who issues it, why do others accept it, what backs the promise to redeem it, and what happens when holders doubt that promise. Banks earn a living by turning illiquid private debts into liquid money, which is useful and fragile at once.

## 1.1 What makes something money
- **Thesis**: money is a medium of exchange, a unit of account and a store of value; anyone can issue debt, but only some debt is accepted as money.
- **Mechanism**: a claim gets accepted as money when (1) it is easy to transfer between parties, (2) it can be exchanged for the more basic form (coins, cash, central bank reserves) on demand, and (3) holders believe the issuer is good for the debt. Acceptance is the scarce ingredient, not the act of writing a promise.
- **Trade-off**: the more a claim is accepted, the more the economy depends on its issuer staying credible.
- **Counter-view**: commodity-money views stress that value comes from the metal or the asset behind the claim; credit views stress the web of promises and acceptance. Economic history shows both: coins whose metal content drifted, and deposit money accepted at a premium over coins.

## 1.2 How a bank creates money (step by step)
- **Thesis**: a bank that lends does not need to collect deposits first; lending creates a matching deposit.
- **Mechanism** (a worked chain): shareholders put in initial coins as reserves. A borrower (say a railway company) takes a loan and, instead of withdrawing coins, opens a deposit account at the bank. Now two debts exist for the same amount: the borrower owes the bank (repayable in a few years, illiquid) and the bank owes the borrower (a deposit, redeemable on demand). Workers paid in deposits accept them because transfers are easy, deposits convert to cash, and the bank holds both reserves and a loan book. The bank has turned an illiquid private debt into a liquid, widely accepted one.
- **Trade-off**: the bank earns the gap between the loan rate and the deposit rate, but takes the risk that borrowers default or depositors withdraw at once.
- **Counter-view (textbook money multiplier, position of the model: weak)**: the older teaching says banks wait for reserves and then lend out a fixed fraction, so the central bank controls total money through reserves. In this model the causality runs mostly the other way for lending decisions: banks lend when they find creditworthy borrowers and then obtain the reserves they need; reserves constrain mainly through prices (the rate) and regulation. Some economists defend the multiplier as a useful classroom device or as binding in particular regimes; treat the strict version as contested.

## 1.3 Who disciplines money creation
- **Thesis**: because banks create money by choosing whom to lend to, their screening is both a public good and a source of risk.
- **Mechanism**: a banker who monetises the debts of weak borrowers faces a run when depositors doubt the loans; so the bank has an incentive to monetise only debts it believes will be repaid. Benefits go to the economy when loans fund production (a factory, a railway): new money comes with new output, so it is less likely to push all prices up.
- **Trade-off**: credit that funds existing assets rather than new output (see chapters 03 and 07) raises asset prices instead.
- **Counter-view**: critics argue private money creation is inherently procyclical and under-regulated ("dangerous"); defenders point to growth finance and competition among banks. The disagreement is about regulation design (capital, liquidity, deposit insurance), not about whether banks create deposits.

## 1.4 Bank run and the fragility of deposit money
- **Thesis**: a bank is liquid only as long as depositors do not all ask for cash at once.
- **Mechanism**: deposits are redeemable on demand, loans are not. If holders doubt the bank, each has an incentive to withdraw first. The bank must sell assets, borrow reserves from other banks (using loans as collateral) or from the central bank. A sound bank can still fail if the run is fast; an unsound bank can survive while confidence lasts.
- **Trade-off**: deposit insurance and a lender of last resort stop runs but create moral hazard (see chapter 06).
- **Does not hold when**: deposits are fully backed by reserves (a full-reserve bank cannot be run, but also cannot lend deposits).

## 1.5 Full-reserve deposit banks in history
- **Thesis**: public deposit banks that promised a coin for every deposit existed long before modern central banks; in practice the promise was often broken.
- **Mechanism**: from the early fifteenth century in Italian and Spanish ports, and later in a seventeenth-century Dutch city bank, merchants settled large payments by ledger transfer instead of counting coins. Deposit money traded at a premium over coins (about five percent in one Dutch case) because it was safer and easier. But research on surviving records shows these banks quietly advanced credit to cities and trading companies, and several failed in a run when invaded or over-extended.
- **Trade-off**: a 100% reserve rule gives safety but is very hard to enforce when a government or a powerful borrower wants credit in an emergency.
- **Counter-view**: some readers treat these banks as proof that safe money needs full reserves; others read them as proof that any bank under pressure from its sovereign will lend. Both readings fit the evidence.

## 1.6 Private banks, commercial banks and layers of risk
- **Thesis**: a modern large bank stacks several businesses on one balance sheet, and the safest layer (local lending and deposits) can be hostage to the riskiest.
- **Mechanism**: layer one is classic deposit-and-loan banking. Layer two is wealth management: taking in large deposits and investing them, plus advice. Layer three is investment banking: fees for issuing bonds and stocks and advising on takeovers, which gradually turned into own-account lending and trading. A single set of shareholders and one reputation tie the layers together, so a loss or scandal in one drains confidence from all.
- **Trade-off**: size and diversification give profit and stability in some years, and complexity makes monitoring employees and risks very hard.
- **Counter-view**: universal-bank defenders point to diversification; critics prefer separating deposit banking from trading or ring-fencing the local retail unit so it can be rescued alone (see chapter 06).

## 1.7 Banks and secrecy: why small open economies court foreign deposits
- **Thesis**: a small open economy needs foreign currency to pay for what it cannot produce, and some states historically attracted it by guaranteeing bank secrecy.
- **Mechanism**: foreign depositors bring foreign exchange; secrecy attracts tax-avoiding or illicit funds as well as legitimate privacy seekers; the bank earns a spread on large deposits. Reputation damage arrives when regulators fine the bank for helping evade tax or sanctions, or when a local scandal shows the funds were not safe.
- **Trade-off**: reserves and fee income against reputational and legal risk.
- **Counter-view (position)**: economists differ on how much of the business was legitimate; tax evasion is often named as the main driver.

## Checklist
- Name the issuer, the redemption promise and what happens if many holders ask at once.
- Ask whether new credit funds new output or existing assets.
- Separate the money-creation point (settled mechanics) from the regulation point (contested).
