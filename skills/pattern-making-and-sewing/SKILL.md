# Pattern Making and Sewing
**Chapters**: 8 | **Corpus**: ~1,300 practitioner tutorials and commentaries on pattern drafting, sewing, fit and fashion study | **Generated**: 2026-11-21

## Role and Boundaries

This skill **explains and structures how garments are designed, drafted, fitted and sewn**, and turns it into sequences a maker can follow: which measurement to take, which block to start from, how to move a dart, how to read a fit wrinkle, in what order to alter, how to finish a seam, how to match needle, thread and cloth, how to judge a commercial pattern. It gives methods with their evidence status; the maker owns the garment and the test.

**Stay in lane.** This skill gives **no medical, therapeutic or safety-certified advice** (for example on protective or compression garments, or on adaptive wear for a medical condition). Measurements, yardage and percentages are **starting points taken from worked examples**: every number is to be confirmed on a test garment, on the actual fabric and on the actual body. Figures are dated, context-bound snapshots; "approximate" marks values that were unclear in the sources; "Contested" marks places where practitioners disagree.

## Facts vs Positions: how evidence is labelled

Each concept in the chapters has fields such as **Principle**, **How it works / sequence**, **Moves**, **Evidence**, **Trade-off** and **Does not hold when**. The **Evidence** line uses these labels:

- **Practitioner**: a method that several independent makers teach the same way, no controlled test (almost everything in craft: seam allowances, pressing, order of alterations).
- **Observational**: a pattern seen across many garments or fittings (where a fabric tends to pull, which sizes fit which bodies).
- **Position**: one maker's rule or taste, written as such (a preferred ease amount, a favourite palette rule).
- **Contested**: methods that give different answers (for example cup naming, grading amounts, shoulder increments, how short a reinforcing stitch should be); the chapter lists the variants and when each wins.

Read **Does not hold when** before recommending anything: if the limit applies, drop the rule and use a test garment instead.

## The Core Principles (recurrence order)

1. **A block is a base, not a finished truth.** Draft from measurements plus controlled ease, then confirm on a test garment before cutting good cloth.
2. **Move volume, do not lose it.** Most styles come from relocating darts, ease and contour lines; check that every connected seam still matches in length.
3. **Work in a fixed order.** Measure, choose size from the right measurement, make a test, read the fit, alter the pattern (never the finished garment), carry every change to connected pieces, true the lines, add notches, then add seam allowances.
4. **Fit depends on the cloth.** The same pattern behaves differently in another fabric; there is no single correct ease.
5. **Test on the real materials.** A sample in the actual fabric, needle, thread and interfacing is the cheapest insurance in the process.
6. **Do not stretch while sewing.** Most puckers, twisted bias, wavy hems and lumpy corners come from stretching, skipped stabilising or late pressing.
7. **Press at every stage,** trim, clip and finish edges according to fabric weight.
8. **Mysterious machine problems are mismatches** between needle, thread, foot, pressure and cloth: change one variable at a time.
9. **Choose fabric for the silhouette,** fabric changes the result more than any cut detail; read the technical drawing, not the styling photograph.
10. **Trends are raw material.** Adjust a silhouette you already have (length, neckline, pockets, fabric) before buying a new pattern.
11. **Declare every measurement with its measuring point** and unit; many failures begin with a number nobody can reproduce.
12. **Learn fundamentals before specialising;** order skills so each one reuses the last.

## Scope & Limits

Built from practitioner tutorials and commentary, mostly English and some Italian, with automatic captions as the main text source. Captions garble numbers and units (inches versus centimetres, fractions), so every measurement in these chapters is either confirmed by several independent sources or marked approximate. Treat any figure as a starting point and confirm it on a test garment. The material covers women's and general garments; children's, men's tailoring, knitwear and industrial production are touched only briefly. Brand, product and price information is deliberately left out: it dates quickly and belongs in a current search, not in this skill.
