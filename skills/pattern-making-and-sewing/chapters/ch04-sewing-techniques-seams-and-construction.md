# Chapter 4: Sewing Techniques, Seams and Construction

## Core Idea
Clean construction comes from a small set of repeatable habits: stabilize curves before handling them, mark accurately, sew at a controlled distance from the edge, trim and press at every stage, and finish raw edges according to fabric weight. Most visible flaws (puckered necklines, twisted bias, wavy hems, lumpy corners) come from stretching fabric while sewing, skipping a test sample, or pressing too late. Practitioners generally treat a test sample on the real fabric, with the same thread, needle and interfacing, as the cheapest insurance in the whole process.

## 4.1 Straight stitching and seam guidance
- **Principle**: Accuracy comes from guiding the fabric edge, not from watching the needle.
- **How it works / sequence**: Choose a stitch length for the task, align the edge to a throat-plate line, a foot edge or a guide, and let the feed dogs pull the fabric.
- **Moves**:
  - Standard stitch about 2-2.5 mm; reinforcing stitch about 1-1.5 mm; long stitch 4 mm or more for basting and gathering.
  - Common seam allowances: 1 cm (3/8 in), 1.5 cm (5/8 in); a narrow 7 mm allowance is used when sewing with the foot as the guide.
  - Look at the fabric edge and plate marks; keep fabric flat against any magnetic or paper-tape guide, otherwise the seam gets wider.
  - Start and end with 2-3 stitches forward and back, or shorten the stitch to under 1 mm, or knot the tails by hand on delicate fabrics.
  - To resume a broken seam, restart about 2 cm back and overlap.
  - Never sew over pins; remove them as you approach.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Backstitching is fast but adds bulk and shows on sheer or visible seams.
- **Does not hold when**: Gathering lines (no backstitch) or stretch fabrics (straight stitch breaks when pulled).

## 4.2 Corners and curves
- **Principle**: Pivoting with the needle down keeps layers from shifting; trimming removes bulk without cutting the seam.
- **How it works / sequence**: Shorten the stitch about 2.5 cm before the corner, stop with the needle down, lift the foot, pivot, continue; trim, then turn and press.
- **Moves**:
  - Clip the tip at roughly 45 degrees or to about 3 mm from the stitching.
  - Concave curves: clips. Convex curves: notches or small wedges. Stagger cuts between layers.
  - Turn corners with a point tool, pulling gently so the tip is not punched through.
  - A thin cord laid in the seam can help push a sharp corner out.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Aggressive trimming gives a flat result but a weak, fray-prone corner.
- **Does not hold when**: Very thick or loosely woven fabrics, where more allowance or a hand finish is safer.

## 4.3 Marking and basting
- **Principle**: Transfer marks before removing the pattern; baste where the fabric will slide.
- **Moves**:
  - Tailor's tacks: double thread, loose loops, cut between layers; carbon paper with a smooth wheel is faster but may stain.
  - Basting stitches 4 mm or longer, inside the allowance when they must not show, on the stitching line for fittings.
  - Hand-baste slippery, patterned or sheer fabrics.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Basting costs time but prevents unpicking.
- **Does not hold when**: Marking tools that leave residue on white or dry-clean-only cloth.

## 4.4 Seam types and edge finishing
- **Principle**: Match the seam to fabric weight and edge fraying.
- **Moves**:
  - French seam (light and sheer fabrics): wrong sides together, stitch about 5-7 mm, trim to 2-3 mm, press, fold right sides together, stitch about 5 mm to enclose the edge. Trimming before the second pass is essential. Not suited to tight curves.
  - Mock French: fold each allowance to the seam and stitch the folds together; easier on curves, one extra row of stitching visible inside.
  - Flat-felled seam (denim, shirts): trim one allowance to about 3 mm, fold the other over it, topstitch about 2-3 mm from the fold.
  - Without an overlocker: zigzag in the middle of the allowance (about 2 mm wide, 1-1.5 mm long), then trim close; or an overcast foot; pinking after sewing.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner for French and flat-felled; Contested on second-pass width (5 mm vs 7 mm depends on fabric weight).
- **Trade-off**: Enclosed seams are neat but need generous allowance and more time.
- **Does not hold when**: Heavy fabrics (bulk) or very curved seams.

## 4.5 Darts, pleats and gathers
- **Principle**: Shape comes from symmetric, accurately marked fullness.
- **Moves**:
  - Darts: mark on the wrong side, pin matching legs, sew from the wide end to the point, run off the fold with 2-3 stitches rather than reversing at the tip, compare pairs, press over a curved form.
  - Pleats: planned fullness is twice the finished pleat depth; press before stitching; use the foot edge as a spacing guide; press from the wrong side first.
  - Gathers: two rows of long stitch with loosened tension, pull one thread, knot, distribute, sew with the gathering row inside the allowance.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Gathers add volume at the waist; pleats lie flatter.
- **Does not hold when**: Slippery fabrics, where gathering threads slide and pleats need basting.

## 4.6 Necklines, armholes and facings
- **Principle**: Curves on the bias distort unless stabilized immediately.
- **Moves**:
  - Stay-stitch (about 1.5 mm stitch, around 3 mm outside the final seam line) or fuse bias interfacing before handling.
  - Facing: interface, sew right sides together, trim facing allowance smaller than body allowance (about 3 mm vs 5 mm), clip curves, understitch 2-3 mm from the seam on the facing, tack at shoulders.
  - V-necks: sew to the point with needle down, pivot, trim close without cutting through.
  - Bias binding: never stretch on straight edges; slightly ease on curves; stitch about 1 mm from the fold.
  - Knit necklines: band about 85% of the opening.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner; Observational for the 85% figure (approximate).
- **Trade-off**: Facings are invisible from outside but can flip; binding is visible but stays flat.
- **Does not hold when**: Heavy fabrics where facing bulk shows.

## 4.7 Zippers, buttonholes and fasteners
- **Principle**: Test and position the closure before final seaming.
- **Moves**:
  - Invisible zipper: press teeth away from the tape, use the dedicated foot, sew one side at a time, insert before closing the seam below; check symmetry with the zip closed.
  - Buttonhole length: button width plus about 3 mm (up to 5 mm for thick buttons); sew a test hole on the same fabric and interfacing; cut open only after stitching.
  - Hand-sewn button: shank by winding thread about 3 times; machine: use a shank foot.
  - Hook and eye: set about 3 mm from the edge.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Machine buttonholes are fast; hand buttonholes are more durable on tailoring.
- **Does not hold when**: Pile or fleece fabrics where the buttonhole foot jams.

## 4.8 Pockets
- **Moves**:
  - In-seam pockets: opening about 13-20 cm (15-17 cm commonly preferred), pocket bag attached about 6 mm from the seam edge so it hides, interface the opening, reinforce the corners.
  - Patch pockets: double-fold the top, press with a cardboard template for matching pairs, stitch about 2-3 mm from the edge, reinforce upper corners.
  - Compare both pockets for symmetry before attaching.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: In-seam pockets are hidden but can gape.
- **Does not hold when**: Thick fabrics.

## 4.9 Sleeves, collars and cuffs
- **Moves**:
  - Sleeve: single notch front, double notch back; two gathering rows between the notches; pin underarm, shoulder, center; sew with the sleeve on top.
  - Collar and lapel: match notches, ease slightly at points, trim and topstitch about 1 mm.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Hand-basting is slow but stable.
- **Does not hold when**: Loosely woven or sheer fabrics need hand basting first.

## 4.10 Hems
- **Moves**:
  - Narrow rolled hem without a special foot: stitch about 5 mm from edge, fold, stitch again, trim close, fold and stitch a third time. Fine needle, short stitch, tissue paper underneath on sheer fabrics.
  - Curved hems: a gathering stitch along the edge shapes the curve before pressing.
  - Blind hem: finish the edge, baste, bite just one thread of the outer fabric.
  - Knit hems: ballpoint or stretch needle, walking foot, fusible stretch web, narrow zigzag or twin needle.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner; Contested on whether twin-needle hems match a true coverstitch (they stretch less).
- **Trade-off**: Hand hems are invisible but slow.
- **Does not hold when**: Very elastic fabrics with straight stitching.

## 4.11 Pressing
- **Principle**: Press, do not iron: lift and place rather than slide.
- **Moves**: Press each seam flat before opening, use a curved form for shaped seams, a pressing cloth on delicate fabrics, steam only on pile fabrics.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Over-pressing leaves imprints; under-pressing looks homemade.
- **Does not hold when**: Heat-sensitive synthetics (test first).

## Quick use
| Situation | Start with |
|---|---|
| Wavy or stretched neckline | Stay-stitch or bias interfacing before anything else |
| Sheer, fraying fabric | French seam or rolled hem with tissue underneath |
| Jersey hem rolls or waves | Stretch needle, fusible web, walking foot |
| Visible corner bulk | Shorten stitches, clip at 45 degrees, turn with point tool |
| Fabric slides when sewing | Hand-baste, then machine |
| Unsure about a setting | Test sample on the same fabric |
