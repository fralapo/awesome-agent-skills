# Chapter 5: Fabrics, Notions, Tools and Repair/Upcycling

## Core Idea
Every garment starts with a material decision and a tool decision. Fabric behaves according to its grain, weight, fibre and structure; the sewing machine and hand tools must be matched to that behaviour, not the other way round. Most "mysterious" problems (puckering, skipped stitches, drifting layers, distorted pieces) trace back to a mismatch between needle, thread, foot, pressure and cloth. The same logic carries into pattern software, fabric estimating, repair and reuse: measure, test on a scrap, then commit.

## 5.1 Grain, selvedge and pattern placement
- **Principle**: Woven cloth has three directions with different stretch. The pattern must follow the grain.
- **How it works / sequence**: Lengthwise grain runs parallel to the selvedge and is the most stable. Crosswise grain is perpendicular and gives slightly. Bias runs at 45 degrees and stretches most.
- **Moves**:
  - Recognise the selvedge: a finished edge that does not fray, often with printed text, regular dots or holes, or a print running to the edge.
  - Do not trust a shop-cut end as straight; it is cut on the crosswise direction.
  - Place the grainline arrow parallel to the selvedge and measure from arrow to selvedge at both ends; the two values must match.
  - To find the bias, mark a square on the fabric and draw its diagonal.
  - Pin inside the seam allowance, cut with long continuous strokes, transfer marks with a method tested on a scrap.
  - Wash or steam natural fibres before cutting to allow for shrinkage.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Careful grain alignment uses more cloth and time.
- **Does not hold when**: The fabric is a knit or a deliberately cross-cut design; then the design decides the direction.

## 5.2 Choosing fabric: weight, drape, hand
- **Principle**: The fabric must suit the silhouette. A strong design in the wrong cloth fails.
- **How it works / sequence**: Judge weight (very light to heavy), fibre, hand and structure before judging colour.
- **Moves**:
  - Rigid, geometric shapes need crisp, compact cloth; fluid shapes and drapes need soft, light cloth.
  - Strong textures, pile or prints call for simple patterns.
  - Shop tests: crumple for creasing, let the sample slide vertically for drape, view it in a mirror.
  - Beginner ladder: stable cotton, then detailed cotton, then wool, then silk, then viscose or slippery synthetics.
  - Silks differ: satin-backed crepe and charmeuse are fluid (good on the bias), organza is stiff, chiffon and georgette are light (georgette is grainier), taffeta and duchess satin are structured, jersey silk is stretchy.
  - Velvet has an extra yarn system (pile), and the pile may have a direction. Chenille is not velvet.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Fluid cloths need cleaner finishing and are less forgiving of mistakes.
- **Does not hold when**: The sample is too small to show drape; always test a larger piece.

## 5.3 Underlining, prints and special fabrics
- **Principle**: An extra layer or a special fibre changes how cloth behaves; handle it as one fabric.
- **Moves**:
  - Underlining: cut with the same pattern, join layers by hand basting (running stitch about 5 mm / 0.2 in inside the seam allowance) or temporary spray (about 25-30 cm / 10-12 in away), then sew as one.
  - Hand basting is safer on silk; test any adhesive on a scrap.
  - Directional prints force a one-way layout; the colour on a screen rarely matches cloth.
  - Sequins are small and dense; larger flat discs are usually called paillettes (terms are used loosely).
  - Satin of polyester needs a low iron and a pressing cloth.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner for underlining; Contested for the sequin/paillette distinction (sources use the terms interchangeably).
- **Trade-off**: Underlining adds weight and stiffness.
- **Does not hold when**: The garment must be very light or stretchy.

## 5.4 Sewing difficult cloth
- **Principle**: Treat the cause of the problem, usually the needle, before the tension.
- **Moves**:
  - Slippery silks: sandwich the cloth between tissue paper, cut through all layers, pin in the seam allowance, mark with basting; use fine sharp needles (about 65/9-75/11), a short straight stitch (about 1.5-2 mm), a walking foot, and knot the thread ends instead of backstitching.
  - Double gauze: lower presser pressure, longer stitch, stay stitch the edges, finish seams or use French seams.
  - Fleece-like knits: knit or stretch needle, walking foot, washable tape instead of pins.
  - Stretch knits: ballpoint or stretch needle, a stretch stitch or overlock.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Stabilising and prepping takes longer than sewing.
- **Does not hold when**: The cloth is stable and light; ordinary settings work.

## 5.5 Estimating fabric
- **Principle**: Yardage depends on garment length, fabric width, direction and pattern repeat.
- **Moves**:
  - One-way layouts use more cloth than two-way; checks and stripes need extra for matching.
  - Width matters: pieces that sit side by side at 150 cm (about 59 in) may not at 120 cm (about 47 in).
  - Rough rules at about 145 cm width: add hem and waist allowances to garment length, plus roughly 5 cm for shrinkage and cutting corrections (approximate).
  - Sampling uses only the pattern length; production nests pieces across layers and sizes in a marker and averages by size mix.
  - Compute per fabric variant: lining and contrast separately.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner (examples are approximate)
- **Trade-off**: Generous estimates waste cloth; tight ones risk running short.
- **Does not hold when**: The layout is asymmetric or cut on the bias; simulate with scale patterns instead.

## 5.6 The sewing machine: needle, thread, tension
- **Principle**: Diagnose in a fixed order: rethread, check the needle, then adjust tension.
- **Moves**:
  - Thread with the presser foot up so the tension discs open; leave a 7-10 cm (3-4 in) tail.
  - Needle by cloth: universal 80/12 or 90/14 for medium cloth; finer for light (about 65-70); heavier for denim and upholstery (about 110-116); ballpoint for knits; wider eye for thick thread. Change the needle for each project.
  - Flat side toward the back, push fully up, tighten.
  - Wind the bobbin with taut thread; insert it in the direction shown by the machine; pick up the bobbin thread by turning the handwheel toward you.
  - Tension: a loose top thread shows on the underside; a tight top thread pulls the bobbin thread up. Adjust the bobbin screw only on a spare bobbin.
  - Use good-quality polyester thread; cheap thread sheds lint.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Frequent needle changes cost little but are often skipped.
- **Does not hold when**: The machine is industrial or specialised; follow its manual.

## 5.7 Presser feet and pressure
- **Principle**: The foot must match the stitch and the cloth.
- **Moves**:
  - A straight-stitch foot is for straight stitch only; zigzag needs a foot with side room, or the needle strikes metal.
  - A zipper foot or a special foot for specific tasks.
  - A walking foot (even feed) suits slippery, stretchy, thick, quilted and striped fabrics; check the attachment spacing (about 5 mm or 7 mm, approximate) for the machine.
  - Pressure: higher for light cloth that shifts, lower for thick cloth that will not feed; non-stick foot or tape for vinyl and leather; tear-away stabiliser as a fallback.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner; Contested for pressure numbers (sources quote values that need checking against the manual).
- **Trade-off**: More feet means more cost and more storage.
- **Does not hold when**: The machine has no pressure control.

## 5.8 Machine care, buying, overlock
- **Moves**:
  - Clean with a soft brush; oil only with the supplied oil, a drop on the hook, never on plastic; rest with the foot down; service yearly.
  - When buying: straight stitch quality over feature count, weight and stability, movable needle position, even feed; test used machines.
  - Overlock: guide the edge to the knife, never pull the cloth, slow down at corners with the foot raised and needle up, do not use it to trim pattern pieces.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Overlock is fast but threading is complex.
- **Does not hold when**: The cloth is very heavy for overlock settings.

## 5.9 Cutting, marking, pressing tools, dress forms
- **Moves**:
  - Dressmaking shears for cloth only; a separate pair for paper; light, sharp blades reduce hand fatigue.
  - Glass-head pins resist the iron; plastic heads melt; fine pins for delicate cloth.
  - Tracing wheels and chalk wheels give fine lines; keep magnetic holders away from computerised machines.
  - Pressing: press a closed seam flat before opening it; use a tailor's ham for curves.
  - Dress forms: not essential at home; soft pinnable forms suit draping; padding can be added but not reduced.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Tool quality matters more than count.
- **Does not hold when**: Cloth is very heavy and needs stronger pins or shears.

## 5.10 Pattern software and markers
- **Principle**: Digital patterns separate drawing from production pieces.
- **Moves**:
  - Draft in a plan view, then set production pieces; add seam allowances there. Only pieces that are set appear in the marker.
  - Example allowances (approximate): neck and armhole 0.7 cm (0.3 in), shoulder and side 1 cm (0.4 in), hem 1.7 cm (0.7 in).
  - Assign pieces to fabric lists (main, contrast, lining, rib); different fabrics need separate markers.
  - Limit piece rotation to 180 degrees on directional cloth; efficiency drops.
  - Exchange files as DXF with correct units; export at 100% scale for printing.
- **Evidence**: Observational
- **Trade-off**: Setup is slow but saves time across sizes.
- **Does not hold when**: A single quick sample is needed.

## 5.11 Repair, reuse, decoration
- **Moves**:
  - Scraps: plan a patchwork or strip layout before sewing; replace sleeves on tired shirts; for reused garments, dismantle first and choose a pattern for the pieces available.
  - Fur offcuts: waistcoat, scarf (strips about 10 cm / 4 in), muffs; free pile from seams with a needle.
  - Vintage coats: tighten sleeves, lower shoulders, shorten and reuse offcuts.
  - Fabric paint: generous colour, card inside the garment, dry one to two days, iron on the reverse, hand-wash cold; white only in small areas, or the cloth stiffens.
- **Evidence**: Observational; Contested for drying time (two hours versus one to two days).
- **Trade-off**: Reuse costs time and offers limited yardage.
- **Does not hold when**: The base cloth is synthetic or very dark; paint adheres poorly.

## Quick use
| Situation | Start with |
|---|---|
| Pattern looks crooked on cloth | Measure arrow-to-selvedge at both ends |
| Puckered seam | Check needle size, then tension |
| Slippery cloth | Tissue paper, fine needle, walking foot |
| Skipped stitches | New needle, rethread |
| Estimating yardage | Width, direction, pattern repeat, shrinkage |
| Choosing fabric | Weight and drape test against the shape |
| Painting on cloth | Card inside, thin even coats, long drying |
