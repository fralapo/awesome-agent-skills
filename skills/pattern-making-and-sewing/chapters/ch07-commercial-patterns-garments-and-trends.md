# Chapter 7: Commercial Patterns, Garment Types and Trends

## Core Idea
A commercial sewing pattern is a product with a format, a size range, a level of instruction and an intended fabric. Choosing well means reading the envelope and the technical line drawing rather than the styling photograph. Trend-driven sewing rarely needs a new pattern: practitioners generally pick the closest silhouette they already own or can buy, then adjust length, neckline, pockets and fabric. Fabric choice changes the result more than any detail of the cut, and most recurring failures come from a mismatch between pattern, fabric and the wearer's measurements.

## 7.1 Evaluating a pattern before buying
- **Principle**: The information that predicts success is on the back of the envelope or in the listing, not on the cover image.
- **How it works / sequence**: Check the size range and cup options, then the finished garment measurements, then the intended fabric type, then the instruction style and stated level.
- **Moves**:
  - Compare your own body measurements (bust, waist, hip), not your usual shop size, with the size chart.
  - Read the finished garment measurements and compare them with a garment you already own and like.
  - Check whether the pattern is drafted for knit or woven fabric; many are not interchangeable.
  - Look at the line drawing for darts, pleats, pockets, collar shape and back details that the photo hides.
  - Check the fabric requirement and the layout plans before buying cloth.
  - Match the instruction style to your experience: photographic or step-by-step illustrated for beginners; diagram-only or sparse text for experienced sewers.
  - Check whether seam allowances are included; this is a frequent unanswered question for magazine and book patterns.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Careful reading takes time, but avoids buying a pattern that stops at a size you need or assumes skills you lack.
- **Does not hold when**: Vintage or out-of-print patterns, where the size chart and back view may be missing, so checking measurements directly on the pattern tissue is the only reliable method.

## 7.2 Pattern formats
- **Principle**: The same design reaches the sewer as tissue in an envelope, a PDF, a pull-out sheet in a magazine, a trace-off sheet in a book, or a single colour-coded sheet. Each format shifts effort to a different stage.
- **How it works / sequence**: Choose the format that fits your printing access, tolerance for tracing and shipping cost.
- **Moves**:
  - Envelope tissue: sizes are nested, so cut around your size; paper weight is typically light (roughly 60 to 80 g/m², approximate).
  - Single large sheet with one colour per size: economical, but dashed and solid lines can be confusing and instructions are usually minimal.
  - Magazine and book patterns: pieces overlap on shared sheets, often two designs per sheet in different colours; trace only the pieces needed. Tracing is usually the longest step.
  - PDF: layered files let you print only the sizes you need; large-format print shops avoid taping dozens of A4 or Letter sheets; check for missing alignment marks after printing.
  - For a pattern used repeatedly, a professionally printed copy saves tracing time.
  - International shipping of paper patterns can cost more than the pattern; PDF avoids this.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Paper saves assembly but costs shipping and storage; PDF is instant and gradable by layer but demands printing and taping.
- **Does not hold when**: Instructions are only available by QR code or download and you want a printed booklet.

## 7.3 Size ranges, cups and children's sizing
- **Principle**: Size range is the most common reason a pattern is rejected, and ranges vary widely between designers.
- **Moves**:
  - Expect ranges that stop around UK 20 to 22 for some publishers and extend to roughly UK 30 to 34 for others (approximate).
  - Some patterns split into two size bands that do not overlap; check which band holds your size.
  - Check cup options (commonly A to D) against your own pattern block, not only your size.
  - Children's patterns use height, age or, for babies, weight; read the chart and compare with finished measurements.
  - Vintage patterns often come in a single size.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Wide size ranges are rare but valued; narrower ranges are easier to grade well.
- **Does not hold when**: A designer states different ranges for paper and PDF versions; always read the listing for the format you buy.

## 7.4 Matching a pattern to a reference garment
- **Principle**: Look for the closest silhouette and construction, not an exact copy.
- **How it works / sequence**: Read the technical drawing of the reference, identify the defining detail (neckline, sleeve, waist seam, pocket), find a pattern with the same structure, then modify.
- **Moves**:
  - Start from the nearest pattern and change length, neckline depth, pockets, collar or ruffles.
  - Hybrids are common: bodice of one pattern, skirt of another, sleeve of a third.
  - Skirt slits and splits should be drafted with a centre seam, not added later.
  - Remove details to save time (lining, pockets, cuffs) without changing the silhouette.
  - Add pleats to the flat pattern before cutting rather than to the finished garment.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Hybrids need extra fitting; modifications to armhole and neckline are the riskiest edits.
- **Does not hold when**: The garment depends on a custom-draped or fabric-specific effect that no flat pattern reproduces.

## 7.5 Translating trends into sewing
- **Principle**: Most fashion trends are combinations of a familiar silhouette, a fabric and one or two details.
- **Moves**:
  - Oversized looks: practitioners generally recommend sizing up one or two sizes rather than redrafting. Contested: some sources say one size, others two for jackets and bombers. A cut for a small frame can look overwhelmed by oversize.
  - Outerwear: bombers, trenches, car coats, pea coats, barn jackets and quilted jackets; quilt the outer fabric before cutting when making a quilted jacket; a coat with no closures and dropped shoulders is a gentle first coat.
  - Trousers: wide-leg, barrel, balloon, harem, culotte, palazzo; elastic or flat-fronted waists; one base pattern can give several lengths; cropped versions need lengthening by roughly 2 to 3 cm (about 1 inch, approximate) when a pattern is too short.
  - Pull-on trousers: fabric with at least about 5% elastane to hold shape.
  - Dresses: pinafores, shirt dresses, wrap dresses, slip dresses, cut-out styles (easier in jersey, which forgives measurements); check bust support.
  - Skirts: A-line midi, bias slip, pleated, pencil with slit; hem length mid-calf or longer is common.
  - Co-ords: matching top and bottom from one pattern, or one fabric across two patterns.
  - Puff sleeves: elastic set in the shoulder seam or darts in the sleeve head give volume.
  - Shirring is not smocking; the terms describe different techniques.
- **Evidence**: Observational
- **Trade-off**: Trends date quickly; the base silhouettes are more durable.
- **Does not hold when**: The reference garment is knitted and the substitute is cut from woven or jersey fabric; only the silhouette transfers.

## 7.6 Garment categories and their points of risk
- **Principle**: Each category has a predictable difficult point.
- **Moves**:
  - Dungarees: try the bib and pin darts before sewing; cross-back straps and back attachments are delicate; structured fabrics need reinforced pockets.
  - Swimwear: sew like jersey but line with a stable lining, since unlined fabric turns see-through when wet; add band support under the bust; cup sizes vary.
  - Pyjamas: raglan sleeves without piping are the quickest start; knit is more forgiving than woven; very wide sleeves can tangle in bedding.
  - Bags: no fit problems and little fabric; hardware, interfacing and strap attachment are the hard parts; kits help.
  - Bias-cut garments: handle with extreme care; many practitioners let the piece hang for about 24 hours before hemming (approximate) and hand-baste edges while flat.
  - Zero-waste and rectangle designs: work from written measurements marked directly on fabric; loose cuts reduce fitting problems but can overwhelm small frames.
  - Coats and jackets: many pieces; trench patterns can exceed about 30 pieces (approximate); plan the order of work and spread it over weeks.
  - Children's wear: simple forms; small linings and closures take longer than expected.
  - Sequinned and metallic fabrics need suitable needles; remove sequins from seam allowances.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Quick builds suit beginners, but those with fewer fitting seams may lose shaping.
- **Does not hold when**: An experienced sewer already knows the technique; the "risk" labels reflect beginner experience.

## 7.7 Fabric drives the outcome
- **Principle**: The same pattern behaves differently in crisp cotton, fluid viscose, knit or heavy denim.
- **Moves**:
  - Choose fabric first for drape-dependent designs such as wrap dresses; stiff fabric loses the drape.
  - Slippery fabrics (satin, chiffon, silk) need patience and finished seams; a walking foot or slip-fabric foot helps.
  - Knit patterns need knit fabric; woven in place of jersey changes both fit and drape.
  - Faux suede and faux leather are synthetic and sew differently from the real material.
  - Heavy or stiff fabric inflates a tailored or oversized silhouette.
  - Test on a muslin (toile) in similar fabric before costly cloth, especially for modified or upsized patterns.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Testing costs time; skipping it is where most fit failures start.
- **Does not hold when**: The garment is low-risk, loose and in a forgiving fabric.

## 7.8 Planning and choosing projects
- **Principle**: A flexible, comfort-led plan beats a long ambitious list.
- **Moves**:
  - Beginners: one new technique per project; raglan or grown-on sleeves avoid set-in sleeve errors; a drawstring waist is forgiving.
  - Capsule wardrobes of seven to eight interchangeable pieces make sense of a pattern collection.
  - Review the list mid-year; a long list leads to frustration.
  - Small, quick makes (hats, aprons, bags) suit gifts and scraps.
- **Evidence**: Observational
- **Trade-off**: Quick projects teach fewer skills.
- **Does not hold when**: Deadlines or events force a particular garment.

## Quick use
| Situation | Start with |
|---|---|
| Considering a pattern purchase | Size chart, finished measurements, intended fabric |
| Copying a garment from a photo | Technical drawing, nearest silhouette, then modify |
| Want an oversized look | Size up one or two sizes, test on a toile |
| First coat or jacket | No closures, dropped shoulder, simple lining |
| Swimwear | Stretch fabric with stable lining and bust support |
| Bias-cut skirt or dress | Rest piece hanging about 24 hours before hemming |
| Heavy printed PDF | Large-format print shop instead of taping A4 |
| Children's garment | Choose by height or age chart, compare finished measurements |
| Seam allowance unknown | Check before cutting; measure the piece against the drawing |
