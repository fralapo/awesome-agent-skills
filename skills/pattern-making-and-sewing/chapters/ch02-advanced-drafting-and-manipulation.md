# Chapter 2: Advanced Pattern Drafting and Manipulation

## Core Idea
Advanced drafting is the art of changing a fitted block without losing its fit. Almost every style is made by moving volume (darts, ease, contour lines) to a new place, then checking that every pair of seams still matches in length. Practitioners work in a fixed order: choose the block, modify it by slashing, pivoting or contouring, true the lines, add notches, and only then add seam allowances. Measurements quoted below come from worked examples; they are starting points to be recalculated on your own block, and a test garment settles the doubt.

## 2.1 Dart manipulation and bust adjustments
- **Principle**: Moving a dart changes design lines, not fit. The dart's volume can become a seam, gathers, pleats, a tuck or drape.
- **How it works / sequence**: Anchor at the bust apex (the pivot). Cut along one dart leg up to the pivot without cutting through; leave a hinge of paper about 2-3 mm (about 1/10 in). Rotate to close the old dart and open the new one; back the opening with paper; true the result.
- **Moves**: Pull the dart point back from the apex by about 2.5 cm (1 in) for close-fitting garments, more for loose jackets and coats. For a full-bust adjustment, mark apex-to-waist, apex-to-armhole (about one third up from the underarm) and dart-to-apex lines, cut with hinges, spread by half the total on a half front, then realign the side seam and reposition the dart point (roughly 2.5-3 cm from the apex, further for larger busts). A small-bust adjustment overlaps instead (approximately 2.5 cm per cup size, halved for the half front). For gaping necklines or armholes, pinch the excess on a test garment to the apex and transfer it as a dart.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner.
- **Trade-off**: A larger setback is more forgiving but leaves a visible hollow if too far; too close makes a point.
- **Does not hold when**: The bodice is intentionally loose or draped, or the block has no bust shaping at all.

## 2.2 Contouring, necklines and drape
- **Principle**: Small contour lines (about 0.3-1.3 cm / 1/8-1/2 in) remove the gap that opens at edges of a bodice such as necklines and armholes.
- **How it works / sequence**: Mark contour lines from the edge toward the apex, close them with tape, and redistribute the removed amount into a waist dart. Then redraw the neckline.
- **Moves**: Halter and strapless styles take about 1 cm contour from the edge. For cowl necks, move the side dart to the centre front, redraw the fold line and recopy the facing. A shoulder correction on a cowl is made inside the neckline rather than at the shoulder edge. Twist details use three to four radial slash lines opened about 1.5 in (3.8 cm) each; more opening gives more gathering.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner for contouring; Observational for cowl and twist numbers.
- **Trade-off**: Contouring gives a clean edge but reduces room.
- **Does not hold when**: Fabric is stiff (cotton, linen), where a cowl will not drape; stable fabrics leave excess at the centre.

## 2.3 Sleeves: matching, cap height and styles
- **Principle**: A sleeve cap must match the body armhole length on the notches, with ease only in the cap.
- **How it works / sequence**: Measure the armhole on the seamline (front, side, back), measure the cap, divide any ease between front and back, and place notches in the same places on both.
- **Moves**: Ease is about 1 cm for simple garments and up to about 2.5 cm for jackets; none under the arm. Cap height is commonly one third of the armhole length, within a range of about 25-35% (lower for casual, higher for formal), or 70-75% of the armhole depth; drop-shoulder sleeves use a low cap (roughly 18-25%). A higher cap gives a narrower bicep; lowering the body underarm forces a higher cap or wider bicep. Slash and spread: never cut through, tape to guide paper; more spread means more volume. Gathered caps give more volume than pleats at the hem. Cap pleats are drawn like darts with equal legs. Two-piece sleeve: elbow line at half length plus about 2.5 cm up, hem split about 70/30 between upper and under sleeve, double notch at the back. Raglan: front and back shoulder angles must be equal or the sleeve twists; a sloped shoulder raises the cap and narrows the bicep. A shoulder that passes the underarm line becomes a kimono sleeve.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner for matching and ease; Contested for cap fractions (different bases are used) and bishop spreads (about 9 cm, 3/4 in or 4 in per slash).
- **Trade-off**: More cap height gives a smoother hang but less mobility.
- **Does not hold when**: Stretch fabrics, where ease can be negative.

## 2.4 Collars, lapels and hoods
- **Principle**: Collar length equals the neckline length measured along the curve.
- **How it works / sequence**: Measure back and front neck on the body, add them, then draw the collar from the break point or the neck line.
- **Moves**: A straight stand collar stands away; a curved one (over about 2.5 cm) lies flat. For lapels, the press line starts about 2 cm (3/4 in) from the high shoulder point; draw the lapel shape first, mirror it on the press line, then draw the collar (collar width minus 3/8 in). Single-breasted overlap is about 4 cm, thicker coats 5 cm; double-breasted is set by button spacing. Hood neck equals back neck plus front neck; height is half of head circumference plus about 2.5 cm; depth is half of eye-to-eye measure plus about 5 cm. A two-piece hood converts to three pieces without changing measurements.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner for lapel press line; Observational for hood formulas.
- **Trade-off**: Lower stand is more comfortable, higher is more shaped.
- **Does not hold when**: Rules written for woven fabric are applied to knits.

## 2.5 Skirts and trousers
- **Principle**: Flare and fullness come from spreading pieces; trouser shape depends on the crotch.
- **How it works / sequence**: Spread equally front and back, then check hem and seam lengths. For trousers, set crotch depth, then check inseam lengths.
- **Moves**: Godet template base about 7.6 cm (3 in), height about 20 cm; open lines 3.8 cm and 7.6 cm. Long full skirts: fold across the grain if the width is short. Flare a straight skirt by 3-5 cm per side. Back crotch extension is about hips/8 (plus about 2 cm); the back crotch seam should be no longer than the front. A curved waistband needs a seam on top for low-rise styles. Fly: decide which side is on top as seen by the wearer. Welt pocket: two reference holes about 0.5 cm (1/4 in) from each end. A design gusset does not change fit; a functional one raises the crotch (about 5 cm).
- **Evidence**: Practitioner for crotch and fly; Observational for skirts.
- **Trade-off**: A deeper crotch is comfortable but less tailored.
- **Does not hold when**: Draped skirts of unusual cuts.

## 2.6 Jackets, coats and drafting from measurements
- **Principle**: A block can be built from body measurements with simple fractions.
- **How it works / sequence**: Rectangle (half chest plus ease by length), armhole line, neck and shoulder points, side seam, then panels.
- **Moves**: Ease on half chest approximates 2 in (cropped), 3.5 in (blazer) or 4 in (thick coat). Armhole depth is about chest/4; back neck and high shoulder point about chest/12 plus 2.5 cm; shoulder is half of shoulder width. When hips exceed chest, overlap panels. Sleeve vents are about 11 cm (4.5 in).
- **Evidence**: Practitioner; Contested for the exact ease (varies by garment).
- **Trade-off**: Fast but approximate.
- **Does not hold when**: Unusual body proportions.

## 2.7 Linings and facings
- **Principle**: Lining is cut larger than the shell so the garment does not pull.
- **How it works / sequence**: Copy the shell, then adjust.
- **Moves**: Raise the lining armhole about 1 cm (3/8-1/2 in); lengthen it 1.3-2 cm (1/2-3/4 in); add a centre-back pleat of about 2.5 cm (1 in); lining bicep equals or slightly exceeds the shell. A front facing is about 5 cm (2 in) at the shoulder. Turn-of-cloth is a few millimetres, and sources differ on direction.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner; Contested for facing direction.
- **Trade-off**: More ease means more bulk.
- **Does not hold when**: Unlined garments.

## 2.8 Grading
- **Principle**: Circumferences grow by increment divided by four per panel; distances grow less.
- **How it works / sequence**: Grade the body first, then collars, hoods and sleeves; measure curves, not straight lines.
- **Moves**: For a 5 cm (2 in) chest increment each of four panels grows about 1.25 cm (1/2 in). Bicep grows about 2 cm (3/4 in); sleeve hem about 0.6 cm (1/4 in); sleeve length is not graded. Armhole depth grows about 0.6 cm. Trousers: crotch depth about 0.6 cm; zip length constant; darts and pleats move rather than widen.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner; Contested for shoulder width (1/4 in or 3/4 in per 2 in of chest).
- **Trade-off**: Hand grading is slow but exact.
- **Does not hold when**: Brand tables differ.

## Quick use
| Situation | Start with |
|---|---|
| Side dart gone, want drape | Pivot at apex, keep hinge |
| Sleeve twists | Check shoulder angles and notches |
| Collar does not close | Re-measure neck on the curve |
| Lining pulls | Raise armhole, add length and pleat |
| New size | Grade body first, then measure curves |
