# Chapter 1: Basic Pattern Drafting, Blocks and Draping

## Core Idea
A block (sloper) is a plain, well-fitting base drafted from body measurements plus controlled ease. Every design modifies that base: darts are moved or removed, necklines and armholes reshaped, edges finished with facings or bands. Drafting formulas are starting points built on body proportions; confirm them on a toile (a sample garment in similar fabric) before cutting good cloth. Draping reaches the same result in three dimensions by shaping cloth on a form and transferring it to flat paper. The two approaches complement each other.

## 1.1 Measurements and ease
- **Principle**: A pattern is only as good as the measurements and the ease added to them.
- **How it works / sequence**: Measure over the underwear that will be worn, in thin non-stretch clothing, standing naturally, tape parallel to the floor, snug but not tight. Tie an elastic at the natural waist as a reference. Take bust, waist, hips, back length, shoulder, arm (elbow bent), biceps and crotch depth.
- **Moves**: Crotch depth is outside leg minus inside leg, or waist to a flat chair when seated (add about 0.5-1 cm / 1/4-1/2 in for woven trousers). Repeat measurements on another day: differences above about 1/2 in (1.3 cm) point to technique problems. Typical ease on a fitted woven bodice is about 2.5 cm (1 in) at bust, waist and hips, or roughly 5-7 cm (2-2.75 in) total on the bust circumference (approximate). Loose garments need much more.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: More ease means comfort and a forgiving fit; less means a closer line but less movement.
- **Does not hold when**: Fabric is very stretchy (ease is reduced or negative) or the posture is unusual.

## 1.2 Bodice and torso blocks
- **Principle**: A bodice block is built on a rectangle (half bust plus ease by back length) and refined with proportion-based points.
- **How it works / sequence**: Draw the rectangle, mark armhole depth, neck width, shoulder, waist and hip lines, then add darts and trace separate front and back.
- **Moves**: Neck width is commonly bust/12; the back neck rises about 2-3 cm (3/4-1 1/4 in). Shoulder length is about half the shoulder width, dropped from a few millimetres up to about 4 cm depending on the garment. Armhole depth starts near bust/4 (sometimes minus about 1 cm), bust/5 for close-fitting or knit garments, or 20-25% of the bust (22-23% sleeveless). The front is often about 1/2 in (1.3 cm) wider than the back, accommodating fuller cups. On structured garments the front armhole is about 2 cm shallower than the back. Waist reduction is split between side seam, centre back and dart. Knit blocks are dartless; children's blocks are boxier.
- **Evidence**: Contested for armhole depth (several non-equivalent formulas); Practitioner for neck width and the wider front.
- **Trade-off**: A fitted block gives shape but restricts movement and does not suit stretch fabric.
- **Does not hold when**: The body has a very full bust, sloping shoulders or unusual posture; test and correct on the person.

## 1.3 Darts: types, transfer, truing
- **Principle**: A dart removes a wedge of fabric to shape a flat piece around a curve; its position is a design choice, its size a fit decision.
- **How it works / sequence**: Mark the bust point (apex). To move a dart, draw a line from the new position to the apex, cut along it, close the original dart so the volume transfers, trace the new shape and mark notches after folding.
- **Moves**: Move the whole dart quantity, never half. The dart point stops about 2-3 cm (1 in) short of the apex; ending on it gives a pointed bust. Types: single-point, double-ended (contour), French (from the side seam) and cut-away (centre removed when the dart exceeds about 5 cm / 2 in). For waist darts on skirts and trousers, a starting rule: drop = hip minus waist, split roughly 13% at the side seam, 8% front dart, 16% back dart; with a large drop (about 25-30 cm) use two back darts. Truing: dart legs must be equal; a 3-4 mm difference is split at the midpoint.- **Evidence**: Practitioner for transfer; Position for the waist percentages (one source, stated as a starting point).
- **Trade-off**: Moving a dart keeps the fit but changes style lines.
- **Does not hold when**: The dart is very large or the fabric stretches and shapes itself.

## 1.4 Necklines, facings, collars
- **Principle**: Draft edge finishes on the stitching line and add seam allowance last.
- **How it works / sequence**: Draw the new neckline on the front, check that front and back shoulder seams match, then add allowance. Trace the facing from the finished neckline; cut it in the garment fabric, usually interfaced.
- **Moves**: Deep necklines gape unless a dart is moved into the neckline. Facings are about 5 cm (2 in) wide; the facing edge is made 1-3 mm smaller ("favouring") so it stays inside. Keep about 1 cm flat at centre front to avoid a peak. A collar whose outer edge equals the neck length stands up; a shorter edge hugs the neck (convertible types), a longer edge lies flat on the shoulders. To reshape, cut and open the outer edge keeping the neck length constant. The undercollar is slightly smaller than the upper collar: about 3 mm (1/8 in) for thin fabric, 1-1.3 cm (3/8-1/2 in) for thick.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Wide facings stabilise but add bulk.
- **Does not hold when**: Edges are bound or banded; then the finished shape is used with no allowance.

## 1.5 Sleeves
- **Principle**: The sleeve cap is sewn into an armhole of nearly the same length; cap height and biceps width trade off.
- **How it works / sequence**: Measure the bodice armhole front and back, set cap height, draw the biceps line and cap curve, mark notches (one front, two back), add ease on the cap only.
- **Moves**: Cap height formulas vary: total armhole /3 plus about 1/4 in, total /4 plus 1/4-1/2 in, or about 28-30% of the armhole (approximate). A higher cap gives a narrower biceps and shorter underarm seam; a lower cap allows more movement. Cap ease is about 1/2 to 1 1/2 in (1.3-3.8 cm), never at the underarm. In a two-piece sleeve, add biceps width mostly to the upper piece (for example 3/8 in upper, 1/8 in under).
- **Evidence**: Contested for cap height; Practitioner for ease placement.
- **Trade-off**: A high cap looks neat but limits arm lift.
- **Does not hold when**: The sleeve is dropped-shoulder or raglan.

## 1.6 Skirts and waist finishes
- **Principle**: Circle skirts are geometry; other skirts are rectangles reduced at the waist.
- **How it works / sequence**: Compute the waist radius, draw a right angle, mark radius, length and hem.
- **Moves**: Waist radius = waist x factor / 6.28, factor 4 (quarter circle), 2 (half), about 1.3 (three-quarter), 1 (full); waist / 12.56 for a double circle. The seam allowance is not part of the radius. Bias-cut panels stretch: let the garment hang before hemming. A gathered tier is about 3-4 times the hem circumference. A finished waist is usually about 1 cm (1/2 in) smaller than the pattern waist.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner for circle formulas; Observational for gathering ratios.
- **Trade-off**: More circle means more drape and fabric, and more bias stretch.
- **Does not hold when**: Fabric is narrower than the pattern; pieces must be split.

## 1.7 Trousers
- **Principle**: Crotch depth and extension decide balance; the back is deeper and longer than the front.
- **How it works / sequence**: Set waist, hip and crotch lines, then front and back extensions, knee, hem, darts, waistband.
- **Moves**: Extension is about hip/16 at the front and double (hip/8) at the back; stretch or slim cuts use smaller values, baggy or pleated cuts 1-2 cm more. The hip line sits roughly 7-9 cm above the crotch line. The crotch curve must be smooth. Never change only the back crotch; change front and back proportionally. Check that inseams and side seams match.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner for the 1:2 ratio; Contested for hip-to-crotch distance (sources differ between about 7 and 11 cm).
- **Trade-off**: Deeper crotch gives comfort but excess fabric.
- **Does not hold when**: Body shape is unusual (prominent abdomen, flat seat).

## 1.8 Fitting, grading, truing
- **Principle**: Every pattern change must be trued, then confirmed in a toile.
- **How it works / sequence**: Measure seam lines with a flexible tape and "walk" them, matching notches. Correct small differences at the midpoint; split larger ones (about 2-2.5 cm) between the ends. Make a toile in fabric of similar weight and drape.
- **Moves**: Choose size from bust, waist, hips and finished measurements, not the label. Between two sizes use the intermediate line and blend smoothly; connected pieces stay in the same size. Grade by repeating the step between adjacent sizes.- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Extra toiles cost time but save good fabric.
- **Does not hold when**: The garment is simple enough to build directly on cloth.

## 1.9 Paper, tracing, direct construction
- **Principle**: Paper behaves differently from cloth; pick each for the job.
- **Moves**: Pattern paper has a grain: fold and cut along it. Trace notches, grain lines and key points, and label every piece. Print patterns at 100% and check the scale square (about 5 cm / 2 in). Simple garments can be drafted directly on fabric; paper suits flared, many-piece or bias garments.
- **Evidence**: Observational
- **Trade-off**: Paper patterns are reusable; direct construction is faster for one-offs.
- **Does not hold when**: Pieces must be cut in quantity.

## 1.10 Draping
- **Principle**: Draping builds shape on a form in trial fabric, then transfers it to flat pattern.
- **How it works / sequence**: Mark guide lines on the form (centre front and back, bust, waist, hip, princess lines), prepare rectangles with the grain marked, pin, smooth, mark, transfer to paper.
- **Moves**: Pin at an angle through cloth and form, about 1.5 in (4 cm) apart. Place the grain on centre front. Clip at waist and hips to release tension. End darts short of the apex. Mark seams and notches, then add about 1/2 in (1.3 cm) allowance.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Intuitive for complex shapes, but needs a form and is hard to standardise.
- **Does not hold when**: No form is available or the cloth differs greatly from the trial fabric.

## Quick use
| Situation | Start with |
|---|---|
| Starting any pattern | Measurements plus chosen ease, then a toile |
| Neck width | Bust / 12 |
| Armhole depth | Bust / 4 (fitted), bust / 5 (knit), check on the person |
| Moving a dart | Slash to the apex, move the whole quantity |
| Circle skirt | Radius = waist x factor / 6.28 |
| Trouser crotch | Front hip/16, back double, adjust both |
| Collar sits wrongly | Compare collar edge and neck length |
| After any alteration | True the seams, make a toile |
| Complex shape | Drape first, then flatten |
