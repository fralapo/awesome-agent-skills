# Chapter 8: Wardrobe Building, Styling and Working in Sewing and Fashion

## Core Idea
A wardrobe works when it is built from what already exists, with a small number of decisions repeated consistently: a neutral base, one or two accents, proportions that balance volume against slimness, and accessories that carry the outfit. The same discipline applies on the making side: every garment passes through a fixed sequence (sketch, test fabric, fit, correction, final cloth), every measurement is declared with its measuring point, and every professional habit (checking before cutting, working in stages, learning fundamentals before specialising) reduces rework. Trends are raw material to be filtered, not rules to be obeyed.

## 8.1 Auditing and building a wardrobe
- **Principle**: Buy only what the existing wardrobe lacks.
- **How it works / sequence**: Take stock, group by type or colour, decide what stays, what is stored, and what is missing, then buy against a fixed budget.
- **Moves**: Empty the wardrobe onto a bed roughly once a month. Pair a simple cut with a strong colour and a complex cut with a neutral one. Prefer structured pieces (jackets, blazers) because they last several seasons. Try lightweight pieces with the layer that will go under them. Avoid duplicates: two bags and two pairs of shoes can serve three or more outfits. When sewing, choose the pattern first and the fabric second, and match the fabric to the pattern.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Fewer impulse purchases but less novelty.
- **Does not hold when**: The wardrobe has no usable base yet (a first capsule must be built, not audited).

## 8.2 Colour: palettes, temperature, texture
- **Principle**: One dominant colour, one supporting colour, neutrals as ground.
- **How it works / sequence**: Decide whether the garments are warm or cool, keep to one family, add a single accent, repeat that accent once in an accessory.
- **Moves**: Keep to two or three hues per outfit. Match warm with warm and cool with cool. Wear a colour that does not flatter the face lower down (trousers, skirt, shoes) rather than near the face. For tonal outfits, keep one colour family but vary materials (shiny/matte, smooth/rough). For colour blocking, use full but non-fluorescent colours, at most three, anchored by a neutral. To test a new colour, try the same model in the usual colour and two new ones in the fitting room. A coloured tight or sock can unify shoe, bag and skirt when it repeats one of them.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Safe palettes can look flat; accents add risk.
- **Does not hold when**: Complexion makes a specific neutral (beige, cream) dull near the face; in that case move it to the lower body.

## 8.3 Proportion, length and fit rules of thumb
- **Principle**: Balance volume: more volume on one half of the body, a slimmer line on the other.
- **How it works / sequence**: Choose the top length first, then the bottom width and the shoe.
- **Moves**: Cropped jeans should show just the ankle bone (for a polished look, about two fingers above the ankle); flared jeans graze the top of the foot; a gap of four to five fingers reads as an unintended capri. Trouser hems should cover the shoe without bunching. Avoid mid-calf lengths on long skirts. With a long skirt, a top ending near the navel keeps the figure in proportion (the two-thirds rule). A short coat reaching mid-thigh or just above the knee is the most flattering short length; check for at least 60 to 70 percent wool. Light-coloured skirts, dresses and swimwear in thin fabric need lining; a thicker satin shows less. For ceremony dresses, keep the knee covered and line satin and lace.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner for lengths and lining; Contested for how "oversized" is judged (many sources call it outdated, a few accept it with a slim counterpart).
- **Trade-off**: Defined waists suit many bodies but not all; belts can shorten a short torso.
- **Does not hold when**: Height, body shape or climate change the proportions: try garments on, and for very tall or short wearers adjust all lengths.

## 8.4 Accessories: belts, bags, jewellery, scarves, hats, shoes
- **Principle**: Accessories change an outfit more than the garments do.
- **How it works / sequence**: Fix the garments, then pick bag, shoes and belt as one decision.
- **Moves**: Belts: under 2 cm reads as slim and dressy; about 3 cm is the most versatile; 4 cm is already wide; 5 to 6.5 cm are wide belts to rest on the waist, not thread through loops. Choose a belt with at least five holes and try it on, since sizing varies. A contrast belt should reappear in bag or shoes. Bags: read the material description; coated imitation leather tends to crumble after one or two years. Enlarge photos to see whether a woven pattern is real or printed, and compare the stated size with a bag you own. Jewellery: one dominant metal, the other in small amounts; one strong element per zone; real stone feels cold and heavy, resin is light; heavy earrings can stretch the lobe. Scarves: wool or cashmere gives volume, silk goes limp; a tie should reach the belt. Hats: measure the head; a brim of about 6 cm is medium and 8 cm or more is wide. Shoes: low heels of 3 to 4 cm are the most wearable; wool insoles help against cold.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner for belts, bag materials and insoles; Contested for mixing metals (some sources: mix with one dominant; others: keep one metal).
- **Trade-off**: Real leather and fine materials cost more but last longer.
- **Does not hold when**: Budget rules out leather, in which case compare composition labels before paying for the look alone.

## 8.5 Trends and how to filter them
- **Principle**: Take the idea, leave the extreme detail.
- **How it works / sequence**: Start from a simple base, add one trend element, keep the rest plain.
- **Moves**: Use at most one vintage piece per outfit, with current fits for the rest. Break rich fabrics (jacquard, brocade) with a plain piece. Do not combine several prints. Strong trend colours and details date quickly; versions with a longer life are better value.- **Evidence**: Observational
- **Trade-off**: Safer, less distinctive looks.
- **Does not hold when**: The event or dress code asks for a specific look.

## 8.6 From sketch to finished garment; spec charts
- **Principle**: Make mistakes on test fabric, not on final cloth.
- **How it works / sequence**: Sketch, drape or draft in test fabric, cut, sew a full sample, fit, correct the pattern, repeat only if corrections are large, then cut the real fabric.
- **Moves**: In a specification chart, give every measurement a named measuring point and a unit. Lay the garment flat and align shoulder seams before measuring. Chest from armhole point to armhole point; give front and back neck drops separately. For knits check consistency: side length plus armhole depth should match front length within a small tolerance. For each sample record date, who measured and tolerances. For trousers, give waist as half-measure along the waistband, thigh about 1 in (2.5 cm) below the crotch point, and state where length starts.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Detailed charts take time but prevent supplier disputes.
- **Does not hold when**: Drapey, gathered or asymmetric garments, where fixed points must be agreed case by case.

## 8.7 Pattern files, printing and digital tools
- **Principle**: Protect digital files and verify scale.
- **Moves**: Download and save purchased files immediately; links expire. Check spam for confirmation emails. Large-format print removes the need to glue sheets, but printed instructions are separate. Always check print scale. In pattern software, place notches at 90 degrees to the edge, keep only essential points when tracing, and for checks set the repeat of the stripe and plaid before placing pieces.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Digital speed against learning the hand method first.
- **Does not hold when**: Software settings differ from what these steps assume.

## 8.8 Costing
- **Principle**: Cost is the sum of fabric, trims, labour and packaging, then a multiplier.
- **Moves**: Estimate fabric as price per metre times metres; add trims; allocate pattern, grading and cutting per garment; apply a multiplier of roughly two for profit and surprises (approximate, single source); the shop price is the wholesale price plus a retail mark-up.
- **Evidence**: Position
- **Trade-off**: Simple, but omits overhead and marketing.
- **Does not hold when**: One-off garments or very different volumes.

## 8.9 Career and working habits
- **Principle**: Experience and fundamentals first, brand second.
- **Moves**: Work in a tailor's or company before launching your own brand; choose roles by what you can learn. People tend to overestimate their own project; find a mentor. Years of practice do not guarantee competence; build a solid base block and adapt it. Keep a project notebook. Break large projects into stages. Check twice before cutting. Choose a course by goal (hobby, job, improvement) and keep fixed hours.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner for planning habits; Position for career advice.
- **Trade-off**: Slower start, fewer costly errors.
- **Does not hold when**: Formal experience is unavailable; self-study with feedback can substitute.

## 8.10 Running a small pattern or content business
- **Principle**: Release only what has been checked.
- **Moves**: Test every size (use an extended-size form for larger sizes) before release. Allow hours for product photography. Keep a steady publishing rhythm; long formats deepen, short ones attract. A useful review states size, fabric and trims.
- **Evidence**: Observational
- **Trade-off**: Delays against reputation.
- **Does not hold when**: Rapid prototypes are explicitly labelled as such.

## 8.11 Small sewn projects
- **Principle**: Consistent seam allowances give clean shapes.
- **Moves**: Use about 5 mm (1/4 in) allowance, trim to about 3 mm, leave a 2.5 to 5 cm opening, stuff until firm, close with invisible stitch. Cut on the bias for round forms. Cut embroidery thread to about elbow length.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Heavier fabric is harder to turn.
- **Does not hold when**: Leather, paper or felt need different needles and stitches.
