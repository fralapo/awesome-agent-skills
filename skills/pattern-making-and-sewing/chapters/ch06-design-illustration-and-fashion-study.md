# Chapter 6: Fashion Design, Illustration and Fashion Study

## Core Idea
A fashion illustration exists to communicate a garment idea, not to be a finished painting. Practitioners generally separate three jobs: the technical flat (a drawing a pattern maker can read), the presentation figure (colour, mood, fabric effect) and the study of history and trends that feeds both. Good work starts from a light pencil construction, simplifies the reference, and adds colour and texture in a controlled order. Trend reading and historical study are supporting disciplines: they improve choices, but they are not technique.

## 6.1 Drawing garments on a croquis (collars, jackets, hoodies)
- **Principle**: Work on an enlarged template figure (approximately 100% enlargement), constructed in pencil so errors can be corrected.
- **How it works / sequence**: Draw a base guide line parallel to the neck, slightly above it; then the collar or lapel on top; then closures and seams; erase hidden lines last.
- **Moves**:
  - Convertible shirt collar: a V at centre front that does not touch the guide line, two sides at the same angle and width, an inner line parallel to the neck.
  - Band collar and mandarin collar: neck line close to the neck, front lines straight (band) or curved (mandarin). Low round flat collar: low stand, steeply angled sides reaching mid-shoulder.
  - Notched lapel: deep V crossing at mid-chest, two equal sides reaching the edge, notch made by a horizontal exit then a vertical return.
  - Jacket opening is not at the body centre: the button line is drawn further across; the lapel belongs to the front piece, not a separate piece.
  - Hoodie: hood from head to mid-shoulder, rib at hem and cuff, zip as two lines with zigzag teeth, topstitching as dashed lines.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Pencil allows correction but is slower than a pen; a template figure speeds proportion but can make drawings look alike.
- **Does not hold when**: The garment is drawn from an unusual pose or for a loose, non-tailored shape; construction lines then differ.

## 6.2 Flats and technical drawing
- **Principle**: A flat communicates construction to a pattern maker; clarity matters more than artistry.
- **How it works / sequence**: Light construction lines first (half of the garment, length against the waist, shoulders, sleeve attachment), then confirm proportions (jacket length against sleeve length), then fine-liner and hatching for volume.
- **Moves**:
  - Memorise the base shape of each garment, then draw variants (different pocket, zip instead of buttons, other collar).
  - Outerwear is the hardest category: start from shoulders and length, then details.
  - For a double-breasted front, draw a construction line where the other side closes so collar and lapel match.
  - Oversized fit is often drawn as about one size up with dropped shoulder; align pockets to a button.
  - Perfect straight lines are not required in a sketch; they matter for a technical sheet.
  - A flat colour-washed for presentation takes much longer: use it only when needed.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: Speed (hundreds of styles per season) against polish.
- **Does not hold when**: A side or three-quarter view hides the garment; the hidden part must be guessed.

## 6.3 Setting up the fashion figure
- **Principle**: The face is secondary; cuts, proportions, fabrics and details carry the idea.
- **How it works / sequence**: Simple frontal pose in soft pencil, stylise the reference rather than copy it, then pen or fine-liner over cleaned pencil.
- **Moves**:
  - Keep a body base under the sheet so the figure is not redrawn each time.
  - Draw front and back to show construction.
  - Use a soft pencil (B) for sketching, a harder one (H or HB) when watercolour will follow, because soft graphite dissolves.
  - Correct with overlapping lines rather than early erasing.
  - Shoe or accessory sketches should take minutes, not hours.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: A stylised figure is quick but hides fit.
- **Does not hold when**: A client asks for a realistic or fit-focused illustration.

## 6.4 Colour media and paper
- **Principle**: Match the medium to the paper and keep the sketch fresh.
- **How it works / sequence**: Light base tones, then shadows, then highlights left as white paper, then fine-liner on key edges only.
- **Moves**:
  - Alcohol markers: use marker-specific paper; lay light then dark, re-pass with the light tone as blender; dry colour looks lighter; going over wet colour bleeds; blender lightens, so use sparingly; tones of similar value blend best.
  - Water-soluble markers: lay colour, dilute with a wet brush, let each layer dry (a hair dryer helps); use the brush tip for areas and the fine tip for details.
  - Liquid watercolour inks: leave a drop of colour at the point where the brush lifts to avoid join lines; work on heavy paper (approximately 300 g/m2); a grey base in the shadows before the garment colour gives quick shading.
  - Water-soluble pencils: pressure controls coverage; keep white paper for lights; a non-soluble skin base under water washes keeps it from moving.
  - Three tones (dark, medium, light) are usually enough.
  - Paper weights are approximate and vary: about 180 g/m2 hard-cover sketchbook for mixed media with little water; about 300 g/m2 (ideally cotton) for watercolour; about 75 g/m2 only for blendable markers.
- **Evidence**: Contested on exact paper weights (sources give 75, 100, 180, 200 and 300 g/m2 for different uses); Practitioner on the order of layering.
- **Trade-off**: Markers are fast and clean; watercolour is richer but needs pace and dry time.
- **Does not hold when**: The paper is too thin for water, or an ink is not water-resistant.

## 6.5 Rendering fabrics and patterns
- **Principle**: Suggest the texture with a few controlled marks instead of filling the surface.
- **How it works / sequence**: Light-to-dark base, irregular texture strokes mostly at edges, then highlights.
- **Moves**:
  - Bouclé tweed: irregular hatching, fringed edges, faint check, gel pen for loops and highlights.
  - Pied de poule: freehand square grid, one square in two coloured, grid following the body curve.
  - Fur: broken, dashed outline; short strokes following the hair direction; ragged edges.
  - Lace: stylise as many small flowers, built on a geometric scheme and enlarged, then reduced.
  - Transparency: light washes, thinner towards the hem.
  - Denim: contrasting brown topstitching. Drapery: light tone overall, darker in folds.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: More detail looks richer but flattens and takes time.
- **Does not hold when**: The illustration is tiny; patterns then disappear and must be simplified.

## 6.6 From a reference garment to a pattern
- **Principle**: Compare the technical drawing of a pattern with the garment, not a styled photograph.
- **How it works / sequence**: Identify the defining detail (neckline, sleeve, closure), choose a base pattern, then modify or combine two patterns.
- **Moves**:
  - Bodice changes are harder than skirt changes; add detail pieces (collar, tie, frill) separately.
  - Fabric changes the result more than cut; drape-dependent designs fail in stiff cloth.
  - Bias layouts need considerably more fabric.
  - Vintage sizing runs smaller than current sizing.
  - Capsule wardrobes use few patterns with compatible proportions; one pattern may give several garments.
- **Evidence**: Practitioner
- **Trade-off**: A fast approximation against an exact copy that rarely exists.
- **Does not hold when**: The garment hides key construction or the photo is low quality.

## 6.7 Trend reading and styling
- **Principle**: A trend persists across seasons and follows a curve (rise, peak, decline); a single brand proposal is not a trend.
- **How it works / sequence**: Observe many collections, count recurring elements, filter for the client and market.
- **Moves**:
  - Ready-to-wear is conceived about a year ahead.
  - Each brand picks its own shade of a trend colour.
  - At most two strong colours plus a neutral; darker layer outside.
  - Mix only related styles.
- **Evidence**: Observational; styling rules are Position (single practitioners' opinions).
- **Trade-off**: Following trends sells but dates quickly.
- **Does not hold when**: The client has an established style.

## 6.8 Study: tailoring, history, training
- **Principle**: History and craft study supply method and ideas but are cultural, not technical, material.
- **Moves**:
  - Bespoke tailoring: first pattern from measurements, usually 3-4 fittings (about 8-10 weeks for a new client), pattern archived for variants.
  - Hat blocking: soften felt or straw with steam, pull over a wooden block, pin, dry on the block.
  - Study route: hand sewing, paper patterns, costume history, collection books; judge schools on content and placements, not price alone.
  - Illustration and AI rendering: sketch by hand first; generated back views are invented and must be checked.
- **Evidence**: Observational
- **Trade-off**: Depth of study against time.
- **Does not hold when**: Exhibition dates and statistics quoted in reviews are unverified.

## Quick use
| Situation | Start with |
|---|---|
| Drawing a collar or lapel | Enlarged template, guide line, pencil, erase hidden lines |
| Jacket or coat flat | Shoulders, length, sleeve, then details |
| Colour with markers | Marker paper, light then dark, blend sparingly |
| Colour with water media | Heavy paper, dry each layer, keep white lights |
| Textured fabric | Irregular marks at edges, do not fill |
| Matching a pattern to a look | Compare technical drawings, add details separately |
| Reading a trend | Count recurrence across collections |
| Learning tailoring or millinery | Fittings, archived patterns, block and steam |
