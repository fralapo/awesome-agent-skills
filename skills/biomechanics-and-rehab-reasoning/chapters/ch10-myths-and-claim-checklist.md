# Chapter 10: Common Myths, Why They Persist, and a Checklist for Judging a Rehab Claim

> Educational, not medical advice. This chapter helps evaluate claims; it does not decide any individual's care. A person with pain, injury or worrying symptoms should see a qualified clinician.

## Core Idea
In this model most rehabilitation myths are **plausible simplifications** that were once reasonable, spread because they are simple, and survive because they sell (a fix for a problem you did not know you had). The remedy is a short habit of questions about the claim, the comparison and the mechanism, not a list of "right" answers.

## 10.1 Why myths persist
- **Thesis**: four forces keep myths alive.
- **Mechanism**: (1) **intuitive mechanics** (a machine analogy: wear, misalignment, weak link) is easy to picture; (2) **black-and-white framing** gets attention while "it depends" does not; (3) **commercial incentive**: a problem that does not exist can be sold a solution, and a catchy fix performs better online than a nuanced answer; (4) **authority and repetition**: a claim stated as fact by a trusted person is accepted without a source (a historical analogy: a once-universal belief about the centre of the universe).
- **Does not hold when**: a claim is supported by controlled evidence and a plausible mechanism; simplicity alone does not make something false.

## 10.2 Six recurring myths and the counter-arguments
For each: claim, why it appeals, what the evidence suggests, and the best objection.

1. **"The image shows the cause of the pain."** Appeals because a picture feels like proof. Evidence: findings are common in people with no pain, severity does not map to prognosis, fixing the image does not reliably fix the pain (ch08). Objection: imaging matters for serious or specific questions.
2. **"Never exercise through pain."** Appeals because pain feels like damage. Evidence: pain is protective and often occurs before or without damage (ch02); in one trial of acute hamstring strains, a group progressing only pain-free and a group allowed moderate pain returned in a similar time with similar re-injury at six months; waiting for zero pain can lead to doing less and less. Objection: some tissue problems (a fracture, an acute rupture) need protection; the aim is a tolerable range, not ignoring symptoms.
3. **"No pain, no gain."** Appeals through toughness culture. Evidence: pushing into significant pain can cause flare-ups that require rest, and repeating the cycle goes nowhere. Rehab is a balance between 2 and 3: you rarely need to avoid all symptoms and should not aim for pain as a goal; ups and downs are expected.
4. **"There is a perfect posture."** See ch09. Appeals because "good" posture seems visibly clear. Evidence: no consensus definition, weak links with pain, large natural variation. Objection: sustained positions can matter for specific tasks.
5. **"You can become bulletproof."** Appeals through marketing. Evidence: injuries are multifactorial and unpredictable; training reduces risk, it does not eliminate it; even people who claim immunity have pain episodes, which are a normal human experience.
6. **"Some exercises are universally bad."** Appeals through simple rules for social media. Evidence: no research establishes one exercise as inherently more dangerous; stress is not good or bad but the stimulus for adaptation; a "bad" exercise depends on sets, reps, load, range, frequency and the person. Knee stress in a leg extension versus a squat depends on knee angle and load; the question is dose and context, which cannot be judged from a label.
- **Does not hold when**: a movement is contraindicated for a specific medical reason after surgery or injury; individual restrictions are real.

## 10.3 More myths and unhelpful defaults
- **"Rest, ice, compression, elevation" as the only response**: replaced in some statements by protection plus loaded movement; see ch05 for the contested evidence on ice and anti-inflammatories.
- **"Three sets of ten" for everyone**: see ch05 (specificity, intensity, individual goal).
- **"Your core is weak / pelvis is out / legs are uneven"**: a structural label can damage confidence in a vulnerable person; the evidence for these labels as causes is weak (ch02, ch09).
- **"A scan is safer than no scan"**: unnecessary imaging has cost, delay, and potential fear (ch08).
- **"Static stretching always before sport"**: see ch05.
- **"Corrective exercise fixes the movement"**: see ch07 and ch09; hypotheses, not facts.
- **"Strong evidence for passive treatments because patients feel better"**: feeling better can come from care and expectation (ch08).
- **"Most shoulder pain heals just by hanging" and similar sweeping percentages**: a round figure ("95%", "100% healed") attributed to an authority or to a small study is a red flag; ask for the design, group size, follow-up and who was excluded (ch01), and note that hanging may suit some shoulders and aggravate others. Treat it as a hypothesis to try gently, not as a cure (ch09, 9.9).
- **"Injections or stretching alone fix rounded shoulders / neck tightness"**: both sides oversimplify; tightness is rarely the single cause of pain (ch09, 9.5), and a drug that relaxes a muscle does not retrain capacity.
- **"Crunches shorten the abs and ruin posture" or "only deep-core drills count"**: contested positions (ch09, 9.10); the spine moves through many ranges and most people tolerate flexion; judge by symptoms and by what the person can do, not by a universal ban.

## 10.4 A checklist for judging a rehab claim
Use the checklist in order; if a question cannot be answered, the claim is weaker.

1. **What exactly is claimed?** State it as one sentence with a cause, an effect and a population.
2. **Which kind of evidence supports it?** Mechanism only, a case series, a case-control comparison, a trial, a systematic review? Mechanism and association alone are the weakest (ch01).
3. **Is it association or causation?** Could pain have caused the finding? Is there a prospective design?
4. **What is the comparison?** Placebo or sham, no treatment, another treatment? What was the outcome and its size, not only its existence (ch01)?
5. **Is the finding common in people without the problem?** Ask for the base rate (ch08, ch09).
6. **What would change if it were true?** If nothing would change the plan, the claim has little practical value (the "decision threshold" idea in ch01).
7. **What is the active ingredient?** Specific effect, context, expectation, attention, or time? (ch08)
8. **Is the claim reversible and harmless?** What is the harm of being wrong (fear, avoidance, delay in care, cost)?
9. **What is the counter-argument?** State the best objection. If the claimant cannot, be cautious.
10. **Who gains if I believe it?** Commercial incentives do not make a claim false, but they warrant a closer look.
11. **Does it fit with load and capacity?** Is there a simpler explanation (too much too soon, too little, poor sleep, stress) (ch05)?
12. **Does it respect the limits of this skill?** It cannot diagnose or prescribe; if the question is individual, a clinician is needed.

## 10.5 What a good rehab claim looks like
- It states population, comparison and outcome, and it gives the uncertainty.
- It separates mechanism ("how load reaches tissue") from guarantee ("this will fix you").
- It is communicated without threat language and invites questions (ch02, ch03).
- It points to a qualified assessment for individual decisions.
