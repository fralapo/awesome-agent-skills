# Chapter 03: The Biopsychosocial Model, Red and Yellow Flags, Guideline Common Ground

> Educational, not medical advice. This chapter explains how clinicians frame problems and what screening is for. It does not tell any reader whether their own symptoms are serious; that needs a qualified clinician.

## Core Idea
In this model a musculoskeletal problem is understood on three levels at once: **biological** (tissues, nerves, load), **psychological** (beliefs, fear, mood, expectations) and **social** (work, family, finances, culture). The point of the frame is breadth: each level offers something to work with, and a narrow frame gives fewer options.

## 3.1 What the frame adds
- **Thesis**: the model is not "psychology instead of biology"; it adds levels so that two people with the same imaging or the same test result can be understood as different.
- **Mechanism**: history-taking widens to include what a person does, who they live with, sleep, what they think is going on, why they think they are in this situation, how favourable they expect the outcome to be. Questionnaires administered before the visit can screen beliefs and distress so that sensitive questions are not asked cold.
- **Trade-off**: a broader assessment takes more time, can seem overwhelming, and can drift into diagnosing what a clinician has no training to treat. Sleep, nutrition and mental health are touched on, not managed, by physical clinicians.
- **Does not hold when**: used as a ritual. Asking the questions without using the answers adds time and no benefit.

## 3.2 Distress, beliefs and clusters
- **Thesis**: in groups of people with persistent back pain, psychological profiles cluster, and the cluster with high distress, negative beliefs and low self-efficacy has higher pain, more disability and more life-stress history.
- **Mechanism**: the relationship runs both ways (pain causes distress, distress amplifies pain). Combined with a diagnosed mental health problem, effects on work absence multiply rather than add. Early life stress can set a long-term vulnerability.
- **Does not hold when**: the findings are group averages from specific samples; an individual's profile is assessed, not assumed.

## 3.3 Yellow flags
- **Thesis**: yellow flags are psychosocial factors that predict worse recovery (poor prognosis), as opposed to red flags (see 3.4) that suggest serious pathology.
- **Mechanism**: examples in the literature are: belief that pain and activity are harmful, avoidance or prolonged rest, low expectations of active participation, negative mood or anxiety, social withdrawal, overprotective or absent support, work problems, disputed claims or compensation, and high stress. Two patients with the same findings and the same acute course can differ sharply in risk of chronicity because of these. Later, work-related factors were split into "blue" and "black" flags.
- **Use**: they are a prompt to address modifiable factors early, with validated tools (a short stratification tool for back pain, a fear-of-movement scale, and similar).
- **Trade-off**: a flag is a risk marker, not a trait; labelling a person by flags can become another form of nocebo.
- **Does not hold when**: used to dismiss a physical problem as "psychosocial".

## 3.4 Red flags and screening
- **Thesis**: screening exists to recognise a **pattern** that suggests serious pathology (cancer, infection, fracture, serious neurological involvement, referred visceral pain) and to refer for medical assessment.
- **Mechanism**: a typical screen asks closed questions and looks across several domains: pain at rest or at night; movement-independent pain; fever, malaise, night sweats; unexplained weight loss; history of cancer; trauma and prolonged use of corticosteroids; widespread neurological change (sensation, strength, balance, vision, dizziness); region-specific problems (for example a nerve-compression syndrome in the lower back or a bone blood-supply problem at hip or shoulder). Organs can refer pain to the shoulder, back or other regions, so the question "is the pain related to movement?" matters: musculoskeletal pain can usually be linked, at least in part, to specific movements, even if constant.
- **Course**: a normal course is gradual decrease of pain and increase of activity; acute symptoms usually settle within about three months, while tissue remodelling takes longer (ligaments one to two years). An abnormal course prompts a search for negative prognostic factors (psychosocial, work, coping); if the abnormal course is unexplained, suspect serious pathology.
- **Key principle**: a single red flag rarely means much; patterns do. Screening never fully stops; response to treatment over time is information too.
- **Trade-off**: sensitivity vs specificity again (ch01): most red flags have low accuracy on their own, which is why they are read in clusters and with a low threshold for referral in doubt.
- **Does not hold when**: used by a layperson to decide whether symptoms are serious. A reader with worrying symptoms should see a qualified clinician.

## 3.5 Eleven areas of common ground in guidelines
- **Thesis**: across high-quality clinical practice guidelines for musculoskeletal pain, a consistent set of recommendations appears. Summarised:
  - care that is patient-centred with shared decisions and appropriate communication;
  - screening for serious pathology as the first step in direct-access settings;
  - assessment of psychosocial factors (yellow flags, mood, fear of movement, expectations);
  - discouraging routine imaging unless serious pathology is suspected, response to conservative care is poor, signs progress unexplained, or imaging is likely to change management (misreading by clinicians, misreading by patients, and radiation exposure are the three ways imaging can harm);
  - a physical assessment that includes neurological screening, mobility and strength, with the limits of special tests understood (ch01);
  - validated outcome measures to follow progress;
  - education, self-management and a clear prognosis (four questions a person usually has: what is wrong, how long will it take, what can I do, what can you do);
  - physical activity and exercise as first-line (maintaining normal activity, aerobic, strength, mobility, neuromuscular education);
  - manual therapy only as an adjunct, a "could do" and not a "should do";
  - non-surgical care and a period of conservative treatment before surgery;
  - facilitation of work continuation or return, since prolonged absence is a negative prognostic factor.
- **Mechanism**: the common thread is limited use of tests and invasive options, and a larger role for education, activity and psychosocial awareness.
- **Counter-argument (position)**: guidelines lag behind evidence, differ by country and condition, and averages hide subgroups; individual circumstances (a high-level athlete, a rare condition) may justify departing from them.
- **Does not hold when**: the person is outside the scope of the guideline.

## 3.6 Changing a belief, staged
- **Thesis (position)**: beliefs are changed with trust, not with information alone.
- **Mechanism**: first build a working relationship (sometimes by initially working within the person's structural frame), then explore what evidence would change the person's view, offer an explanation, and ask what they make of it. If non-verbal signs of resistance appear (arms crossed, looking away), step back; delivering the whole explanation too early can end the relationship. The first session may be devoted entirely to intake with no treatment, with expectations set beforehand.
- **Counter-argument**: some argue that delaying challenge allows harmful beliefs to continue; others that the relationship is itself the intervention. Both can be right in different people.
- **Does not hold when**: a belief is actively harmful and imminent (for example a person about to undergo an unnecessary procedure) which may justify earlier discussion.
