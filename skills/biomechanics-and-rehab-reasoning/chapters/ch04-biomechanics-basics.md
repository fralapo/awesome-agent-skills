# Chapter 04: Biomechanics Basics (Kinetics, Levers, Joints, Spine, Gait)

> Educational, not medical advice. This chapter gives the physics and anatomy vocabulary used to reason about movement. It does not assess any person's movement and does not prescribe exercises.

## Core Idea
In this model biomechanics is **biology plus physics**: tissues (bone, muscle, tendon, ligament, cartilage) are the biology; forces, torques and motion are the physics. It is a tool for explaining how load reaches tissue, not a source of verdicts about "good" or "bad" movement (see ch09 for that debate).

## 4.1 Branches
- **Thesis**: biomechanics divides into **statics** (no acceleration: rest or constant velocity) and **dynamics** (acceleration present); dynamics divides into **kinematics** (describing motion: positions, angles, velocities) and **kinetics** (the forces and torques that cause motion).
- **Mechanism**: living movement is almost always dynamic (jumping, landing, even trying to stand still involves tiny perturbations), so most reasoning is kinetic or kinematic.
- **Does not hold when**: a posture is held and slow enough to approximate statics; then static equations are a useful simplification.

## 4.2 Force, vectors and the free-body diagram
- **Thesis**: a force is a push or pull with a magnitude and a direction (a vector); force = mass x acceleration.
- **Mechanism**: a free-body diagram draws every force on one object (a ball struck by a racket, a body on the ground) as an arrow; the vector sum is the **net force**, which determines the change of motion. A force plate measures the **ground reaction force** (equal and opposite to what the body pushes into the ground); its length and direction change through a landing.
- **Trade-off**: clean diagrams hide the many tissues sharing a force; a net joint torque is not the force in any one muscle.
- **Does not hold when**: used for a single tissue's load without modelling muscle forces (they are usually much larger than the external load because of short muscle moment arms; general knowledge).

## 4.3 Torque, moment arm and levers
- **Thesis**: torque (rotational effect) = force x perpendicular distance from the axis (the **moment arm**). A joint rotates when net torque is not zero.
- **Mechanism**: the moment arm is measured at 90 degrees to the line of the force, and gravity always pulls straight down. So holding the same dumbbell with the arm extended in front produces a larger torque at the shoulder than holding it close to the body or overhead, because the horizontal distance changes while the arm length does not. The same logic applies to a barbell and the hip, knee and low back in a squat or deadlift: a bar further from a joint (horizontally) means a longer moment arm and a larger torque at that joint. Muscles must produce a counter-torque; a muscle also has its own (short) moment arm, which is why muscle forces are far larger than the weight lifted (general knowledge).
- **Levers (general knowledge)**: bone as rigid bar, joint as fulcrum, muscle force and load acting at different distances; the lever class tells whether the muscle gains force or range and speed.
- **Trade-off**: reducing a moment arm lowers torque at one joint but can raise it at another (in squats, bar position shifts the share between hip and knee; general knowledge).
- **Does not hold when**: the rigid-link model ignores soft-tissue deformation and co-contraction.

## 4.4 Joints
- **Thesis**: joints are classified by how freely they move; the freely movable ones are where most rehabilitation reasoning takes place.
- **Mechanism**: **fibrous** joints (very little movement), **cartilaginous** joints (little movement, for example the junction of a vertebra and its disc), and **synovial** (diarthrodial, freely movable) joints with four defining features: articular (hyaline) cartilage on the bone ends, a fibrous capsule (like a thick balloon), synovial fluid that lubricates, and bursae that reduce friction. Some capsule has slack (the shoulder has a downward recess that unfolds as the arm lifts), and a capsule can tear in a dislocation.
- **Trade-off**: more mobility means less intrinsic stability (see ch06): the same design rule appears in every region.
- **Does not hold when**: an individual has atypical anatomy; typical descriptions are averages.

## 4.5 The spine
- **Thesis**: the spine is a stack of motion segments, built for stiffness and efficiency rather than free motion.
- **Mechanism**: 24 movable vertebrae (7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar) over fused sacral and coccygeal segments (counts vary slightly). The **motion segment** is two adjacent vertebrae with the disc between them and the nerve roots leaving on each side. The primary curves (thoracic and sacral, convex backward, kyphotic) are present early; the secondary curves (cervical and lumbar, concave, lordotic) develop with lifting the head and with upright posture. Curves help absorb shock. The disc and facet joints influence the nerve root: a large disc protrusion or narrowing of the exit canal can irritate a root and produce sensory, motor or pain effects (see ch06 and ch08).
- **Counter-point (position)**: because the disc is a deformable, load-sensitive structure, some argue that the position of the spine under load matters for where stress concentrates (ch07); others hold that discs and vertebrae adapt to the loads placed on them (ch09). Both claims are supported by part of the evidence.
- **Does not hold when**: the question concerns an individual's symptoms; anatomy is not a diagnosis.

## 4.6 Gait
- **Thesis**: walking is a repeated cycle whose phases tell what each region must do: absorb shock, support, propel and swing.
- **Mechanism**: a gait cycle runs from one foot's contact to its next contact (one stride = two steps; about a second in healthy walking). About 60 to 62% is **stance** (foot on the ground) and the rest is **swing**. Stance has five events: initial contact (usually the heel), loading response (roughly 10 to 12% of the cycle; weight transfer and shock absorption by eccentric work of quadriceps and ankle dorsiflexors), mid-stance (limb stacked under the body), terminal stance, pre-swing. Terminology varies between textbooks (heel strike, foot flat, toe-off).
- **Link to the foot**: in propulsion, extension of the toes tightens the plantar fascia (windlass mechanism; ch06) and raises the arch.
- **Trade-off**: textbook angles are averages of healthy samples; healthy walking varies by person.
- **Does not hold when**: used as a definition of "normal" to diagnose an individual from gait alone.
