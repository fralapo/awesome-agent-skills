# Chapter 05: Load, Capacity, Healing and Tendons

> Educational, not medical advice. This chapter describes how tissues are thought to respond to load and how healing is staged. It gives no dose, no schedule and no exercise to treat any condition; those belong to an individual plan made with a qualified clinician.

## Core Idea
In this model many musculoskeletal problems are **mismatches between load and current capacity**. Tissues adapt to what is asked of them: too little load leads to deconditioning, too much too soon exceeds recovery, and a window in between drives adaptation. Pain monitors the system but does not equal damage (ch02), so load management and symptom management are related but different tasks.

## 5.1 The Goldilocks principle of load
- **Thesis**: there is a "too little", a "too much" and a "just right" amount of loading, and just right is a **range that moves**.
- **Mechanism**: lying in bed loses muscle; maximal training for many hours with no rest produces injury; the right volume and intensity builds capacity. Just right is individual and changes with training history, sleep, nutrition and stress. Many injuries are described as doing too much too soon: volume, frequency and intensity, in one session or over several, exceed current capacity to recover and adapt. Typical examples are a sudden spike in running mileage or a high-repetition daily challenge started from nothing.
- **Practice (principle)**: begin from an educated guess based on averages and on what suits the person's life, then adjust in small steps by response. "Searching for just right" is better than aiming for perfection.
- **Management in three moves (position)**: relative rest or modifying provoking activities, activity modification, graded loading. The aim of graded loading is to do a little more over time; the path is a rising line with ups and downs, not a smooth ramp.
- **Trade-off**: gradualism is slow; urgency (a competition date) tempts a jump.
- **Does not hold when**: the injury is a fracture or an acute event that needs protection first.

## 5.2 The envelope of function
- **Thesis**: for each tissue or joint there is a boundary, in the plane of **load** against **frequency**, within which tissue homeostasis is maintained; the boundary is individual and can shift.
- **Mechanism**: high loads are tolerated at low frequency, lower loads at high frequency. Below a minimum effective dose there is deconditioning or no adaptation; inside the envelope there is maintenance or adaptation; above it is a zone of supra-physiological overload, which disrupts homeostasis without instant structural failure; beyond that lies structural failure. After overload the envelope can **shrink**: activities once tolerated (stairs, ordinary walking) fall outside it, not because the activity changed but because tolerance did.
- **Use (principle)**: settle what the person is conditioned to tolerate and what they need to reach; reduce time in the overload zone; avoid complete rest (a recipe for further shrinking); keep doing tolerated activities; train between the minimum effective dose and the maximum tolerated dose so the envelope expands; specificity matters (to tolerate two hours of a sport, the tissue must eventually be exposed to something like that, usually starting with less volume); keep expanding after the target is reached so that a bad night's sleep or a longer session does not tip it back.
- **Trade-off**: the diagram is conceptual; the real curve is unknown and unmeasured.
- **Does not hold when**: used as a quantitative tool. Axes are illustrative; the numbers are not data.

## 5.3 Healing phases
- **Thesis**: soft tissue heals in overlapping phases whose timing depends on the tissue and its blood supply.
- **Mechanism**: (1) **inflammatory**, roughly the first 1 to 3 days: immune cells clear debris and nociceptors are sensitised, so touch hurts more; (2) **fibroblastic (proliferative)**, from about day 3 to about week 3: cells lay down collagen scaffolding and strength returns; this is when gentle loaded movement is thought to help (rest-only advice has been replaced by "loaded movement" in newer statements); (3) **remodelling**, from about 4 to 6 weeks out to years: tissue reorganises toward its former strength; ligaments, with poorer blood supply than muscle, can keep remodelling for a year or two.
- **Position**: current practice statements favour not suppressing early inflammation with ice and anti-inflammatory drugs, on the argument that they delay healing while providing only short-term pain relief. **Counter-argument**: the evidence that these measures delay healing in humans is limited and mixed (general knowledge); they can reduce pain, and the decision involves other factors such as medical history. This model holds the question open.
- **Pain-free is not fully healed (position)**: pain commonly resolves before remodelling ends; keeping some loading in place is argued to protect against relapse.
- **Trade-off**: timelines are group averages; grade of injury and location change them.
- **Does not hold when**: a serious structural injury needs surgery or immobilisation, or the pain has become persistent (then the tissue is usually not the main driver; ch02).

## 5.4 Types of injury by tissue
- **Thesis**: asking which tissue is the likely source guides which impairment to expect (weakness, instability, stiffness).
- **Mechanism**: **muscle strain**: typically in the belly of the muscle, graded 1 to 3 (grade 1: minimal functional loss, little pain, near-normal strength; grade 2: some torn fibres with swelling, weakness and loss of range, possible bruising; grade 3: major tear). **Ligament sprain**: joint instability is the main concern, graded similarly. **Cartilage injury**, **tendon problems** and **bone**: each differs in blood supply and healing.
- **Trade-off**: tissue labels are hypotheses; imaging often cannot confirm them (ch08).
- **Does not hold when**: pain is nociplastic (ch02), where the question "which tissue" is less relevant.

## 5.5 Tendons
- **Thesis**: persistent load-related tendon pain is no longer thought of as simple inflammation.
- **Mechanism**: the older label "tendonitis" assumed inflammation; microscopy found few inflammatory cells, and a degenerative label ("tendinosis") then took over; today an expert consensus prefers a neutral umbrella term for persistent tendon pain and reduced function related to mechanical loading, because degenerative changes are common in people without pain and cannot be reliably assessed without imaging that is not recommended routinely. Symptoms show a dose relationship with the **magnitude and rate** of loading: fast and elastic loads (jumping) hurt more than slow ones, and a single-leg task hurts more than a double-leg one. Some tendons show a **warm-up phenomenon** (pain eases once active). Symptoms can improve without any change in tendon structure on imaging.
- **Aim of rehab (principle)**: improve tolerance to forms of loading and restore the function of tendon, muscle and the whole limb. Pain is monitored during, immediately after, and the next day; the next-day response is the most informative about whether the current exposure was acceptable. Research protocols commonly use a "tolerable pain" ceiling, set differently by different clinicians. This skill does not set one.
- **Counter-argument (general knowledge)**: a tolerable-pain approach is common practice, comparative trials are limited, and some programmes prefer pain-free exercise.
- **Does not hold when**: the person has a sudden loss of function, a possible rupture, or atypical pain; those are assessments for a clinician.

## 5.6 Bone
- **Thesis**: bone responds to mechanical load like other tissue, and the stimulus must be strong enough.
- **Mechanism**: the older recommendation of walking and running is now considered a weak stimulus for increasing density; the stronger stimuli are heavy resistance training and impact activities such as hopping and jumping. For someone with low bone density, the appropriateness of an impact stimulus is a medical question.
- **Does not hold when**: used as advice for a person with a diagnosed bone condition; that belongs to their clinician.

## 5.7 Static versus dynamic stretching before activity
- **Thesis**: held stretches before power tasks tend to lower power for a short period; dynamic movements through range do not, and may help.
- **Mechanism**: in several experiments, a held calf or hamstring stretch before jumping or speed tasks reduced jump height, speed, torque or muscle activation, while dynamic stretching improved or preserved them; range of motion gains were similar between the two. Held stretches are therefore better placed away from the performance moment.
- **Trade-off**: studies are small, on specific populations and short effects.
- **Does not hold when**: the goal is long-term flexibility (separate question, ch09) or the sport needs no power.

## 5.8 Specificity and intensity in exercise
- **Thesis**: the "three sets of ten, three days a week" default is better than nothing, but specificity matters; intensity is the most commonly missing variable.
- **Mechanism**: for muscle size, **total volume** is reported to matter more than the repetition range; EMG signal is lower with light loads, but EMG is not growth; near failure, average hypertrophy is reported to be similar from about 30% to about 85% of the maximum (as reported). Intensity can be gauged by repetitions in reserve or perceived exertion; these adapt day by day to stress, sleep and discomfort, unlike a fixed percentage. Some clinicians use pain-monitoring models to guide progression; this skill does not set thresholds. Chronic **underloading** is proposed to explain some poor outcomes in patients with simple problems.
- **Trade-off**: tolerance, risk and psychology make people shy away from higher intensities.
- **Does not hold when**: the person does not need a tailored plan at all. Dose is a topic for `exercise-science-and-programming`; this skill does not give any.

## 5.9 Returning to a sport (principles)
- **Thesis**: return to sport is a continuum of exposures, not a date.
- **Mechanism**: progress along three axes: from double-leg to single-leg, from slow to fast and from lower to higher intensity, and from one direction to many (vertical, horizontal, lateral, multi-plane). Joint contributions differ by task: in a vertical jump the hip, knee and ankle each contribute about a third to take-off and landing work; in a horizontal jump the hip and ankle drive and the knee absorbs the landing. Landing and hopping tasks are therefore not interchangeable between injuries.
- **Trade-off**: more criteria give more safety and more delay.
- **Does not hold when**: used as a protocol. It is a framework; the timeline depends on injury, surgery, symptoms and sport.

## 5.10 Modifying exercises around pain (concept)
- **Thesis (position)**: rehab is training in the presence of injury, so the two sit on one continuum; the variables of an exercise can be altered to find an entry point without gimmicks.
- **Mechanism**: variables include load, repetitions (lighter load with more repetitions keeps effort), range of motion, tempo, stance or support, and the exercise chosen. Tissue growth can happen across many repetition ranges when effort is sufficient.
- **Counter-argument (general knowledge)**: modifications can become a way to avoid challenging a fear (ch03).
- **Does not hold when**: the pain pattern is unexplained or worsening; see ch03 on red flags.
