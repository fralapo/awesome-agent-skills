# Chapter 07: Lifting Biomechanics (Squat, Deadlift, Depth, Spine, Mobility vs Stability)

> Educational, not medical advice. This chapter explains mechanical reasoning used around squats and deadlifts and records where practitioners disagree. It does not give technique instructions for treating any condition, and it does not replace coaching or an assessment.

## Core Idea
In this model "form" is a set of **mechanical trade-offs** (where load acts, how long the lever is, where tissue stress concentrates), not a single correct shape. Two broad positions exist: one holds that movement quality under load is central to risk and performance (ch07 here); the other that tissues adapt to what they are exposed to and that apparent faults often do not matter (ch09). Read both before judging a lifting cue.

## 7.1 The squat as a movement before an exercise
- **Thesis (position)**: the deep squat is a basic human movement as well as a loaded lift, so being able to do it unloaded is a screening observation.
- **Mechanism**: a deep bodyweight squat needs foot and ankle mobility, hip mobility, and trunk and pelvic control. If it is missing, something may be limiting, though one person's anatomy (very deep hip sockets, for example) can cap the depth at roughly parallel and no amount of mobility work changes it. Mobility not used is lost.
- **Counter-argument (general knowledge)**: a squat limitation is not a diagnosis; most unloaded movement screens have unclear predictive value for pain or injury (ch09), and populations who squat all day have other loading patterns.
- **Does not hold when**: a sport requires partial depth (some strength sports), or the person has pain on deep flexion.

## 7.2 Depth
- **Thesis (position)**: train as deep as you can control without breakdown or pain, if the sport rewards depth; do not push depth for its own sake.
- **Mechanism**: in weightlifting the lowest catch position of a snatch or clean must be stable, so the bottom of the squat is trained deliberately; stronger at depth means more load that can be caught. Specificity: the range trained is the range strengthened. A team-sport athlete who can reach full depth with control gains from training the whole range. The common counter-claim that elite lifters seldom train to full depth in the gym (a pattern reported in some training halls) is acknowledged: they train depth with lighter loads or use depth only in the competition lifts.
- **Counter-argument**: for jumping or sprinting power some people train partial ranges for specificity; partial-range training is reported to have raised a vertical jump in one study cited. Depth is a goal-dependent choice.
- **Trade-off**: depth increases range of motion and often reduces maximal load.
- **Does not hold when**: pain occurs in the range; then the position is "modify and look at why", not "push through" (ch05, ch10).

## 7.3 Squat mechanics: the bar over the middle of the foot
- **Thesis**: balance requires the combined centre of mass (body plus bar) over the middle of the foot.
- **Mechanism**: the bar's line of gravity creates moment arms at the knee, hip and low back (ch04). Moving the bar lower on the back (a low-bar position) makes the trunk lean more and shifts torque towards the hip; a front-loaded bar keeps the trunk more upright and raises knee demand (general knowledge). A front squat feels harder with the same weight partly because of upper back and bracing demands.
- **Trade-off**: reducing torque at one joint raises it at another; there is no free lunch.
- **Does not hold when**: used to say that one variation is "better for the knees" in general; the effect depends on the individual and the load.

## 7.4 Butt wink
- **Thesis**: butt wink is a posterior pelvic tilt at the bottom of a squat, usually reflecting the hip reaching its end range.
- **Mechanism**: past roughly parallel, the femurs cannot flex further in some people (bone shape and the way the socket is oriented). To go lower, the pelvis rolls under, so the lumbar spine moves from its lordotic curve into flexion. The disc theory is that, under heavy load, flexion at one or two lower segments concentrates stress and drives the nucleus backward.
- **Position and counter-position**: (a) a bit of butt wink in an unloaded squat is not a concern for most healthy people, since disc damage from unloaded squats with pelvic tilt has not been directly studied; (b) with a heavy barbell the forces are larger and "excessive" tilt carries more risk, but what counts as excessive is a subjective call; (c) some argue (general knowledge) that the lumbar flexion measured in squats is not what harms discs and that repetitive, load-sensitive adaptation is the key variable; (d) experienced lifters frequently show a small amount without reported injury.
- **Does not hold when**: a person has a history of back pain: then even bodyweight depth may be reduced for a while (a decision for a clinician).

## 7.5 The loaded spine: neutral zone and stiffness
- **Thesis (position)**: under high load, a stiff, controlled trunk transmits force efficiently and spreads stress; losing that stiffness lets the spine "bleed" force and migrate stress into tissues.
- **Mechanism**: a joint's neutral zone is the range around its resting position with almost no resistance; beyond it, ligaments and other structures take over. The spine has a disc, not a ball joint, so its neutral zone is **a variable**: it widens with repeated motion (a few cycles soften the tissue), shrinks under compression as the disc stiffens, and can approach zero shortly before failure. The neutral zone is also larger after a night in bed because discs swell overnight, which is why bending stresses are higher soon after waking; a study in people with chronic low back pain reported that limiting spinal flexion in the early morning reduced pain (small evidence base; population-specific).
- **Flexion and extension in the deadlift**: a "curved but locked" back distributes load; a spine that is crushed into flexion under the load is different. Many powerlifters pull with a slightly rounded, stiff upper back and maintain tension; a posterior pelvic tilt is also visible in some elite lifters who do not report injuries. The model distinguishes a **gentle, stiff curve** from **motion at one or two segments**.
- **Disc as adaptable fabric (position)**: repeated flexion and extension can loosen the fibre bundles that contain the nucleus; for a gymnast or golfer, a looser matrix is an adaptation for range; for a strength athlete, stiffer collagen gives resilience. Adaptation may be good or bad depending on goal.
- **Counter-argument (general knowledge)**: the group-level evidence that lifting with a rounded spine causes disc injury in trained people is limited; tissues adapt to gradually increased load (ch09), and some research shows lumbar flexion at heavy loads in healthy lifters without symptoms. Case examples of elite lifters, though striking, are survivorship stories.
- **Does not hold when**: loads are light relative to capacity; most movements are harmless then.

## 7.6 Perceived stability and force
- **Thesis (position)**: strength is partly neural, and the nervous system limits output if it perceives instability.
- **Mechanism**: when a spinal segment or joint is perceived as unstable, the brain reduces drive (as an unstable knee causes limping); a failed rep in a heavy squat is often preceded by small wobbles and hips sliding out. Stiffening and bracing may raise perceived stability and allow higher drive.
- **Trade-off**: bracing raises pressure and effort and may not suit every person (blood pressure, breathing).
- **Does not hold when**: the failure is purely metabolic or technical.

## 7.7 Deadlift set-up as leverage
- **Thesis**: three alignments reduce horizontal bar travel and wasted torque: bar over the middle of the foot, bar close enough to touch the shins, and arms vertical so the bar stays close.
- **Mechanism**: a bar in front of the midfoot must travel back toward the body, adding horizontal displacement; a bar far from the shins puts the lifter out of balance. A vertical arm position matters most with a mixed grip: otherwise the bar drifts away on the underhand side and twists the body.
- **Does not hold when**: long-limbed lifters need small adjustments; cues such as "shoulders over the bar" produce more variation than a fixed anatomical landmark (position).

## 7.8 Mobility versus stability
- **Thesis (position)**: movement problems are looked at as combinations of mobility limits (joint range), flexibility (tissue extensibility) and stability or control; many pains are attributed to a weak link in the chain.
- **Mechanism**: if one joint lacks range (for example the ankle), another (the knee or hip) may take extra, the kneecap tracks less well, and stress concentrates. One framework recommends testing the pain-free side first, tracking movement under load, and then changing one variable at a time.
- **Examples used (position)**: limited internal rotation of the shoulder forces the bar to loop away in a snatch; a tug of war between a short chest muscle and a weak lower trapezius is suggested to tilt the shoulder blade forward; a hip flexor strain presents with pain on resisted flexion.
- **Counter-argument (general knowledge)**: (a) most "weak links" are found in people without pain (no one is symmetrical); (b) the evidence that correcting a movement pattern removes pain is mixed and many exercise-induced changes are short-lived; (c) improvements often occur from any plausible training and reassurance (ch02); (d) "retraining movement, not muscles" is a coherent hypothesis, but strength training of the muscle often works as well. This model reports the position and the objection and does not choose.
- **Does not hold when**: used to label every movement difference a dysfunction.

## 7.9 Movement-based classification of back pain (a position)
- **Thesis (position)**: some clinicians classify back pain by which movement provokes it (for example "flexion intolerance" if the bottom of the squat or rounding triggers pain) and then modify depth or exposure.
- **Mechanism**: modify the range at which pain appears, watch if symptoms drop, and use that to guide temporary adjustments (for example a box to limit depth for a period).
- **Counter-argument (general knowledge)**: provocation tests are not diagnostic (ch01), and the explanation "the movement itself is harmful" can feed fear (ch02, ch03). This skill treats such classification as one reasoning aid.
