# Chapter 08: Imaging and Passive Modalities: What They Are Worth

> Educational, not medical advice. This chapter explains how to weigh a scan result or a passive treatment as evidence. It does not interpret anyone's scan and does not say whether anyone should or should not have imaging or a treatment; that decision belongs to the person and their clinician.

## Core Idea
In this model an image is **a picture, not a pain scan**. It shows structure; pain is an output of the nervous system (ch02). Because structural findings are very common in people with no pain, an image has diagnostic value only when it is interpreted against a specific, prior clinical question. The same reasoning applies to passive treatments: the question is what the active ingredient is.

## 8.1 Why findings are not causes
- **Thesis**: abnormal findings are common without symptoms and often reflect normal age-related change, like grey hair or wrinkles.
- **Mechanism**: in groups of adults with **no** back pain, a large share show disc degeneration on imaging (in one review, around half of people in their thirties and roughly two thirds in their forties; exact ages and percentages as reported, treat as approximate) and about a third show a disc protrusion. In knees of uninjured adults (a study of about a hundred, median age mid-forties) nearly all showed at least one abnormality and about 30% a meniscal tear. In one-sided shoulder pain, most abnormal findings were as common in the painless shoulder as in the painful one. In people over about forty without shoulder symptoms, partial-thickness cuff tears were reported with a prevalence that increases with age (age-band dependent, dated; single-study figures, not systematic estimates).
- **Consequence**: when searching for a link between a finding and symptoms, a link will usually be found. After an accident, a finding on a scan may have been there beforehand.
- **Trade-off**: the finding can still be relevant; absence of a clear signal does not mean the pain is not real.
- **Does not hold when**: findings are acute and consistent with a known trauma (a fracture), or are specific and match a focal clinical picture.

## 8.2 Reading differs between readers
- **Thesis**: reports from different readers differ substantially, even for the same patient.
- **Mechanism**: in a study in which one patient had ten scans in different centres over three weeks, the reports together listed about fifty different findings and none was common to all. A report written by a different person can say something different.
- **Does not hold when**: a single clear finding (a large tumour, an obvious fracture) is evident to all readers.

## 8.3 Image severity does not predict outcome
- **Thesis**: a more dramatic image does not mean a worse prognosis.
- **Mechanism**: a larger disc herniation or greater nerve compression does not automatically mean worse recovery (the largest, sequestered ones often resorb; ch06). In osteoarthritis, symptoms do not follow the amount of radiographic change: a person can have large spurs and minimal pain, or the reverse. Labels such as "bone on bone" or "the worst I have seen" are heard by people as threat (ch02) and may do more harm than the image.
- **Counter-argument (position)**: some clinicians like to see the image and find it interesting, but report it does not change the plan when the movement-based assessment is good; others argue that explaining the image honestly can reassure.
- **Does not hold when**: progressive neurological deficit, a suspected fracture, a mass or an infection.

## 8.4 Fixing the image does not fix the pain
- **Thesis**: removing or repairing an abnormality does not reliably remove symptoms.
- **Mechanism**: a systematic review of arthroscopic surgery for degenerative meniscal tears found little or no clinically important benefit for pain or function compared with placebo surgery or non-surgical management; older trials comparing surgery, therapy and a collar for neck-arm pain pointed the same way for some outcomes. Placebo surgery is reported to match real surgery for a range of conditions.
- **Counter-argument (general knowledge)**: for some indications (a locked joint, a displaced fracture, progressive neurological loss) surgery is needed; and averages hide responders.
- **Does not hold when**: the abnormality is the cause and a procedure has proven benefit.

## 8.5 When imaging is worth getting
- **Thesis**: imaging is a tool for specific, pre-formed questions.
- **Mechanism**: it is appropriate when a clinician, after history and examination, suspects something serious (cancer, infection, a fracture after trauma, compression of the nerve bundle at the base of the spine, severe or progressive neurological deficit) or when imaging is likely to change the plan. In practice a small minority of people with back pain (about 5% in one framing) need it. Routine imaging in uncomplicated low back pain is discouraged in guidelines (ch03).
- **Three harms**: misinterpretation by the clinician, misinterpretation by the patient, and radiation exposure. In addition, one study reported worse outcomes in people who were scanned than in people who were not (association; unclear in source whether causal), and scary wording leads people to avoid movement.
- **Counter-argument (general knowledge)**: a scan can reassure when serious pathology is excluded; the harm arises when it is routine and results are explained badly.
- **Does not hold when**: red flags exist (ch03).

## 8.6 Therapeutic ultrasound as a case of a passive modality
- **Thesis**: a treatment can have no specific physical effect and still have value through context; the issue is dependency and opportunity cost.
- **Mechanism**: several national and international guidelines advise against therapeutic ultrasound as a main treatment for low back pain or osteoarthritis, citing insufficient evidence, and advise preferring active approaches (exercise, weight management) over passive ones. Trials that compare real with sham devices (switched off but looking real) commonly show no difference, which suggests that the benefit is not from the waves.
- **Counter-argument (position)**: patients often report the experience as soothing and attentive; placebo and care effects are real and can be useful; there is no objection to a person choosing it. The objection is to a long course of passive sessions that builds reliance on a clinician and displaces active self-management. Some patients use a device at home, which removes the dependency.
- **Does not hold when**: the question is whether the physical effect exists: sham comparisons are the test of that, and they are mostly negative.

## 8.7 Manual and other passive therapies (general knowledge, brief)
- **Thesis**: guidelines classify manual therapy as an adjunct ("could do"), not a core ("should do") element (ch03); the same logic of asking for the active ingredient applies.
- **Does not hold when**: a person values it and uses it alongside active care; this is a legitimate choice.
