# Chapter 06: Regional Functional Anatomy (Shoulder, Spine, Hip, Knee, Foot, Hand, Calf)

> Educational, not medical advice. Anatomy is used here as a mechanism: why a structure is built as it is and what that predicts. It is not a way to identify the cause of any person's pain.

## Core Idea
In this model every region shows the same trade-off: **mobility costs stability**, and the structure that buys back stability (a lip of cartilage, a ligament, a muscle group acting as a unit) predicts where failures occur. A name or a text-book action of a muscle is a starting hypothesis, not a measurement of what it does in life.

## 6.1 Shoulder: mobility first
- **Thesis**: the glenohumeral joint is better described as a ball on a saucer than a ball in a socket, which explains both its range and its frequent dislocation.
- **Mechanism**: only about one third of the humeral head is in contact with the glenoid at a time. A fibrocartilage rim (the labrum) deepens the surface a little. Compare the hip, where the socket covers more of the head: more congruent, less mobile, fewer dislocations. Most dislocations are anterior: the common mechanism is abduction with external rotation, which levers the head forward; more range is available in front of the body, and the anterior restraints are the structures most often stressed. After a dislocation, imaging to rule out bony injury or a non-reduced joint is standard medical practice, not something to decide alone.
- **Trade-off**: large range of motion needed for sport and daily tasks comes with a shallow joint.
- **Does not hold when**: connective tissue is lax in general or the dislocation is traumatic; the mechanisms differ.

## 6.2 Shoulder: abduction and the rotator cuff
- **Thesis (position, supported by electromyography studies)**: one tendon does not "start" abduction; many muscles activate together, and the rotator cuff's main job is to **centre and slightly depress the humeral head** so the deltoid can lift.
- **Mechanism**: the deltoid inserts far down the humerus, a long lever. Acting alone it would drive the head up into the acromion. The rotator cuff muscles insert lower on the tuberosities and together pull the head down and in, creating space for the arm to rise. A study of onset timing found the top cuff tendon's muscle did not fire before the others; the deltoid and the other cuff muscles started around the same time. A confirming natural experiment is the reverse shoulder replacement: when the cuff cannot work, surgeons reverse ball and socket so that the deltoid can abduct without needing a head-depressing force.
- **Counter-argument (general knowledge)**: electromyography shows activation, not force; the idea that a particular muscle initiates is not wrong in every task, and onset times change with load and speed.
- **Does not hold when**: used to claim a specific muscle is "the" cause of a painful arc; many structures share the mechanical task.

## 6.3 Biceps: more than an elbow flexor
- **Thesis**: the two-headed arm muscle is a forearm supinator as much as an elbow flexor, because it inserts on the radius.
- **Mechanism**: elbow flexion has three contributors; the deep muscle beneath it is the most powerful flexor. The biceps inserts on the radial tuberosity, so when it contracts it rotates the radius into supination. After a rupture of the distal tendon, supination power falls more (reported figures: up to about half or more) than elbow flexion power (about a third), because the other flexors are intact. Figures differ between studies.
- **Does not hold when**: the question is total elbow strength; the biceps is then secondary.

## 6.4 Spine: stabilisers and the disc
- **Thesis**: the spine is stabilised by ligaments that bind each level and by muscle acting as a **single functional unit**, not as isolated parts.
- **Mechanism**: the lumbar and thoracic extensors (three columns of muscle, from lateral to medial) work together to extend the spine when both sides contract and side-flex it when one side does. Ligaments: the anterior longitudinal ligament (thick, binds vertebral bodies and discs and resists extension), the posterior longitudinal ligament (thin, behind the bodies), supraspinous and interspinous ligaments (posterior stability), intertransverse ligaments (lateral stability), and the ligamenta flava (between laminae).
- **Disc "herniation" stages**: bulge (annulus intact, common, often without symptoms), protrusion (focal push of the nucleus, annulus holding), extrusion (annulus torn, nucleus escapes while connected), sequestration (a fragment separates). "Slipped disc" is a misnomer: the disc is fused to the vertebrae above and below and does not slip. Most herniations go posterolateral, where the posterior longitudinal ligament is thinner, near a nerve root; direct central herniation is rarer.
- **Natural history (position, widely held)**: most discs do not progress beyond a bulge or protrusion, and discs can shrink and settle over weeks to months; a sequestered fragment, exposed to blood supply, is often resorbed by an immune response, so the worst-looking image may have among the best recoveries.
- **Trade-off**: reassurance can be undermined by a person's fear if the structural language ("herniation") is used without context (ch02, ch08).
- **Does not hold when**: nerve-root signs worsen, bladder or bowel control changes, or weakness progresses; those are matters for urgent medical review (ch03).

## 6.5 Hip: flexors and blood supply
- **Thesis**: the front of the hip is served by several muscles that also relate to the lumbar spine, and the femoral head's blood supply is fragile.
- **Mechanism**: the four hip flexors are the deep lumbar muscle, the iliac muscle (together the iliopsoas, inserting on the lesser trochanter), the straight head of the quadriceps, and the sartorius. The deep lumbar muscle originates on the lumbar vertebrae and discs, so it also affects side-flexion of the trunk and stabilises the front of the joint. About 90% of the blood supply of the femoral head and neck comes from a ring formed by two circumflex branches of the deep femoral artery; the rest from a small ligament in the head. A fracture of the femoral neck can separate head from supply, and loss of supply leads to bone death (avascular necrosis). Risk factors named in clinical teaching include repeated high-dose corticosteroids, trauma, and heavy episodic alcohol use, but this skill does not assess risk for any person.
- **Trade-off**: an anatomical mnemonic helps memory but encourages treating muscles as separate when they work as a system.
- **Does not hold when**: the question is about pain in the groin; many conditions overlap.

## 6.6 Knee: ligaments as restraints
- **Thesis**: each ligament resists a direction; the direction of injury forces predicts the injury.
- **Mechanism**: the medial collateral ligament resists valgus (and is attached to the medial meniscus, which is why severe medial injuries can involve the meniscus); the lateral collateral ligament (to the fibula head) resists varus; the anterolateral ligament (to the tibia) adds control of internal tibial rotation and can be injured with a cruciate injury (sometimes shown as a small avulsion fracture at the lateral tibial rim). The anterior cruciate ligament runs from the back of the femur to the front of the tibia and resists forward translation of the tibia; it is typically injured in non-contact cutting and pivoting, and because it is well supplied with blood, swelling is rapid. The posterior cruciate ligament resists backward translation.
- **Trade-off**: ligaments are often injured in combination.
- **Does not hold when**: the person has major instability after trauma; that is a medical assessment (ch03).

## 6.7 Foot: the windlass mechanism
- **Thesis**: the plantar fascia is a cable under the arch whose tension rises as the toes extend, helping to raise the arch in propulsion.
- **Mechanism**: the plantar fascia runs from the heel bone to the toes (it attaches to plates at the metatarsal heads). The medial longitudinal arch is formed by the heel bone, the talus, the navicular, three cuneiforms and the first three metatarsals. Body weight through the shin tends to flatten the arch. As the big toe extends at push-off, the fascia is wound around the metatarsal heads, shortens the distance between heel and forefoot and lifts the arch, much as a card pushed from both ends buckles upward.
- **Counter-argument (general knowledge)**: the arch is also held by muscles and ligaments, and it is not clear that arch height as such predicts symptoms (ch09).
- **Does not hold when**: used to explain individual foot pain without assessment.

## 6.8 Hand: two finger flexors
- **Thesis**: two deep and superficial finger flexors differ by where they insert, and that explains different roles.
- **Mechanism**: the deep flexor inserts on the end of the finger bones (fingers 2 to 5) and can bend the end joint; the superficial flexor has a tendon that splits to let the deep tendon through and inserts on the middle bones, so it bends the middle and knuckle joints but not the end joint.
- **Does not hold when**: used for isolating a muscle clinically; many hand tests overlap.

## 6.9 Calf: why one muscle is injured often
- **Thesis**: the large calf muscle crosses two joints, contains many fast fibres and fatigues easily, and is loaded in fast explosive tasks.
- **Mechanism**: it has two heads from the back of the femoral condyles, inserts on the heel bone through the calcaneal tendon, and plantarflexes the ankle. It is often cited as one of the commonest muscle injuries in football (about one in eight in one report). Injuries cluster at the medial head, which is more active than the lateral head, especially when the foot is turned out. Gradual overload (a sudden jump in running volume) and sudden explosive loads are two routes.
- **Does not hold when**: used as a reason to avoid explosive loading; exposure is also how tissue gets conditioned (ch05).
