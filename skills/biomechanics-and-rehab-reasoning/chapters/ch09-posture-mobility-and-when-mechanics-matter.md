# Chapter 09: Posture, Mobility, Feet, and When Biomechanics Matters

> Educational, not medical advice. This chapter weighs claims about posture, flexibility, foot shape and movement quality. It does not assess a person's posture and does not recommend corrective exercises for a condition.

## Core Idea
In this model the contested question is not "does mechanics matter?" (nobody argues it does not) but **"which mechanical features predict pain, and do changes to them change outcomes?"** The evidence for most static features (curves, tilts, asymmetries, arch height) is weak; the evidence for load exceeding capacity is stronger (ch05). Both sides of the debate are laid out because the field is not settled.

## 9.1 Posture and pain: the evidence against
- **Thesis (position, majority of group-level studies)**: most postural features show no association with pain.
- **Mechanism**: studies report no association with: a leg-length difference, the degree of lumbar lordosis, neck curvature, pelvic tilt, length of abdominal, hamstring and hip flexor muscles, asymmetry, thoracic kyphosis or lordosis in teenagers and later back pain, and lordosis in pregnancy and back pain. A systematic review of about fifty studies concluded that an association between sagittal spinal curves and spinal pain is not supported. Correlation would not prove cause anyway: people in pain may adopt a posture because of the pain.
- **Why the intuitive story is weak**: (1) **tissues adapt**: bodies are not machines that wear out; joints, tendons, discs and muscles adapt to their loads, and weightlifters withstand large flexed positions gradually; (2) **damage does not equal pain**: tissue findings are common without pain (ch08), so the "accumulated micro-damage" story is incomplete; (3) **natural variation**: asymmetry is the norm; an ideal posture does not exist, and a pattern called dysfunctional in one person may be fine in another (elite athletes with strong compensations show this).
- **The nocebo concern**: telling someone about postural flaws creates worry and vigilance; patients who sit rigidly straight, afraid to slouch, are reported to be as common as those with "bad" posture; a more plausible issue is **staying in one posture too long and lacking variation**.
- **Counter-argument (position)**: (a) posture is dynamic and snapshot photographs mislead (a snapshot cannot predict how someone moves or feels); (b) (general knowledge) sustained loads in specific tasks (a prolonged awkward position at work) can matter, and "posture matters sometimes" is consistent with the evidence; (c) people have a rough idea of "good posture" but do not agree on definitions, so debates often fail (for example about anterior pelvic tilt); (d) individuals with symptoms in specific postures may genuinely feel better when they change position, which is an observation about that task and not a claim about structure.
- **Does not hold when**: a specific posture reliably provokes a symptom; then changing the exposure is reasonable (ch05).

## 9.2 When biomechanics does not matter (a framework from the biomechanics side)
- **Thesis (position)**: biomechanical "dysfunctions" found in case-control studies are numerous, limited mainly by the measurement equipment of the lab, and rarely changed clinical practice.
- **Mechanism**: comparing a group with pain and a group without pain for any measurable variable always finds differences (muscle timing, tightness, range, weakness, stability, scapular and pelvic patterns, breathing, "force closure"). The differences can be consequences of pain (motor-control changes are common in persistent pain), so they are likely **secondary** rather than causal, which differs from earlier assumptions. Open question: which biomechanical finding of recent decades clearly changed practice and outcomes?
- **Reductio**: if sitting were dangerous by biomechanical theory, the commonly quoted bracing routines would be impossible to maintain; an exaggerated list of how to "protect the spine" while sitting illustrates the theory's implausibility.
- **Counter-argument (position)**: movement-based clinicians say: if mechanics did not matter, a clinician would only be talking about pain; the profession's task is to find which movements, postures and loads trigger a person's pain and change them, and to build capacity. They regard some biomechanical claims as exaggerated while keeping that load and movement are the means to change pain. Both sides agree that **load matters**; they differ on whether **form** can be judged from a look.
- **Reconciliation**: nobody claims biology is unimportant; biomechanics is strongest at explaining how load reaches tissue (ch04, ch07) and weakest at predicting who will have pain from a static feature.
- **Does not hold when**: a mechanical event is clear and acute (a fall, a sudden overload).

## 9.3 Foot posture
- **Thesis (position)**: there is no single normal foot; variation is the norm and "fixing" flat or pronated feet is unsupported in most cases.
- **Mechanism**: in one sample of healthy young women, most (about eight in ten) showed the "pronated" posture, a third had side-to-side asymmetry; in another sample of pain-free people none matched the ideal foot. Consensus statements in the literature say that pronation is a natural movement, evidence linking it to running injuries is weak, and "excessive pronation" has no clinical definition. The ideal was set arbitrarily long ago, so the normal got pathologised.
- **Modifiable factors**: rehab targets contributors that can change, such as training load and strength or endurance; the shape of the foot is largely not modifiable, and before-and-after photographs can look dramatic through camera angle and weight shift alone.
- **Counter-argument (general knowledge)**: foot structure may contribute to symptoms in some people, and a person with a severe deformity or a specific problem may need dedicated assessment. The claim is not that structure never matters.
- **Does not hold when**: pain is clearly provoked by one specific foot function.

## 9.4 Rounded shoulders and "tug of war" (position, other side)
- **Thesis (position)**: a forward shoulder posture is attributed to a short chest muscle and weak lower trapezius pulling the blade forward; it may limit overhead reach.
- **Counter-argument**: the same objections as 9.1 apply: no ideal posture and weak association with pain; and (general knowledge) short-term changes after a set of exercises do not show a long-term benefit. This chapter reports the mechanism as a hypothesis, and the wall-based reach test as a way to see whether overhead range changes, without claiming it predicts pain.

## 9.5 Stretching and flexibility
- **Thesis**: flexibility gains and performance are separate questions.
- **Mechanism**: held stretches immediately before power tasks tend to lower output, dynamic movements do not (ch05); range of motion improvements from stretching are largely tolerance changes, and tight tissue is not necessarily a cause of pain (general knowledge, position).
- **Does not hold when**: the person needs the range for a task (such as a deep squat or overhead position), in which case range is a prerequisite, not a cure.

## 9.6 Mobility versus variation
- **Thesis (position)**: lack of movement variation may be more relevant than any particular posture.
- **Mechanism**: a repeated, unvaried posture or task loads the same tissue; variability spreads load; this is consistent with the load-capacity view (ch05).
- **Does not hold when**: used to say that any posture is equal; some tasks need specific positions.

## 9.7 How to hold both positions
- Use mechanics to explain how a task loads a tissue (levers, moment arms, ranges).
- Do not use a static look as evidence of pain risk or as a verdict on a person.
- Treat "corrections" as experiments, with a clear comparison and a reason to expect benefit.
- Keep language neutral: avoid "dysfunction", "weak", "out of place" unless they describe a measured, relevant fact.

## 9.8 Posture as an automatic control, not a held position (model, position)
- **Thesis (position)**: posture is largely regulated outside voluntary control, so being told to "stand straight" works briefly and then fades.
- **Mechanism**: in this model voluntary movement is driven through conscious pathways while posture is kept by automatic pathways; a corrected posture is held for minutes and then slips back, which is why willpower is a poor posture tool. A person's usual posture is read as an adaptation shaped by genetics, environment, emotion and habit, and it is distinguished from a painful postural syndrome: a posture that differs from textbook pictures does not imply pain (9.1). Approaches that fit the model use sensory reference (a wall, a mirror, a touch cue) and varied positions ("the best posture is the next one", compare 9.6), and treat strengthening of the neck, trunk, hips and feet as general capacity rather than as a cure for existing pain.
- **Counter-argument (general knowledge)**: the pathway account is a simplification; there is little controlled evidence that postural exercise prevents pain; the claim that a strong posture makes a person more attractive or confident is a social-perception opinion, not tested here; scapular "humps" in weight-bearing positions such as quadrupedal work are described by practitioners as functional protraction, not a defect, which is a position.
- **Trade-off**: reassurance reduces nocebo (ch02) but can dismiss a person who feels a specific position provokes symptoms.
- **Does not hold when**: a specific posture reliably provokes pain or a neurological sign appears; see 9.1 and ch03.

## 9.9 Postural endurance, "tonic" holds and the shoulder-blade model (position, contested)
- **Thesis (position)**: some practitioners hold that posture is maintained by slow, endurance-type muscle fibres that fatigue little, so it should be trained with long isometric holds (one to three minutes accumulated, rest about half the work time) rather than short, fast repetitions; and that stabilisers of the shoulder blade (lower and middle trapezius, serratus, rhomboids) work poorly when a person sits for long hours, letting the upper trapezius compensate.
- **Mechanism offered**: slow-twitch-dominant muscles respond to long low-intensity work, so long holds and tempo work would build the endurance needed to keep a position without effort; training the shoulder blade to move and stabilise (retraction, protraction, upward rotation; hanging and rowing patterns) would reduce reliance on the upper trapezius. The figure often quoted for the share of slow fibres in postural muscles is a position and varies by muscle and person (unclear in source).
- **Counter-argument (general knowledge)**: the same objections as 9.1 and 9.4 apply: little controlled evidence that postural exercise prevents or treats pain; "overactive" and "weak" labels are inferences from appearance, not measurements; short-term changes after a set of exercises do not show a lasting benefit. A long hold is a reasonable endurance workout and a reasonable way to practise a position, which is a different claim from "it fixes posture or pain".
- **How to use it**: treat the holds and shoulder-blade drills as general capacity work with a clear comparison (hold time, reps, ease of a task); stretch only what feels tight; do not present a hanging or holding programme as a treatment; keep neutral language (9.7).
- **Trade-off**: simple, cheap and low in load; may reassure a person or may reinforce a belief that a posture is fragile.
- **Does not hold when**: a specific position reliably provokes pain, a hold causes neck, shoulder or nerve symptoms, or a neurological sign appears (see ch03).

## 9.10 "Deep core" claims: breathing, bracing and doming (position, contested)
- **Thesis (position)**: some coaches distinguish an inner unit (deep abdominal wall, pelvic floor, diaphragm) from the visible abdominals and say it must be trained first, with breathing-linked drills, long low-effort holds, and by avoiding trunk flexion drills that bulge the abdomen ("doming") or arch the lower back.
- **Mechanism offered**: coordinated breathing and abdominal wall activity set intra-abdominal pressure and trunk stiffness; if the abdomen visibly pushes out or the lower back leaves the floor during a leg movement, the trunk is no longer controlling the load, so reducing the lever (bent legs) keeps the exercise inside a person's current range. A common bedside test: hand under the lower back, lift the legs to the longest lever at which the back stays still.
- **Counter-argument (general knowledge)**: there is no clear evidence that a single "deep" muscle works in isolation or that training it prevents back pain; trunk muscle activity is shared across layers and trained by many exercises; "doming" and the risk of "separating" the abdominals are mainly described in pregnancy and after it, and the claim that a floor crunch is harmful is a position (spinal flexion is a normal movement and tolerated by most people, see ch06 and ch07). A cue such as "tongue on the roof of the mouth" for neck stability is an anecdotal technique, not a tested one (unclear in source).
- **How to use it**: useful as an exercise-regression framework (shorten the lever until the position holds), as a way to coach breathing, and as a rehearsal of control in the postpartum or low-back-pain population only under clinical guidance; not as proof that crunches are dangerous or that a specific muscle is the cause of pain.
- **Trade-off**: slower progression and much attention to detail; may reduce a person's trust in ordinary movement if presented as "you were doing it wrong".
- **Does not hold when**: there is a postpartum abdominal separation, a hernia, pelvic pain or any specific pelvic floor symptom: see a qualified clinician.
